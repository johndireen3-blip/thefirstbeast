REVELATION 15:01–04

A new Exodus.

A new Exodus.

01John sees another great and wondrous sign in heaven: seven angels holding the seven last plagues, which bring God’s wrath to its completion.

02He sees what looks like a sea of glass mixed with fire. Standing on it are those who have conquered the beast, its image and the number of its name, and they hold harps given them by God.

03They sing the song of Moses, God’s servant, and the song of the Lamb: Great and marvelous are your works, Lord God almighty; just and true are your ways, King of the nations.

04Who would not fear you, Lord, and give glory to your name? You alone are holy. All the nations will come and bow before you, for your just judgments have been made known.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Chapter 15 opens the third and last series of seven. The USCCB notes explain that, as with the seals and trumpets, the bowls are introduced by a heavenly prelude: a vision of the victorious martyrs before the vision of woe (compare 7:9–12). The scene is a new Exodus. Israel crossed the sea, saw Pharaoh’s army destroyed, and sang the song of Moses on the far shore (Exod 15:1–18). Now those who have escaped, as the notes put it, the oppression of the Devil stand by the sea and sing. The fire mingled with the glass, the notes suggest, stands for the holiness of facing God, seen in the trials that refined the faithful and in God’s judgment. Remarkably, the hymn says nothing about the singers’ own victory. It praises only God, and it ends with a hope for every nation.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The “tent of testimony,” the USCCB notes point out, is the name of the meeting tent in the Greek text of Exodus 40, where God’s glory came down on the tabernacle in the desert. The angels are dressed like priests, and the bowls they receive are the same golden bowls that in 5:8 held the prayers of the holy ones. The smoke recalls the glory that filled Solomon’s temple (1 Kgs 8:10–11) and Isaiah’s vision (Isa 6:4). God’s judgments do not come from outside his holiness; they come from the sanctuary itself, in answer to the prayers of his people.

REVELATION 15:2

The sea of glass mixed with fire

In 4:6 the sea of glass lay calm before the throne. Now it glows with fire, like the Red Sea after the crossing. The victors stand where Israel stood, on the safe side of the waters.

02

REVELATION 15:3

The song of Moses and the Lamb

Moses sang after the Exodus (Exod 15); the Lamb leads a greater one, from sin and death to life. One song for both covenants: the God of Israel and the God revealed in the Lamb are one.

Read Exodus 15
03

REVELATION 15:4

All the nations will come

The hymn borrows from Psalm 86:9 and Jeremiah 10:7. Even on the eve of the last plagues, the goal of God’s judgment is that the nations will come and worship.

Read Psalm 86
04

REVELATION 15:5

The tent of testimony

The tabernacle housed the ark and the tablets of the covenant, the “testimony” (Exod 40:20). Its opening means that what follows is judged by the covenant God made with his people.

Read Exodus 40
05

REVELATION 15:7

Seven golden bowls

Bowls are vessels of the sanctuary. In 5:8 golden bowls held the prayers of the holy ones; now golden bowls hold God’s answer. The prayers of the Church are not lost; they shape the course of history.

READ WITH CARE

Revelation’s three series of seven, the seals, trumpets and bowls, are often read as a timeline of twenty-one consecutive future events. The USCCB notes describe the bowls as the third and last group of seven, and many Catholic interpreters see the three series as overlapping retellings of the same reality, each more intense than the last, rather than a calendar. The Church reads them as a picture of God’s justice at work in every age, not a schedule for the end.

WHERE IT FITS

From witness to reign.

Chapter 15 is the shortest chapter in Revelation and a hinge between the visions of conflict (chapters 12–14) and the last plagues (chapter 16). Like the scenes in heaven before the seals (chapters 4–5) and the trumpets (8:1–5), it shows worship before judgment. The victors on the sea are the same faithful seen with the Lamb on Mount Zion (14:1–5) and in the great multitude (7:9–17). The bowls they watch being handed out are poured in chapter 16.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or go on to the next chapter and come back to it.

This short chapter stands between the harvest of chapter 14 and the bowls of chapter 16. Oecumenius and St. Andrew of Caesarea read it of the end, when the wrath of God is completed and the saints stand purified before him. Primasius and St. Bede, following Tyconius, see the Church in the present age: baptized, tested by fire, taught by both Testaments and sent out to the nations. They also disagree about a word. Some copies clothe the seven angels in pure linen and others in a pure stone, and each reading gets its own interpretation.

01

REVELATION 15:1

Seven angels with the seven last plagues

Oecumenius: the number seven stands for the many plagues prepared for sinners, through which the wrath of God will reach its end (Commentary on the Apocalypse 15.1–4).

St. Andrew of Caesarea: John uses the number seven everywhere. The injustices done brazenly during the seven days of the present age will be held back by the seven plagues and the seven angels. After these the future life of the saints will begin, which the sea of glass signifies (Commentary on the Apocalypse 15.1).

St. Bede: the seven angels are the Church, full of the sevenfold grace. The plagues are called the last because the wrath of God always strikes a rebellious people sevenfold, that is, completely, as Leviticus often says (Leviticus 26:24). They are the final persecutions, and the Church will come out from the midst of them (Explanation of the Apocalypse 15.1).

02

REVELATION 15:2

The sea of glass mingled with fire

Oecumenius: St. Paul says that each one’s work, whether gold, silver and precious stones or wood, hay and straw, will be revealed by fire, and the fire will test its quality (1 Corinthians 3:12–13). So the righteous are tested by fire as well as sinners. That is why those who have overcome the beast stand on a sea of glass mingled with fire. It is glass for the brightness and purity of the righteous. It is mingled with fire for their cleansing, since even the righteous need purification: “we all fall short in many respects” (James 3:2), and no one is free of stain (Job 14:4). The harps are the harmonious hymn the saints sing to God (15.1–4).

On purification. The Church reads the same passage of St. Paul when she teaches about purgatory, the final purification of those who die in God’s grace and friendship (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1030–1031).

Primasius: this is the water of Baptism, made holy by the fire of the Holy Spirit. Or the water has been reddened by martyrdom, for, in his Latin text, the furnace tests the potter’s vessels and the trial of tribulation tests the just (Sirach 27:5). His Latin text says that the victors stand “upon” the sea, and he takes this to mean that they are immovable in the faith. The psalm describes them when it says that God “spread the earth upon the waters” (Psalm 136:6): they stand firm on their Baptism. They may still be fed with milk, and yet they are not swept along by every wind of teaching (Ephesians 4:14). St. Caesarius of Arles says outright that they conquer the beast in their Baptism. The harps are their hearts, given to the praise of God and tuned by the harmonious truth of the two Testaments. Or, with Tyconius, the harp is the flesh of the Passion stretched on the wood, which gives the sound of the voice and also the completed good work. That is why Moses is named, of whom God says that he is faithful in all his house (Numbers 12:7) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 15.2).

Andrew: the sea of glass is the multitude of those who are being saved, the purity of the reward to come, and the brightness the saints will give off by their virtue. The fire is the fire that will test each one’s work (1 Corinthians 3:13). It does not harm the pure. St. Basil understood the psalm verse on the voice of the Lord and the flame of fire (Psalm 29:7) to mean that this fire is divided into two workings: it burns sinners and gives light to the righteous. The fire may also be divine knowledge and the grace of the life-giving Spirit, for God appeared to Moses in fire, and the Holy Spirit came down on the apostles as tongues of fire. The harps suggest the putting to death of our members, and a life in the harmony of the virtues, played by the Holy Spirit (15.2).

03

REVELATION 15:3–4

The song of Moses and of the Lamb

Oecumenius: the song of Moses is the one he sang when Pharaoh was drowned with all his army: “I will sing to the LORD, for he is gloriously triumphant; horse and chariot he has cast into the sea” (Exodus 15:1). The present song of triumph is for the punishment of the wicked and the victory over the devil and the Antichrist. It is also called the song of the Lamb because Moses is in agreement with the Lord and with his just judgment. Christ is king of all. He is called “king of the nations” because Isaiah foretold that the nations would seek out the root of Jesse (Isaiah 11:10), and so the passage foretells the calling of the nations and their faith in the Lord (15.1–4).

Primasius: the song shows the purpose of the elect, who pray from the heart to be kept from the way of falsehood and taught God’s law (Psalm 119:29). Moses signifies the Old Testament and the Lamb the New, and the elect are equipped from both and perfected in both. The pages of both Testaments bear a single witness to their song, for all the paths of the Lord are mercy and truth (Psalm 25:10). Christ is rightly called king of the nations, for he is not the God of one people only, and the dew from heaven did not fall on one fleece alone (see Judges 6:37). As Malachi says: “from the rising of the sun to its setting, my name is great among the nations; Incense offerings are made to my name everywhere, and a pure offering” (Malachi 1:11) (15.3).

Andrew: from the song of Moses we learn the hymn sent up to God by those who were justified under the law before grace came. From the song of the Lamb we learn the thanksgiving of those who lived in holiness after the coming of Christ. They give thanks for his gifts to our race, since through the holy apostles he has called all nations to know him (15.3–4).

04

REVELATION 15:5–6

The temple opened; the angels come out

Oecumenius: Scripture calls the tent that Bezalel built in the desert the “tent of testimony,” because it held the testimonies and ordinances of God: the ark, the mercy seat, the table, the altars and the lampstand, all made according to the pattern shown to Moses on the mountain (Exodus 25:9). After that image John calls the temple above the heavens by the same name. The angels come out with seven plagues, for at the end there will be many signs on the earth, as Christ said in the Gospels. His text clothes them in a pure, bright stone. This shows their rank, purity and brightness, and a nature wholly given to the good, because they have put on Christ. Scripture calls the Lord a stone: the precious cornerstone laid in Zion (Isaiah 28:16), and “The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone” (Psalm 118:22). St. Paul tells us to put on the Lord Jesus Christ and make no provision for the desires of the flesh (Romans 13:14). Whoever is clothed in this stone is free of every desire that destroys the soul (15.5–16.1).

Primasius: the hidden secrets of what was foretold about Christ and the Church are now revealed. The seven angels are the Church, against whom a hostile part has rebelled with unending treachery. The plagues come out in defence of the saints, in whom Christ himself suffered, as he said: “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?” (Acts 9:4). Or they come because judgment begins with the household of God (1 Peter 4:17). People are scourged in two ways. The just God punishes sins, as with Pharaoh, or he tests virtue by a greater trial, as with Job, who came through like gold tried in the fire (Job 23:10–12). The number seven shows that the end reaches everywhere.

  • Linen and gold. His text reads “pure white linen.” Linen often signifies mortification. Those who have died to themselves are made white as snow when they see the glory of God in the revealing of his judgments. Gold is wisdom, as Proverbs says (Proverbs 16:16). It is worn around the breast, where the law commanded the priest to wear the breastpiece of judgment, so that his mind would know the things of God, praise the divine judgments, and by the brightness of the gold lead others to praise them without hesitation (15.5–6).

Andrew: there is a tent in heaven, and God commanded Moses to build the tabernacle below according to its likeness. The angels come out dressed in pure linen or, as some copies have it, in a pure stone. Their dress shows the purity of their nature, their nearness to Christ the cornerstone, and the brightness of their virtue. The golden sashes are the power, purity and honour of their nature, and their freedom in the works of their service (15.5–6).

Bede: this vision answers the hymn. So that the Lord might be adored by all nations, the temple of the mysteries of God, once enclosed within the walls of one city, begins to be opened spiritually to the whole world. The angels come out as the apostles did, of whom St. Mark says: “they went forth and preached everywhere” (Mark 16:20). They are robed in a pure white stone, for “all of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ” (Galatians 3:27), and Christ is the chosen cornerstone. Or the one stone stands for many, the various beauties of the virtues. The other reading, “white linen,” is the mortification of the flesh that our teachers practise, as St. Paul drove his body and trained it for fear that, after preaching to others, he himself should be disqualified (1 Corinthians 9:27). Whoever wants to preach mighty things must mortify the body and also bind the breast with the gold of wisdom. Or the golden sash is the chain of love, which alone binds every movement of our changeable thoughts (15.5–6).

05

REVELATION 15:7

Seven golden bowls

Oecumenius: the sashes show that the angels are ready and well equipped for their task, for they are the mighty ones who do God’s bidding (Psalm 103:20). They receive the wrath of God from one of the four living creatures in golden bowls. The bowls are rightly golden. The wrath of God is precious, and it holds what is good and beneficial, even though those who are punished feel distress (15.5–16.1).

Primasius: earlier the bowls held the prayers of the saints (5:8). Now they are full of the wrath of God. The same bowls hold the sweetness of supplication and the wrath of destruction, because the saints pray for the coming of the kingdom of God. When it comes, his judgments will no longer be hidden as in an abyss. They will lie open as in bowls, bringing salvation to the righteous and ruin to the impious. So the Apostle says that we are the aroma of Christ among those who are being saved and among those who are perishing, to the latter an odour of death (2 Corinthians 2:15–16). It was said of our Lord that he is “destined for the fall and rise of many” (Luke 2:34). It is no surprise, then, that the bowls bring sweetness to the just and the plagues to the impious (15.7).

Andrew: the angels receive the bowls from the four living creatures, as in Ezekiel. This shows that in heaven the knowledge of God’s plans is always passed from those who are first in rank to those who are second, as the great Dionysius says (Celestial Hierarchy 8.2; 13.2) (15.7).

06

REVELATION 15:8

The temple filled with smoke

Oecumenius: smoke is the sign of the divine anger, as the psalm says that smoke rose from his nostrils (Psalm 18:9). Isaiah saw the same when the door posts shook at the cry of the seraphim and the house was filled with smoke (Isaiah 6:4). The smoke is said to come from the glory and power of God because God is himself power and glory. No one could enter the temple, for who can face the wrath of God, or live when wrapped in it? No one has stood in the council of the Lord (Jeremiah 23:18), and no one could endure his wrath (15.5–16.1).

Primasius: the smoke shows that no one can enter into the secrets of the judgments of God. The minds of mortals grow dark and tremble at the thought of the plagues. The smoke remains until the plagues are ended, as the psalmist found the fate of the wicked too hard to understand until he entered the sanctuary of God and saw their end (Psalm 73:16–17).

  • Smoke as confession. Smoke comes before fire, and the confession of sins comes before the flame of faith and love. God looks at the earth and it trembles, and he touches the mountains and they smoke (Psalm 104:32). So “the Lord turned and looked at Peter” (Luke 22:61), and Peter, who had been brought to fear and trembling by his denial, washed it away in tears. On this reading no one enters the temple, no one can say that he walks in innocence of heart within God’s house (Psalm 101:2) or that the King has brought him into his chambers (Song of Songs 1:4), unless he has first taken care to wipe out his past sins by confession. Until all the plagues are ended, the Church remains in the lament of confession. This is true of those who are paying a fruitful penalty for past faults, and of those who will be shown to be approved by a better worship (15.8).

Andrew: from the smoke we learn how fearful and terrible the wrath of God is. The temple is filled with it at the time of judgment, against those who deserve it, and most of all against those who obey the Antichrist and do the works of apostasy (15.8).

Bede: when the Church is about to preach to the nations she is first set on fire with love, and she gives off the smoke of a devout confession: “Thanks be to God for his indescribable gift!” (2 Corinthians 9:15). No one enters the temple until the plagues are ended. No one is made a member of the Church unless he hears the mysteries of the faith and learns from our teachers that Jesus is “the one appointed by God as judge of the living and the dead” (Acts 10:42). Or the smoke is the hidden mysteries of God’s judgments. They stay closed to mortals until the plagues of the present age are over and the Lord comes. He will bring to light what is hidden in darkness, and will show how the coming of the Antichrist served to test the faith of the Church (15.8).
