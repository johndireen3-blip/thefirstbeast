REVELATION 15:01–04

A new Exodus.

A new Exodus.

01John sees another great and wondrous sign in heaven: seven angels holding the seven last plagues, which bring God’s wrath to its completion.

02He sees what looks like a sea of glass mixed with fire. Standing on it are those who have conquered the beast, its image and the number of its name, and they hold harps given them by God.

03They sing the song of Moses, God’s servant, and the song of the Lamb: Great and marvelous are your works, Lord God almighty; just and true are your ways, King of the nations.

04Who would not fear you, Lord, and give glory to your name? You alone are holy. All the nations will come and bow before you, for your just judgments have been made known.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Chapter 15 opens the third and last series of seven. The USCCB notes explain that, as with the seals and trumpets, the bowls are introduced by a heavenly prelude: a vision of the victorious martyrs before the vision of woe (compare 7:9–12). The scene is a new Exodus. Israel crossed the sea, saw Pharaoh’s army destroyed, and sang the song of Moses on the far shore (Exod 15:1–18). Now those who have escaped, as the notes put it, the oppression of the Devil stand by the sea and sing. The fire mingled with the glass, the notes suggest, stands for the holiness of facing God, seen in the trials that refined the faithful and in God’s judgment. Remarkably, the hymn says nothing about the singers’ own victory. It praises only God, and it ends with a hope for every nation.

No one could enter.

No one could enter.

05After this John looks, and the temple in heaven, the tent of testimony, is opened.

06The seven angels with the seven plagues come out of the temple, dressed in pure, bright linen with golden sashes around their chests.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The “tent of testimony,” the USCCB notes point out, is the name of the meeting tent in the Greek text of Exodus 40, where God’s glory came down on the tabernacle in the desert. The angels are dressed like priests, and the bowls they receive are the same golden bowls that in 5:8 held the prayers of the holy ones. The smoke recalls the glory that filled Solomon’s temple (1 Kgs 8:10–11) and Isaiah’s vision (Isa 6:4). God’s judgments do not come from outside his holiness; they come from the sanctuary itself, in answer to the prayers of his people.

REVELATION 15:2

The sea of glass mixed with fire

In 4:6 the sea of glass lay calm before the throne. Now it glows with fire, like the Red Sea after the crossing. The victors stand where Israel stood, on the safe side of the waters.

REVELATION 15:3

The song of Moses and the Lamb

Moses sang after the Exodus (Exod 15); the Lamb leads a greater one, from sin and death to life. One song for both covenants: the God of Israel and the God revealed in the Lamb are one.

03

REVELATION 15:4

All the nations will come

The hymn borrows from Psalm 86:9 and Jeremiah 10:7. Even on the eve of the last plagues, the goal of God’s judgment is that the nations will come and worship.

04

REVELATION 15:5

The tent of testimony

The tabernacle housed the ark and the tablets of the covenant, the “testimony” (Exod 40:20). Its opening means that what follows is judged by the covenant God made with his people.

Read Exodus 40
05

REVELATION 15:7

Seven golden bowls

Bowls are vessels of the sanctuary. In 5:8 golden bowls held the prayers of the holy ones; now golden bowls hold God’s answer. The prayers of the Church are not lost; they shape the course of history.

READ WITH CARE

Revelation’s three series of seven, the seals, trumpets and bowls, are often read as a timeline of twenty-one consecutive future events. The USCCB notes describe the bowls as the third and last group of seven, and many Catholic interpreters see the three series as overlapping retellings of the same reality, each more intense than the last, rather than a calendar. The Church reads them as a picture of God’s justice at work in every age, not a schedule for the end.

WHERE IT FITS

From witness to reign.

Chapter 15 is the shortest chapter in Revelation and a hinge between the visions of conflict (chapters 12–14) and the last plagues (chapter 16). Like the scenes in heaven before the seals (chapters 4–5) and the trumpets (8:1–5), it shows worship before judgment. The victors on the sea are the same faithful seen with the Lamb on Mount Zion (14:1–5) and in the great multitude (7:9–17). The bowls they watch being handed out are poured in chapter 16.
