## Who the dragon is

John does not leave it to interpretation. A few verses later he names him four times over: "The huge dragon, the ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan, who deceived the whole world" (Revelation 12:9; again at 20:2). He is the serpent of Eden, and the scene before us, a woman, her child and the serpent, is the promise of Genesis 3:15 coming to its decision. [[auth:scripture]]

The Church teaches the same of him plainly. He is "a fallen angel," who "was at first a good angel, made by God"; the angels' fall "consists in the free choice of these created spirits, who radically and irrevocably rejected God and his reign" (CCC 391–392). In the Our Father, "evil is not an abstraction, but refers to a person, Satan, the Evil One, the angel who opposes God" (CCC 2851). [[auth:church]]

## The words

- **Another sign.** The Woman is "a great sign"; the dragon is only "another sign." He is set beside her and below her.
- **Huge and red.** The color is *pyrros*, fiery red, the color of the horse whose rider takes peace from the earth (6:4). St. Victorinus says he is this color because "the result of his work gave him such a colour." [[auth:father]] The Lord called him "a murderer from the beginning" (John 8:44).
- **Diadems.** He wears *diadēmata*, royal crowns, seven of them. The Woman wears a *stephanos*, a victor's wreath. When Christ appears at the end, "on his head were many diadems" (19:12): the kingship the dragon displays belongs to another.
- **Stood.** He takes up a position and waits. The verb returns at the end of the chapter, when he "took its position on the sand of the sea" (12:18) to call up the beast.

## The Old Testament behind it

John's dragon is assembled from the prophets, who used the sea monster as a name for the empires that devoured Israel.

- **Leviathan.** "On that day, the LORD will punish with his sword that is cruel, great, and strong, Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the coiled serpent; he will slay the dragon in the sea" (Isaiah 27:1). "You smashed the heads of the dragons on the waters. You crushed the heads of Leviathan" (Psalm 74:13–14). The dragon already has more than one head in the Psalms.
- **Egypt.** "I am against you, Pharaoh, king of Egypt, great dragon crouching in the midst of the Nile" (Ezekiel 29:3). It was a Pharaoh who ordered, "Throw into the Nile every boy that is born" (Exodus 1:22).
- **Babylon.** Zion says of Nebuchadnezzar, he "swallowed me like a sea monster" (Jeremiah 51:34).
- **The fourth beast.** Daniel's last beast "had ten horns" (Daniel 7:7), and its little horn "cast down to earth some of the host and some of the stars and trampled on them" (Daniel 8:10).

So the seven heads, the ten horns and the falling stars are not new. They are the marks of Egypt, Babylon and the persecutor of Daniel's day, gathered onto one figure. John is saying that one enemy stood behind them all. [[auth:scripture]]

## The seven heads and ten horns

The beast that rises from the sea in the next chapter has the same heads and horns (13:1), and receives from the dragon "its own power and throne" (13:2). The dragon shows in himself the shape of the power he will work through.

- St. Victorinus reads the heads as "the seven kings of the Romans," and the ten horns as "the ten kings in the latest times" ([*Commentary on the Apocalypse*](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ENG1016/_PC.HTM), on chapter 12). [[auth:father]]
- The notes of the New American Bible take the diadems more generally, as "symbolic of the fullness of the dragon's sovereignty over the kingdoms of this world" ([note on 12:3](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/12)). [[auth:theologian]]

The heads and horns are studied where they belong, with the beast, on the partner site: [The First Beast](https://thefirstbeast.com/built-from-daniels-beasts).

## A third of the stars

"Its tail swept away a third of the stars in the sky and hurled them down to the earth." The Church has not settled what the stars are, and the Fathers give two readings, sometimes in the same breath. [[auth:disputed]]

- **St. Victorinus gives both.** The verse, he says, "may be taken in two ways": either that the devil "may be able to seduce the third part of the men who believe," or that "of the angels that were subject to him… he seduced the third part" ([*Commentary on the Apocalypse*, ch. 12](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0712.htm)). [[auth:father]]
- **The angels who fell with him.** The chapter itself supports this: when the dragon is thrown down, "its angels were thrown down with it" (12:9). So do Jude 6, "the angels too, who did not keep to their own domain," and the Catechism's teaching on the angels' fall (CCC 391–393). This is the reading most Catholics know.
- **Members of the Church whom he drags down.** The words come from Daniel 8:10, where the stars thrown down and trampled are God's people. St. Methodius reads the stars as "the bodies of heresies," those who claimed a place in the heavens and were "dragged down" ([*The Banquet* 8.10](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/062308.htm)). Pope St. Gregory the Great reads them as people who seemed to shine in the Church and fell away to pursue worldly glory ([*Moralia in Job* 32.15](https://lectionarycentral.com/GregoryMoralia/Book32.html)). [[auth:father]]
- **Both together.** St. Bede reads the stars as angels and men alike ([*Explanation of the Apocalypse*, on 12:4](https://archive.org/details/explanationapoc00bedegoog)). [[auth:father]]

The second reading is the more searching for a reader. It says the dragon's tail reaches into the Church, and that those he pulls down may be the ones who appeared brightest.

The text says "a third," as the trumpets strike a third of the earth, the sea and the stars (8:7–12). In Revelation a third is a great loss that is still a limited one. He is not given the whole. A fuller account of these readings is on [A Great Sign](https://agreatsign.si/revelation-12).

## Before the woman, to devour her child

The Gospel shows the dragon's attempt in history. "Herod is going to search for the child to destroy him" (Matthew 2:13); "He ordered the massacre of all the boys in Bethlehem and its vicinity two years old and under" (2:16). The Catechism reads the flight into Egypt and the massacre of the innocents as making "manifest the opposition of darkness to the light," and adds: "Christ's whole life was lived under the sign of persecution. His own share it with him" (CCC 530). [[auth:church]]

St. Victorinus looks past Bethlehem to Calvary. The dragon "standing and desiring to devour her child when she had brought him forth, is the devil," and "he could not devour Him—that is, detain Him in death." [[auth:father]]

Both are true to the text. From the manger to the tomb the serpent sought the child, and at each point he failed.

## What the Fathers said further

Victorinus, Methodius and Bede are quoted in the sections above. The other commentators add the following. [[auth:father]]

**Why a dragon, and why red.** Oecumenius: he is called a dragon for his deceit, as Isaiah speaks of the twisting serpent (Isaiah 27:1), and red for his thirst for blood. The vision shows him first in heaven so that the apostate may know from what height he fell (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.3–6). St. Andrew of Caesarea thinks "heaven" here means the air. He is red for his murderous character, or for the fiery nature he had as an angel (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.3).

**The heads, horns and diadems.**

- **Tyconius:** the heads are kings and the horns kingdoms. In the seven heads and their diadems he sees the rule of all kings, and in the ten horns the persecutors who will stir up persecution against the whole Church in the last times (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.3).
- **Oecumenius:** seven means many, for he has many dominions and many plots. The psalm speaks of God crushing the heads of the dragon (Psalm 74:14). The diadem is a sign of tyranny, and the ten horns of great power, as the book of Job shows.
- **Andrew:** the seven heads are seven powers of wickedness set against the seven gifts of the Spirit. Or they are the seven spirits of the Lord's parable, who settle in the heart that has been swept and left empty (Matthew 12:43–45), or the seven abominations in the heart of the enemy (Proverbs 26:25). The horns are the sins against the Ten Commandments, or the divisions of the kingdom in which he delights. Of the diadems Andrew makes a point of hope: those who conquer the demons receive the crowns of victory.

**A third of the stars.**

- **Angels.** Oecumenius: he cast down a great number of angels with himself, having persuaded them to revolt from God. They were bright as stars and became dark. "By his tail" means by what came last, the final stage of his own rebellion.
- **Members of the Church.** Tyconius reads the tail as false prophets, who throw down the simple who attach themselves to them. St. Caesarius of Arles says the same of heretics who draw the baptized into a second baptism, and notes that many read it of the angels who fell with the devil (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 9).
- **Either.** Andrew: it is the first fall, which brought down the apostate angels, or it is the movement of his tail after his head was crushed, dragging down those who have let go of heavenly thoughts. They are called stars for the brightness of their baptism (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.4).

**Waiting to devour the child.**

- **Oecumenius** reads it of the Lord's own life. Satan watched for the birth of the one who would destroy his dominion, and when the Virgin gave birth he stirred up Herod to kill the child. He quotes Isaiah on the child who conquers before he can call his father or mother (Isaiah 8:4).
- **Tyconius** reads it of Christ and of every Christian. The Spirit shows what is to come in the Church under the form of what has already happened. In her sufferings the Church is always bringing forth Christ, and the devil is always seeking to devour whoever is born to God. Herod stands for the whole company of persecutors: the angel said that those who sought the child's life were dead (Matthew 2:20), as was said to Moses of Pharaoh (Exodus 4:19). His principle is stated in a sentence: "every Christian suffers that which the Head has suffered" (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.4).
- **Andrew:** the apostate is always armed against the Church, wishing to make food of those who are reborn from her. In them he persecutes Christ, who said to Saul, "Why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 9:4).
- **Victorinus** adds to what is quoted above that the devil came to tempt the Lord as a man, and finding him other than he thought, departed until an opportune time (Luke 4:13).

*Source: unless another source is linked, the Fathers in this section are cited from* Revelation, *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 12:3–4.*

## Where readers differ

- **When the stars fall.** If the stars are angels, is this the fall of the angels before the world's history, or the casting down described in 12:7–9? The Church teaches that the angels' sin came at the beginning (CCC 391–392); how John's visions are ordered in time is a separate question, taken up on the page on the war in heaven. [[auth:disputed]]
- **The seven heads.** Roman emperors, a succession of empires, or simply fullness of power: the Church has defined none of these.
- **The ancient myths.** Scholars note that the ancient world told stories of a goddess pursued by a monster while bearing a savior, and that John's first readers knew them (NABRE, note on 12:1–6). If John echoes them, it is to say that what the nations dreamed has been told truly in Israel's Messiah.

## What it asks of us

Two things, and they must be held together. First, not to be naive. The Church speaks of a person, not a symbol of bad luck or human weakness: "Your opponent the devil is prowling around like a roaring lion looking for someone to devour" (1 Peter 5:8).

Second, not to be afraid. "The power of Satan is, nonetheless, not infinite. He is only a creature, powerful from the fact that he is pure spirit, but still a creature. He cannot prevent the building up of God's reign" (CCC 395). In John's vision he is a sign shown beneath a greater sign; he takes a third and not the whole; he waits for the child and loses him. Everything the chapter says of him afterward is about a defeat.
