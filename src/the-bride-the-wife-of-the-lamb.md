## The Woman answered

This study began with a sign in heaven: a woman clothed with the sun, in labor, with a dragon before her (12:1–4). It has followed her into the desert and followed her enemy to his end. Here is where her story arrives.

| | The Woman (chapter 12) | The Bride (chapter 21) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| John sees | "A great sign… in the sky" | "The holy city… coming down out of heaven" |
| Her light | Clothed with the sun, the moon under her feet | "It gleamed with the splendor of God" (21:11); no need of sun or moon (21:23) |
| The number twelve | A crown of twelve stars | Twelve gates, twelve foundations (21:12, 14) |
| Her condition | "She wailed aloud in pain" (12:2) | "No more… mourning, wailing or pain" (21:4) |
| Her place | The desert, "a place prepared by God" (12:6) | The city "prepared as a bride" (21:2) |
| Her enemy | The dragon, standing before her | Gone; "the sea was no more" (21:1) |
| Her relation to Christ | Mother of the male child | Wife of the Lamb |

The word "prepared" is the same in both. God prepared a place to keep her through the trial, and through the trial he was preparing her.

## The words

- **A new heaven and a new earth.** Isaiah's promise: "See, I am creating new heavens and a new earth" (Isaiah 65:17). The word for "new" means new in kind, renewed, not merely another.
- **The sea was no more.** "Because as home of the dragon it was doomed to disappear," say the notes of the New American Bible ([note on 21:1](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/21)).
- **Coming down out of heaven from God.** The city is not built up from the earth. She is given.
- **A bride adorned for her husband.** "Symbol of the church," say the notes, citing St. Paul: "the Jerusalem above is freeborn, and she is our mother" (Galatians 4:26).
- **God's dwelling is with the human race.** "Dwelling" is the word for tent, the tabernacle. "He will dwell with them" is the same word John's Gospel uses of the Incarnation: the Word "made his dwelling among us" (John 1:14).
- **He will wipe every tear.** From Isaiah 25:8, and promised already to the great multitude (7:17). There an elder said it of God. Here the voice from the throne says it.
- **"Behold, I make all things new."** The first words spoken by the one on the throne since the opening chapter.
- **The bride, the wife of the Lamb.** Both words together: the freshness of a bride, and the permanence of a wife.

## The Old Testament behind it

The prophets spoke of the covenant as a marriage. "I will betroth you to me forever: I will betroth you to me with justice and with judgment, with loyalty and with compassion" (Hosea 2:21). "As a bridegroom rejoices in his bride so shall your God rejoice in you" (Isaiah 62:5). The notes of the New American Bible: "Marriage is one of the biblical metaphors used to describe the covenant relationship between God and his people… Hence, idolatry and apostasy are viewed as adultery and harlotry" (note on 19:7).

The covenant formula itself, "they will be his people and God himself will always be with them," comes from Ezekiel 37:27. It was spoken at Sinai, repeated by the prophets, and is here said for the last time, because here it is complete.

The New Testament names the bridegroom. John the Baptist called himself the friend of the bridegroom (John 3:29). St. Paul wrote that Christ "loved the church and handed himself over for her… that he might present to himself the church in splendor, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing" (Ephesians 5:25–27). [[auth:scripture]]

## Two women, one angel

Compare the two invitations.

- "Then one of the seven angels who were holding the seven bowls came and said to me, 'Come here. I will show you the judgment on the great harlot'" (17:1). He carries John away into a desert (17:3).
- "One of the seven angels who held the seven bowls… came and said to me, 'Come here. I will show you the bride, the wife of the Lamb.'" He takes John "to a great, high mountain" (21:9–10).

The wording is deliberately the same. John wants the two set side by side: a woman who is a city, Babylon, clothed in purple and scarlet and seated on the beast; and a woman who is a city, Jerusalem, clothed in the glory of God and given to the Lamb.

With the Woman of chapter 12 there are three women in the second half of the book. The Woman is the people of God in labor and in flight. The harlot is the city that gave itself to the beast. The Bride is the Woman at the end of her road. The harlot is what the Woman refused to become.

## What the Church teaches

**The Bride is the Church.** "The Church… is described as the spotless spouse of the spotless lamb. It is she whom Christ 'loved and for whom he delivered himself up that he might sanctify her'" (CCC 757). At the end "all those he has redeemed… will be gathered together as the one People of God, the 'Bride of the Lamb,' 'the holy city Jerusalem coming down out of heaven from God'" (CCC 865).

**She is not yet what she will be.** "Those who are united with Christ will form the community of the redeemed, 'the holy city' of God, 'the Bride, the wife of the Lamb.' She will not be wounded any longer by sin, stains, self-love, that destroy or wound the earthly community" (CCC 1045). The Church on earth is wounded by these. John says the city is "prepared." She is being made ready now.

**Scripture begins and ends with a wedding.** "Sacred Scripture begins with the creation of man and woman in the image and likeness of God and concludes with a vision of 'the wedding-feast of the Lamb'" (CCC 1602).

**The new creation is real.** "The universe itself will be renewed" (CCC 1042). "In this new universe, the heavenly Jerusalem, God will have his dwelling among men" (CCC 1044). "We know neither the moment of the consummation of the earth and of man, nor the way in which the universe will be transformed" (CCC 1048).

## Read with the Woman

Here the readings of the Woman as Mary and as the Church come closest together, and the Catechism joins them in a single line. Listing those who share in the worship of heaven, it names "the all-holy Mother of God (the Woman), the Bride of the Lamb" (CCC 1138).

Mary is not the Bride in place of the Church. She is the first in whom the Bride is finished. "Mary goes before us all in the holiness that is the Church's mystery as 'the bride without spot or wrinkle'" (CCC 773). "In her we contemplate what the Church already is in her mystery on her own 'pilgrimage of faith,' and what she will be in the homeland at the end of her journey… The Mother of Jesus, in the glory which she possesses in body and soul in heaven, is the image and beginning of the Church as it is to be perfected in the world to come" (CCC 972). [[auth:church]]

So the sign of chapter 12 and the vision of chapter 21 answer each other in her. What John sees coming down at the end, the Church believes has already been given to one of her members. The Assumption is the Bride's future, shown in advance.

Read as Israel, the Woman is here too: the city's gates bear "the names of the twelve tribes of the Israelites" (21:12). Read as the Church: its foundations bear "the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb" (21:14). The three readings that ran side by side through chapter 12 enter the same city. ## The victor and the coward

The voice from the throne ends with a promise and a warning (21:7–8). "The victor will inherit these gifts, and I shall be his God, and he will be my son." Then a list of those whose lot is the second death, and the first word in it is "cowards."

The notes of the New American Bible explain: "their conviction is so weak that they deny Christ in time of trial and become traitors." This is the choice of chapter 13 once more. The mark was taken, for the most part, out of fear. See [Two marks on the forehead](/two-marks-on-the-forehead).

## What the Fathers said

On the first question this page lists under "Where readers differ," the Fathers cited here give one answer: the world is renewed, not replaced. [[auth:father]]

**A new heaven and a new earth.**

- **Renewal, not annihilation.** Oecumenius reads the verse with St. Peter, who waits for new heavens and a new earth (2 Peter 3:13), and with St. Paul, who says creation will be set free from its slavery to corruption (Romans 8:19–21). Heaven and earth do not pass into nothing. They put off their corruption like an old, soiled garment (Psalm 102:27) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.13–21.2). St. Andrew of Caesarea says the same, and adds a comparison from ordinary speech. Of a person much changed we say that "someone has become someone else." So heaven and earth are said to have passed away, and so shall we change to a better condition (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 21.1).
- **Burned clean, as once washed by the flood.** St. Augustine: the judgment comes first. Then this world passes away by fire, as it once did by water. What burns away is the corruptible quality of things, so that a world made new may fit bodies made new (*City of God* 20.16). Elsewhere he says that no nature is evil; evil is the fault of wills. When the soul is restored to God, the body is restored to its first strength, and the saints possess the world instead of being possessed by it (1 Corinthians 3:21–23) (*Of True Religion* 23.44).
- **Established forever.** Cassiodorus asks how the psalm can say that God established the heavens forever (Psalm 148:6) if there is to be a new heaven. He answers that a man is still himself when he rises, and so are heaven and earth when they are made new. Paul says the same of our bodies (1 Corinthians 15:54) (*Exposition of the Psalms* 148.6).

**"The sea was no more."** Augustine is not sure whether the sea will be dried up or changed into something better. He inclines to a figure: this present age, restless and stormy with the lives of mortals, will be over (*City of God* 20.16). Andrew gives a plain reason and the same figure. There will be no need of ships or trade, and no more turbulence or fear among the saints.

**The new Jerusalem coming down.**

- **Coming down since the beginning.** For Augustine the city comes down from heaven because God makes her by heavenly grace. Her descent began with time itself, as her citizens have been born on earth through the bath of rebirth. Only after the last judgment will her brightness be such that no trace of age remains, when bodies pass to incorruption (*City of God* 20.17).
- **The saints themselves.** Apringius of Beja: the city is the multitude of the saints who will come with the Lord (Zechariah 14:5), adorned with holiness and united to him forever (*Tractate on the Apocalypse* 21.2). Oecumenius calls it the blessed destiny and dwelling of the saints, described in splendid images to lead our minds from things seen to spiritual joy.
- **City and bride.** Andrew: she is built of the saints as of holy stones (Zechariah 9:16), with Christ as cornerstone. She is called a city because the Trinity dwells and walks in her, and a bride because she is joined to the Lord in the closest union. Her adornment is within, as the psalm says of the king's daughter (Psalm 45:14–15) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 21.2).
- **St. Irenaeus** sees the earthly Jerusalem as an image of this one, and the tabernacle Moses made as its pattern (Exodus 25:40). He insists that the promise is real and not an allegory, as the resurrection is real (*Against Heresies* 5.35.2). He wrote this while expecting an earthly kingdom of the just before the end, the expectation the Church did not keep; see [The dragon bound](/the-dragon-bound).

**"God's dwelling is with the human race."** The saints themselves become his temple, says Apringius. Oecumenius hears St. Paul: we shall always be with the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:17). Andrew calls this the true tent, of which the tent of Moses was a foreshadowing and the Church of the present day is the type. St. Bede: God himself will be the reward of the elect, who possess him forever because they are possessed by him (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 21.3).

**No more tears, death or pain.**

- **Tertullian** argues from causes. Sorrow and death come from the afflictions of soul and body. Where could these come from in the presence of God, with the devil and his angels cast out? (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 58).
- **Origen** connects the end of death with the end of the devil's power: the last enemy to be destroyed is death (1 Corinthians 15:26), when what is mortal is swallowed up by life (2 Corinthians 5:4) (*Homilies on Joshua* 8.4).
- **Oecumenius** quotes Isaiah on the tears wiped from every face (Isaiah 25:8) and on sorrow and mourning that flee away (Isaiah 51:11). The suffering of the saints is over, and the reward of their labors has come.
- **Primasius** adds a point for the present life. These gifts belong to the life to come. Here, the holier a person is, the more he weeps in prayer (Psalm 42:4; Psalm 6:7) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 21.4).
- **Andrew** reads "the old order has passed away" both ways: the suffering of the saints has ceased, and so has the arrogance of the wicked. **Bede** says that mourning, crying and mortality will remain in Gehenna alone.

**"These words are trustworthy and true… They are accomplished."** Andrew: they are true because Truth himself speaks them, and they are no longer given in symbols but known in the things themselves. Oecumenius notes the tense: they "are done," not they "will be done," and nothing in them is false. Primasius says the words are to be believed, not explained; the past tense is used so that no one may doubt the future. Apringius: the promise is fulfilled so swiftly that it may be counted as done.

**The Alpha and the Omega.** God is without beginning and without end (Oecumenius). Andrew applies it to Christ: he is first by his divinity and last by his humanity, and his care reaches from the first creature to the last. Bede notes that the title has been given three times (1:8; here; 22:13), so that no one may think there is a god before him or after him (Isaiah 43:10). The one who brings the world to its end is the one who made it.

**"To the thirsty I will give a gift from the spring of life-giving water."** Oecumenius asks how the gift can be free when the saints reach it through such hardship. It is free because no labor is worth what is given (Romans 8:18). The thirst is the one the Lord blessed (Matthew 5:6). Primasius: from this spring God refreshes believers who are still on the way, and gives abundantly to those who have arrived, so that no one is left without drink in the desert. St. Caesarius of Arles reads the spring of the forgiveness of sins in the font of baptism (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 18). Andrew reads it of the joy of the Holy Spirit.

**The victor and the coward (21:7–8).** The victor, says Andrew, is the one who conquers in the war against the unseen demons. He becomes a son of God and enjoys the Father's gifts. The cowards are those who deserted in that battle. God urges us in every way, by kindness and by warning, setting the brightness of the heavenly Jerusalem beside the darkness of Gehenna, for he desires the conversion of sinners and not their death. Andrew ends with a prayer that we not receive the grace of God in vain (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 21.8). Oecumenius takes the "cowards" for those who are weak toward every good work, since endurance is a matter of the will. Bede notes that Scripture always mixes the severe with the consoling, so that we stay watchful, as the psalm does (Psalm 145:20). The cowardly are named with the unbelieving because whoever doubts the reward will fear the trial. Of the many kinds of liar, the worst is the one who lies in religion (Revelation 2:9) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 21.8).

**"I will show you the bride, the wife of the Lamb" (21:9).** Andrew notices who speaks. The angel who once brought a plague now shows John the blessedness of the Church: angels are physicians, who at one time cut and at another apply healing. She is rightly called the wife of the Lamb, because Christ betrothed her with his own blood when he was slain as a lamb. As the woman was formed from Adam's side while he slept, so the Church was formed from the blood that flowed from the side of Christ as he slept in death on the cross (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 21.9). Bede explains the two names. She is bride and wife because, remaining pure, she is always bearing spiritual children for God; or because she is betrothed now and will then be led to the wedding feast that has no end (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 21.9). Oecumenius recalls the Letter to the Hebrews, which this study quotes on [The Lamb on Mount Zion](/the-lamb-on-mount-zion): the heavenly Jerusalem and the assembly of the firstborn enrolled in heaven (Hebrews 12:18–23).

*Source: the Fathers are cited from* Revelation, *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 21:1–9.*

## Where readers differ

- **A new world or this one renewed.** "Passed away" could suggest replacement. The Church speaks of renewal and transformation: the universe "will be perfectly re-established in Christ" (CCC 1042). How, she says, we do not know (CCC 1048). The Fathers cited above all read it as renewal.
- **Present or future.** The notes of the New American Bible read the new Jerusalem as "the church," and comment on "They are accomplished" that "God's reign has already begun." The Catechism reads these verses of the consummation. Both are held: the Bride exists now, and is not yet perfected. [[auth:disputed]]
- **Mary and the Bride.** Scripture's Bride is the Church. Calling Mary the Bride's image and beginning is the Church's teaching (CCC 773, 972); identifying her simply as the Bride of this chapter would go beyond it.

## What it asks of us

To know what we are being prepared for. The word for the Church's present life is "prepared": a bride being made ready. Everything in the Christian life that purifies, including what is painful, belongs to that.

And to take the promise personally. The voice does not say that suffering will be explained. It says that God himself "will wipe every tear from their eyes." The one who sat on the throne through every vision of this book rises, at the end, to do that.
