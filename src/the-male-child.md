## The words

- **A son, a male child.** The Greek says it twice, *huion, arsen*. The doubling comes from Isaiah, where Zion "delivers a male child" before her pangs have come (Isaiah 66:7). John is marking the birth as the one the prophet foretold.
- **To rule.** The verb is *poimainein*, "to shepherd." The English of the psalm keeps it: "With an iron rod you will shepherd them" (Psalm 2:9). The rod is a shepherd's staff made of iron: it guards the flock and breaks what attacks it.
- **Caught up.** The verb is *hērpasthē*, "snatched away." It is a strong word: the dragon stands ready to devour, and the child is taken out of his reach. St. Paul uses the same verb of those who "will be caught up" to meet the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:17).
- **To God and his throne.** In Revelation the throne is the center of everything (chapter 4). The child does not merely escape; he reigns.

## The psalm behind the verse

Psalm 2 is the key. God says to his anointed king: "You are my son; today I have begotten you. Ask it of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and, as your possession, the ends of the earth. With an iron rod you will shepherd them" (Psalm 2:7–9). The psalm begins with the nations raging "against the LORD and against his anointed," and ends with their kings warned to serve him.

Revelation uses this psalm three times, and the three uses belong together:

| Where | Who rules with the iron rod |
| --- | --- |
| 12:5 | The child, at his birth: he is "destined" to rule |
| 19:15 | The rider called Faithful and True, at the end: "He will rule them with an iron rod" |
| 2:26–27 | The Christian who conquers: "To the victor, who keeps to my ways until the end, I will give authority over the nations. He will rule them with an iron rod… just as I received authority from my Father" |

The verse about the child is therefore a promise in two directions. It looks forward to chapter 19, where the destiny is carried out. And it reaches back to the letter to Thyatira, where the same reign is promised to the faithful. [[auth:scripture]]

## Who the child is

**Jesus Christ.** No other reading does justice to Psalm 2 and to the throne. The Church's faith in the verse's second half is the article of the Creed: he "ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father." "Being seated at the Father's right hand signifies the inauguration of the Messiah's kingdom, the fulfilment of the prophet Daniel's vision concerning the Son of man" (CCC 664). [[auth:church]]

- **St. Victorinus** reads the catching up as the Ascension: "her son was caught up to God, and to His throne," "just as speaking with the disciples He was caught up to heaven" ([*Commentary on the Apocalypse*](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ENG1016/_PC.HTM), on chapter 12; Acts 1:9). [[auth:father]]
- **The notes of the New American Bible** agree: "reference to Christ's ascension" ([note on 12:5](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/12)). [[auth:theologian]]

## From the birth to the throne

The verse passes from the child's birth to his enthronement and says nothing of what lies between: no ministry, no Cross, no tomb. Three things can be said about that silence.

1. **It is the dragon's view of the story.** He waited to devour, and the child is on the throne. From where he stands, that is the whole of it.
2. **The Cross is not absent from the chapter.** Six verses later the martyrs "conquered him by the blood of the Lamb" (12:11). The victory that the catching up displays was won by the Passion.
3. **The birth itself may include the Passion.** The psalm's "today I have begotten you" is applied by St. Paul to the Resurrection (Acts 13:33), and the Lord called his Passion a woman's hour of labor (John 16:21). Some Catholic scholars therefore read the "birth" of 12:5 as the Messiah's birth into glory through death. St. Victorinus points the same way when he says the dragon "could not devour Him—that is, detain Him in death." This is discussed on [The birth pangs](/the-birth-pangs). [[auth:theologian]]

## The child and his members

Two early Fathers read the verse of the Church's children, and their reading explains why Revelation 2 gives the iron rod to the victor.

- **St. Hippolytus** keeps Christ at the center and sees the birth continuing: "the Church, always bringing forth Christ, the perfect man-child of God, who is declared to be God and man, becomes the instructor of all the nations." That the child is caught up to the throne signifies "that he who is always born of her is a heavenly king, and not an earthly," as David said: "The LORD says to my lord: 'Sit at my right hand'" ([*On Christ and Antichrist* 61](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1872/versions/treatise-on-christ-and-antichrist/divisions/62); Psalm 110:1). [[auth:father]]
- **St. Methodius** goes further and denies that the verse speaks of Christ's own birth: "long before the Apocalypse, the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word was fulfilled," and "John speaks concerning things present and things to come." For him the child is the baptized: "the Church labours and gives birth to those who are baptized" ([*The Banquet* VIII.7](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ENG1159/_P1Q.HTM)). [[auth:father]]

Methodius's denial has not been followed; the Church reads the child as Christ. But what he affirms is sound and is in the text. Christ is the firstborn "among many brothers" (Romans 8:29). What is said of the Head is promised to the members: the iron rod (2:26–27), and the throne itself, "I will give the victor the right to sit with me on my throne, as I myself first won the victory and sit with my Father on his throne" (3:21).

## Where readers differ

- **Bethlehem or Easter.** Most read the birth as the Nativity and the catching up as the Ascension. Some read the whole verse of the Paschal mystery. The two do not conflict in doctrine; they differ on which moment John has in view. [[auth:disputed]]
- **Christ alone, or Christ with his members.** Victorinus reads Christ; Methodius reads the baptized; Hippolytus reads Christ as always born in the Church. Read with Revelation 2:26–27 and 3:21, the fullest answer is Christ first, and in him all who are his.
- **"Caught up."** Because the same verb describes the faithful in 1 Thessalonians 4:17, some modern writers have found in this verse a catching up of the Church before a time of trial. The Fathers quoted here do not read it so; the Woman remains on earth through the whole trial (12:6, 13–17). For the Catholic reading of 1 Thessalonians 4, see The Lamb's Victory on [the rapture](https://thelambsvictory.org/topics/the-rapture).

## What it asks of us

The chapter has hardly begun, and the decisive thing has already happened: the child is on the throne. Everything the dragon does after this, he does as one who has lost. The Catechism describes exactly this time: Christ's reign "is still under attack by the evil powers, even though they have been defeated definitively by Christ's Passover" (CCC 671).

We are asked to live as people who know how the story has already turned, and to "keep to his ways until the end" (2:26), since the promise made to the child is made also to those who conquer with him.
