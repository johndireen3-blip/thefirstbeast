## What the text shows

An angel shows John "the judgment on the great harlot who lives near the many waters. The kings of the earth have had intercourse with her" (Revelation 17:1–2). He sees "a woman seated on a scarlet beast that was covered with blasphemous names, with seven heads and ten horns." She is dressed in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold and pearls, and holds "a gold cup that was filled with the abominable and sordid deeds of her harlotry." On her forehead is a name: "Babylon the great, the mother of harlots and of the abominations of the earth." She is "drunk on the blood of the holy ones and on the blood of the witnesses to Jesus" (17:3–6).

The angel explains. The beast "existed once but now exists no longer," and will come again from the abyss (17:8). Its seven heads are seven hills and seven kings, and the beast is an eighth (17:9–11). Its ten horns are ten kings who "have not yet been crowned," who receive authority with the beast "for one hour," are "of one mind," and give their power to the beast; they fight the Lamb, and the Lamb conquers them (17:12–14). The waters are "peoples, nations, and tongues" (17:15). Then: "The ten horns that you saw and the beast will hate the harlot; they will leave her desolate and naked; they will eat her flesh and consume her with fire. For God has put it into their minds to carry out his purpose" (17:16–17). "The woman whom you saw represents the great city that has sovereignty over the kings of the earth" (17:18).

## Her fall

Chapter 18 sings the fall: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great" (18:2). The kings who lay with her weep "when they see the smoke of her pyre" (18:9); the merchants mourn, for no one buys their cargo any more, a cargo that ends with "slaves, that is, human beings" (18:11–13). "All nations were led astray by your magic potion" (18:23), and "in her was found the blood of prophets and holy ones" (18:24). She had said in her heart, "I sit enthroned as queen; I am no widow, and I will never know grief" (18:7).

## The cities of the prophets

John builds the harlot from the prophets' judgments on the great cities. Babylon was "a golden cup in the hand of the LORD, making the whole earth drunk" (Jeremiah 51:7) and said, "I shall remain always, a sovereign mistress forever!" and "I shall never be a widow" (Isaiah 47:7–8). Tyre, the merchant city, is called a harlot who trades with all the kingdoms of the earth (Isaiah 23:15–17), and Ezekiel's lament over Tyre lists its cargo as John lists Babylon's (Ezekiel 27). Nineveh is "a charming mistress of witchcraft, who enslaved nations with her prostitution" (Nahum 3:4). The harlot is every great city that makes wealth, power and pleasure its god.

## Two women, two cities

Revelation sets the harlot against the bride. The harlot is clothed in purple and scarlet and drunk with the blood of the saints; the bride of the Lamb "was allowed to wear a bright, clean linen garment" (19:7–8). Babylon falls; the holy city, the new Jerusalem, comes down from heaven "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (21:2).

## First fulfillment

The NABRE notes are plain: "Babylon, the symbolic name (Rev 17:5) of Rome, is graphically described as 'the great harlot,'" the image of one "who leads people astray into idolatry," with "the great wealth and idolatrous cults of Rome." The seven hills are Rome's (17:9), and the ten horns are "perhaps Parthian satraps" who would turn on "harlot Rome" (NABRE notes on 17:9, 12–13, 16).

## The Fathers

St. Hippolytus calls her "this unclean harlot" and reads the woman as "that great city, which reigns over the kings of the earth" (*On Christ and Antichrist* 29, 39) [[auth:father]]. St. Irenaeus reads the ten kings as the kingdoms into which the empire will be divided, who "shall give their kingdom to the beast" (*Against Heresies* V.26.1) [[auth:father]]. St. Augustine sees in Babylon the earthly city, formed by "the love of self, even to the contempt of God," set against the city of God (*City of God* XIV.28) [[auth:father]].

### The commentators on the Apocalypse

The later commentators take the chapter verse by verse. They agree that the harlot is a city or a people given over to idolatry. They differ on whether she is Rome, and on who the seven kings are. [[auth:father]]

**Who is the harlot?**

- **Rome.** St. Victorinus: the saints suffered by decree of the Senate of that city, which gave the nations their laws against the faith (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.2). Oecumenius: she is called a harlot because she turned from God, which Scripture calls fornication (Psalm 73:27), and led the nations she ruled into the same idolatry. The seven hills settle it, for no other city is said to stand on seven (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.1–5, 9–14).
- **Earthly rule as a whole.** St. Andrew of Caesarea knows that reading and looks wider. Old Rome lost its dominion long ago. Scripture gives such names to cities for their conduct: the prophets call old Babylon a harlot, and St. Peter calls Rome "Babylon" (1 Peter 5:13). So he reads either the earthly kingdom taken as one body, or whatever city holds power when the Antichrist comes. Every city that delights in bloodshed bears the name (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.1–3, 6–7).
- **The multitude of the lost.** Primasius and St. Bede read a people: those who, forsaken by their Creator, have given themselves over to demons. Bede sets her against the company of believers, who were of one heart and betrothed to one husband (Acts 4:32; 2 Corinthians 11:2) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.1–2). St. Caesarius of Arles says there is one body that opposes the Church from within and from without: false Christians inside, heretics and pagans outside, working with "a unity of spirit" (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 14).

**The wilderness and the scarlet beast.** For Primasius the desert is the absence of God, whose presence is paradise. For Caesarius the woman sits on those who are dead in soul. Andrew calls every city a wilderness that is drunk in soul and unfaithful to God. Oecumenius and Andrew take the beast she rides for the devil, scarlet with the blood of the saints, on whom she rests through her evil deeds. Primasius reads the body arrayed against the Lamb, and repeats a rule of Tyconius: in this book the beast is at one time the devil, at another the head that seemed slain, at another the people of the beast (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.3).

**The gold cup.** Tyconius: the purple is the pretence of her false rule, the scarlet her bloodshed, and the gold is hypocrisy, fair outside and full of uncleanness within (Matthew 23:27–28) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.4). Andrew: the cup is the sweetness of evil deeds before they are tasted, and she is never sated by them. St. Cyprian draws a plain lesson for Christian women: Scripture shows a harlot city finely dressed, and destined to perish for that very finery (*The Dress of Virgins* 12).

**Seven spirits against seven.** Primasius gives this passage its most searching reading. The beast's power is in its ten horns and its fraud in its seven heads, and the fraud is the worse. The Lord warned of the unclean spirit who returns with seven others more wicked than himself (Matthew 12:43–45). Primasius sets each against a gift of the Holy Spirit (Isaiah 11:2–3): the evil spirit is worse because it feigns wisdom, feigns discipline, feigns prudence, hides weakness under a show of strength, steals knowledge, wears "a false piety," and deceives by a show of religion. Those who could not be broken by terror are to be won by an imitation of the truth (2 Thessalonians 2:8–10) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.3).

**"It was, and is not, and is to come" (17:8).**

- **The devil before and after the Cross.** Andrew and Bede: he was powerful before the Cross; by it he lost his hold on the nations; at the end he will come again through the Antichrist, and go to destruction (Andrew, *Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.8).
- **A people that renews itself.** Primasius and Caesarius: the body of the wicked passes away and is replaced, one generation copying the last. Caesarius adds a prayer that, though we cannot avoid all contact with them, we may be so separated in conduct as not to share their end (Matthew 25:34, 41) (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 14).
- **Not in God's remembrance.** Oecumenius: the saints are said to "be," because they are in Christ; the ungodly, though they exist, are not in the memory of God.

Those who marvel at the beast are those not written in the book of life. Primasius says they will learn too late that the one they took for Christ is not, and that our Christ is the judge; the Lord knows those who are his (2 Timothy 2:19).

**The seven kings (17:9–11).** Here the Fathers give five readings, which shows how little the Church has settled.

- **St. Victorinus** reads the emperors of John's own day: Galba, Otho, Vitellius, Vespasian and Titus have fallen; Domitian is; Nerva is to come and stay a short while. The eighth he takes for Nero, expected to return (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.2).
- **Oecumenius** reads the seven emperors who persecuted the Church most: Nero, Domitian, Trajan, Severus, Decius, Valerian and Diocletian. The devil is counted with them because he was their guiding power.
- **Andrew** reads seven kingdoms that have ruled the world in turn, from Assyria, Media, Babylon, Persia and Macedonia to old Rome and the new Rome of Constantine. The Antichrist is an eighth who comes from the seven.
- **St. Hippolytus** reads seven ages of the world, each of a thousand years, with the sabbath of the saints to follow (*Commentary on Daniel* 2.4–5). His count placed the end about five centuries after Christ. That date is long past, and the Church sets none (CCC 673).
- **Primasius and Bede** read no list. Seven is the fullness of earthly rule, whose last period has not yet come. Bede adds that those days will be shortened in mercy, to humble the proud and spare the weak (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.10).

**The ten kings and their one hour (17:12–14).** Oecumenius and Andrew read ten kings at the end, arising from the Roman rule as Daniel foresaw (Daniel 7:24), whose reign is brief. They are "of one mind" only in this, that all alike are overcome by the Antichrist. Primasius and Bede read every kingdom of the world, whose boasting will be fiercest when it also deceives by signs. They make war on the Lamb, and the Lamb conquers them, for he did not lose his kingship by becoming man, and he shares it with his elect (Andrew, *Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.13–14).

**The kings turn on the harlot (17:15–18).**

- **A prize fought over.** Oecumenius supposes the city will be ravaged in wars among the kings, each wanting her as the prize of victory (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.15–18).
- **The devil as God's instrument.** Andrew finds it remarkable that the devil is "both enemy and avenger": through the ten horns he opposes Christ, and through them he also lays waste the city that left God's laws for its pleasures.
- **They hate themselves.** Caesarius reads the harlot as the life of luxury lived by plunder. The proud and dissolute persecute the saints and also come to hate one another: they bite and devour each other (Galatians 5:15) (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 15).
- **God's purpose in their hearts.** Primasius hears St. Paul: God gave them up to do what they should not (Romans 1:28). His words are fulfilled in justice and in mercy.

**The waters and the chalice.** St. Cyprian takes the angel's explanation, that the waters are peoples, into his teaching on the Eucharist. In the chalice the wine shows the blood of Christ and the water shows the people. When they are mixed, the people are joined to Christ and cannot be separated from him. So neither wine alone nor water alone may be offered (*Letter* 63.12–13).

**Two cities.** Bede ends where this page ends. There are two cities in the world, one that rises from the abyss and one that comes down from heaven. The kings of the earth will bring their glory into the second (Revelation 21:24) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.18).

## The Church [[auth:church]]

The harlot is idolatry of wealth and power. Man commits idolatry "whenever he honors and reveres a creature in place of God," including "power, pleasure… the state, money" (CCC 2113); an economy that makes profit "the exclusive norm and ultimate end" is morally unacceptable (CCC 2424). Pope Leo XIV sets the same choice before our age: to "construct a new Tower of Babel" or to "build the city in which God and humanity dwell together," and he calls Babel "a project conceived without reference to God" (*Magnifica Humanitas* 7).

## Where interpreters differ [[auth:disputed]]

- **Rome:** the first meaning, in the NABRE notes and most modern commentary.
- **The earthly city in every age:** St. Augustine, St. Andrew of Caesarea, Primasius and St. Bede; every civilization that organizes itself without God.
- **Apostate Jerusalem:** some modern scholars, reading the harlot as the city that killed the prophets.
- **A world economic and commercial system:** many modern readers, reading chapter 18's merchants and cargo.

The Church has not decided which the text intends at the end.

## The response asked of us

"Depart from her, my people, so as not to take part in her sins and receive a share in her plagues" (Revelation 18:4). Do not drink from her cup, and do not mourn her as the merchants do. The beast and the kings that served her destroy her in the end; the bride does not need her. "They will fight with the Lamb, but the Lamb will conquer them" (17:14).
