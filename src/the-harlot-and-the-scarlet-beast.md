## What the text shows

An angel shows John "the judgment on the great harlot who lives near the many waters. The kings of the earth have had intercourse with her" (Revelation 17:1–2). He sees "a woman seated on a scarlet beast that was covered with blasphemous names, with seven heads and ten horns." She is dressed in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold and pearls, and holds "a gold cup that was filled with the abominable and sordid deeds of her harlotry." On her forehead is a name: "Babylon the great, the mother of harlots and of the abominations of the earth." She is "drunk on the blood of the holy ones and on the blood of the witnesses to Jesus" (17:3–6).

The angel explains. The beast "existed once but now exists no longer," and will come again from the abyss (17:8). Its seven heads are seven hills and seven kings, and the beast is an eighth (17:9–11). Its ten horns are ten kings who "have not yet been crowned," who receive authority with the beast "for one hour," are "of one mind," and give their power to the beast; they fight the Lamb, and the Lamb conquers them (17:12–14). The waters are "peoples, nations, and tongues" (17:15). Then: "The ten horns that you saw and the beast will hate the harlot; they will leave her desolate and naked; they will eat her flesh and consume her with fire. For God has put it into their minds to carry out his purpose" (17:16–17). "The woman whom you saw represents the great city that has sovereignty over the kings of the earth" (17:18).

## Her fall

Chapter 18 sings the fall: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great" (18:2). The kings who lay with her weep "when they see the smoke of her pyre" (18:9); the merchants mourn, for no one buys their cargo any more, a cargo that ends with "slaves, that is, human beings" (18:11–13). "All nations were led astray by your magic potion" (18:23), and "in her was found the blood of prophets and holy ones" (18:24). She had said in her heart, "I sit enthroned as queen; I am no widow, and I will never know grief" (18:7).

## The cities of the prophets

John builds the harlot from the prophets' judgments on the great cities. Babylon was "a golden cup in the hand of the LORD, making the whole earth drunk" (Jeremiah 51:7) and said, "I shall remain always, a sovereign mistress forever!" and "I shall never be a widow" (Isaiah 47:7–8). Tyre, the merchant city, is called a harlot who trades with all the kingdoms of the earth (Isaiah 23:15–17), and Ezekiel's lament over Tyre lists its cargo as John lists Babylon's (Ezekiel 27). Nineveh is "a charming mistress of witchcraft, who enslaved nations with her prostitution" (Nahum 3:4). The harlot is every great city that makes wealth, power and pleasure its god.

## Two women, two cities

Revelation sets the harlot against the bride. The harlot is clothed in purple and scarlet and drunk with the blood of the saints; the bride of the Lamb "was allowed to wear a bright, clean linen garment" (19:7–8). Babylon falls; the holy city, the new Jerusalem, comes down from heaven "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (21:2).

## First fulfillment

The NABRE notes are plain: "Babylon, the symbolic name (Rev 17:5) of Rome, is graphically described as 'the great harlot,'" the image of one "who leads people astray into idolatry," with "the great wealth and idolatrous cults of Rome." The seven hills are Rome's (17:9), and the ten horns are "perhaps Parthian satraps" who would turn on "harlot Rome" (NABRE notes on 17:9, 12–13, 16).

## The Fathers

St. Hippolytus calls her "this unclean harlot" and reads the woman as "that great city, which reigns over the kings of the earth" (*On Christ and Antichrist* 29, 39) [[auth:father]]. St. Irenaeus reads the ten kings as the kingdoms into which the empire will be divided, who "shall give their kingdom to the beast" (*Against Heresies* V.26.1) [[auth:father]]. St. Augustine sees in Babylon the earthly city, formed by "the love of self, even to the contempt of God," set against the city of God (*City of God* XIV.28) [[auth:father]].

### The commentators on the Apocalypse

The later commentators take the chapter verse by verse. They agree that the harlot is a city or a people given over to idolatry. They differ on whether she is Rome, and on who the seven kings are. [[auth:father]]

**Who is the harlot?**

- **Rome.** St. Victorinus: the saints suffered by decree of the Senate of that city, which gave the nations their laws against the faith (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.2). Oecumenius: she is called a harlot because she turned from God, which Scripture calls fornication (Psalm 73:27), and led the nations she ruled into the same idolatry. The seven hills settle it, for no other city is said to stand on seven (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.1–5, 9–14).
- **Earthly rule as a whole.** St. Andrew of Caesarea knows that reading and looks wider. Old Rome lost its dominion long ago. Scripture gives such names to cities for their conduct: the prophets call old Babylon a harlot, and St. Peter calls Rome "Babylon" (1 Peter 5:13). So he reads either the earthly kingdom taken as one body, or whatever city holds power when the Antichrist comes. Every city that delights in bloodshed bears the name (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.1–3, 6–7).
- **The multitude of the lost.** Primasius and St. Bede read a people: those who, forsaken by their Creator, have given themselves over to demons. Bede sets her against the company of believers, who were of one heart and betrothed to one husband (Acts 4:32; 2 Corinthians 11:2) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.1–2). St. Caesarius of Arles says there is one body that opposes the Church from within and from without: false Christians inside, heretics and pagans outside, working with "a unity of spirit" (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 14).

**The wilderness and the scarlet beast.** For Primasius the desert is the absence of God, whose presence is paradise. For Caesarius the woman sits on those who are dead in soul. Andrew calls every city a wilderness that is drunk in soul and unfaithful to God. Oecumenius and Andrew take the beast she rides for the devil, scarlet with the blood of the saints, on whom she rests through her evil deeds. Primasius reads the body arrayed against the Lamb, and repeats a rule of Tyconius: in this book the beast is at one time the devil, at another the head that seemed slain, at another the people of the beast (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.3).

**The gold cup.** Tyconius: the purple is the pretence of her false rule, the scarlet her bloodshed, and the gold is hypocrisy, fair outside and full of uncleanness within (Matthew 23:27–28) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.4). Andrew: the cup is the sweetness of evil deeds before they are tasted, and she is never sated by them. St. Cyprian draws a plain lesson for Christian women: Scripture shows a harlot city finely dressed, and destined to perish for that very finery (*The Dress of Virgins* 12).

**Seven spirits against seven.** Primasius gives this passage its most searching reading. The beast's power is in its ten horns and its fraud in its seven heads, and the fraud is the worse. The Lord warned of the unclean spirit who returns with seven others more wicked than himself (Matthew 12:43–45). Primasius sets each against a gift of the Holy Spirit (Isaiah 11:2–3): the evil spirit is worse because it feigns wisdom, feigns discipline, feigns prudence, hides weakness under a show of strength, steals knowledge, wears "a false piety," and deceives by a show of religion. Those who could not be broken by terror are to be won by an imitation of the truth (2 Thessalonians 2:8–10) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.3).

**"It was, and is not, and is to come" (17:8).**

- **The devil before and after the Cross.** Andrew and Bede: he was powerful before the Cross; by it he lost his hold on the nations; at the end he will come again through the Antichrist, and go to destruction (Andrew, *Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.8).
- **A people that renews itself.** Primasius and Caesarius: the body of the wicked passes away and is replaced, one generation copying the last. Caesarius adds a prayer that, though we cannot avoid all contact with them, we may be so separated in conduct as not to share their end (Matthew 25:34, 41) (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 14).
- **Not in God's remembrance.** Oecumenius: the saints are said to "be," because they are in Christ; the ungodly, though they exist, are not in the memory of God.

Those who marvel at the beast are those not written in the book of life. Primasius says they will learn too late that the one they took for Christ is not, and that our Christ is the judge; the Lord knows those who are his (2 Timothy 2:19).

**The seven kings (17:9–11).** Here the Fathers give five readings, which shows how little the Church has settled.

- **St. Victorinus** reads the emperors of John's own day: Galba, Otho, Vitellius, Vespasian and Titus have fallen; Domitian is; Nerva is to come and stay a short while. The eighth he takes for Nero, expected to return (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.2).
- **Oecumenius** reads the seven emperors who persecuted the Church most: Nero, Domitian, Trajan, Severus, Decius, Valerian and Diocletian. The devil is counted with them because he was their guiding power.
- **Andrew** reads seven kingdoms that have ruled the world in turn, from Assyria, Media, Babylon, Persia and Macedonia to old Rome and the new Rome of Constantine. The Antichrist is an eighth who comes from the seven.
- **St. Hippolytus** reads seven ages of the world, each of a thousand years, with the sabbath of the saints to follow (*Commentary on Daniel* 2.4–5). His count placed the end about five centuries after Christ. That date is long past, and the Church sets none (CCC 673).
- **Primasius and Bede** read no list. Seven is the fullness of earthly rule, whose last period has not yet come. Bede adds that those days will be shortened in mercy, to humble the proud and spare the weak (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.10). Primasius offers a moral reading as well. The beast creeps in through the delights of the senses, as death is said to come in through our windows (Jeremiah 9:20). The five kings who have fallen are the five senses, like the five husbands of the Samaritan woman; and the one she now has, who is not her husband (John 4:18), is error (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.9).

**The ten kings and their one hour (17:12–14).** Oecumenius and Andrew read ten kings at the end, arising from the Roman rule as Daniel foresaw (Daniel 7:24), whose reign is brief. The "one hour" Andrew takes either for that brevity or, as a figure, for one season of the year, three months. Bede reports an older tradition, found in St. Jerome on Daniel, that the Antichrist would arise from Babylon and overcome the kings of Egypt, Africa and Ethiopia, after which the other seven would submit; he gives it as what "some" say, and does not make it his own (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.12). They are "of one mind" only in this, that all alike are overcome by the Antichrist. Primasius and Bede read every kingdom of the world, whose boasting will be fiercest when it also deceives by signs. They make war on the Lamb, and the Lamb conquers them, for he did not lose his kingship by becoming man, and he shares it with his elect (Andrew, *Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.13–14).

**The kings turn on the harlot (17:15–18).**

- **A prize fought over.** Oecumenius supposes the city will be ravaged in wars among the kings, each wanting her as the prize of victory (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 17.15–18).
- **The devil as God's instrument.** Andrew finds it remarkable that the devil is "both enemy and avenger": through the ten horns he opposes Christ, and through them he also lays waste the city that left God's laws for its pleasures.
- **They hate themselves.** Caesarius reads the harlot as the life of luxury lived by plunder. The proud and dissolute persecute the saints and also come to hate one another: they bite and devour each other (Galatians 5:15) (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 15).
- **God's purpose in their hearts.** Primasius hears St. Paul: God gave them up to do what they should not (Romans 1:28). His words are fulfilled in justice and in mercy.

**The waters and the chalice.** St. Cyprian takes the angel's explanation, that the waters are peoples, into his teaching on the Eucharist. In the chalice the wine shows the blood of Christ and the water shows the people. When they are mixed, the people are joined to Christ and cannot be separated from him. So neither wine alone nor water alone may be offered (*Letter* 63.12–13).

**Two cities.** Bede ends where this page ends. There are two cities in the world, one that rises from the abyss and one that comes down from heaven. The kings of the earth will bring their glory into the second (Revelation 21:24) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 17.18).

### The commentators on her fall

The same commentators, with St. Augustine and St. Jerome, read chapter 18. [[auth:father]]

**The seventh bowl: "God remembered great Babylon" (16:17–21).** Her fall is first announced at the last of the bowls: a voice from the throne says, "It is done"; an earthquake splits the great city into three parts; and God gives Babylon the cup of his wrath.

- **"It is done," and the earthquake.** Oecumenius reads the earthquake as the transformation of the visible world, as Haggai foretold (Haggai 2:6–7). St. Andrew of Caesarea hears in "It is done" the commandment of God accomplished. The lightning and thunder announce the coming of Christ as they once announced God's descent on Sinai, and the earthquake is the change of all that exists (Hebrews 12:26) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 16.17–18). St. Bede reads the end, when "the last enemy" is destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26). He adds that the passage does not make clear whether the great signs of that tribulation come from the good, from the evil, or from both, as with Moses and the magicians (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 16.17–18).
- **The great city and its three parts.** Oecumenius takes the great city for Jerusalem, set apart from the cities of the nations. St. Caesarius of Arles takes it for every people under heaven, in three parts: the Gentiles, the heretics and false Christians, and the catholic Church (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 13). Primasius reads a city great in population and haughty in its enterprises. His parts include the Gentiles, the heretics, and the false brothers who profess to know God and deny him by their deeds (Titus 1:16); the fall of the cities of the nations is the fall of all their strength and confidence (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 16.19). Andrew calls Jerusalem great in godliness, made great by the sufferings of Christ. Those who are truly Christians are now mingled with those who have the name alone, and "the cleansing fire of temptations" will unmask them. The fall of the cities is either their destruction or the end of the pagan way of life at the coming of the kingdom the saints will possess (Daniel 7:18) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 16.19).
- **"God remembered great Babylon."** Oecumenius distinguishes this Babylon from the one named in chapter 14. He thinks Rome is meant, with those who will capture it, and that God remembers its former sins, when the saints were persecuted and killed (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 16.17–21). Primasius reads the multitude of all the lost, receiving the retribution they earned. Andrew reads the thronging multitude confounded by the distractions of life and made great by unjust wealth; God passes, as it were, from a merciful forgetfulness to remembrance.
- **Islands, mountains and hail.** Oecumenius reads the islands as the churches of the nations, which rose above idolatry (Psalm 97:1), or else, with the mountains, as the ranks of the demons, which will be wiped out (Psalm 97:5). Primasius reads the islands and mountains as the Church, which withdraws from the ruin, as the prophet says, "Flee from Babylon" (Jeremiah 51:6). The hail is the wrath to come (1 Thessalonians 1:10), and the blasphemy that follows is both a sin and its punishment (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 16.20–21). Andrew says that under the hail people prove harder than Pharaoh, who at least gave way for a time.

**The angel whose glory lights the earth (18:1).** Primasius reads the coming of Christ, whom Isaiah calls the angel of great counsel (Isaiah 9:5) and who is "the true light" (John 1:9); a stronger one has come against the strong, and the wisdom of this world is overthrown as Jericho was (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.1). St. Andrew of Caesarea reads the brightness of the holy powers, which outshines the stars. St. Bede reads both the Lord made flesh and the teachers of the Church, who preach the fall of the world when they say that the kingdom of heaven is at hand (Matthew 4:17) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 18.1).

**"Fallen, fallen is Babylon" (18:2).**

- **A city left to demons.** Oecumenius: demons hate humankind and seek out the places where blood has been shed, and wild creatures take over what people abandon. Isaiah said the same of the first Babylon (Isaiah 13:21–22) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.1–3). Andrew recalls that Isaiah foretold this of the Chaldean capital when Cyrus and the Persians took it.
- **Fallen already, by her sins.** Primasius, following Tyconius, asks how a fallen city can still be a dwelling. By collapsing under the weight of its sins it showed itself fit for demons. The city that rose again and again against the faith in waves of persecution now growls within narrower limits, like the sea gathered "as in a bottle" in his psalm (Psalm 33:7). The birds are evil spirits, like those Abraham drove from the sacrifice (Genesis 15:11) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.2).
- **No city of stones.** St. Caesarius of Arles asks whether the ruins of one city could hold every unclean spirit. The only city that holds them all is the city of the devil, spread through the whole earth in wicked people. Babylon means "confusion" and names the arrogant, the robbers, the dissolute and the impious who persevere in their wickedness; Jerusalem, "vision of peace," names those who are holy before God (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homilies 15–16). Bede says the same in a line: there is no other city than that of the devil which receives every unclean spirit.

**The wine, the kings and the merchants (18:3).** Oecumenius reads her fornication as insatiable greed and the love of money; she bound the nations to her by tribute, and the merchants grew rich from her prodigal way of life. Primasius finds three sins named, avarice, licentiousness and gluttony, and three kinds of people: the nations are the promiscuous crowd, the kings those who seem for a time to succeed, the merchants those who "trade their souls" for temporal abundance, against the Lord's question, "What can one give in exchange for his life?" (Matthew 16:26). Caesarius: the kings corrupt one another, for all the kings of the earth could not sin with one woman; and the merchants are rich in sins, since excess in luxury makes for poverty and not for wealth (Homily 16). Andrew: she led the nations in every transgression, made their rulers enemies of truth by her gifts, consumed far beyond her needs, and was wanton toward those in need (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.3).

**"Come out of her, my people" (18:4–5).**

- **As Lot left Sodom.** Oecumenius: the angels told Lot to save his life and not look back (Genesis 19:17), and Revelation teaches the same. He adds St. Cyril's view that it was the Son and the Holy Spirit who visited Sodom, since the Father "has given all judgment to his Son" (John 5:22). The servants of Christ do not all live in one great city, Rome; the word is spoken to all of them. To share in the sins is to share in the plagues, since the plagues come because of the sins (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.4–8).
- **A separation of the will, not of the body.** Primasius: "The Lord knows those who are his" (2 Timothy 2:19). Until the end of the world the good allow the wicked to be mingled with them. "Go out from there, touch nothing unclean" (Isaiah 52:11) is fulfilled when the righteous no longer share the life of the sinful, less by a separation of body than by the difference of a sound will (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.4).
- **Babylon divided.** Caesarius: as long as God allows, the wicked are converted, and the part that leaves Babylon is the making of Jerusalem. One comes out of Babylon whenever one abandons an evil way of life. Citizens of both cities are found "in each house and in the one church and in each city"; they live together in the body and are far apart in the heart, for the good seek what is above (Colossians 3:2) (Homily 16).
- **St. Jerome to a friend in Rome.** Writing from Bethlehem for Paula and Eustochium, Jerome urges Marcella to join them. He sets the poor crevice of the earth where the Creator of the heavens was born beside the porticoes and gilded ceilings of the city, and quotes this verse. He grants at once that Rome has a holy church, the trophies of apostles and martyrs and a true confession of Christ. It is the display, the power and the size of the city, the seeing and being seen, that are foreign to the quiet of the monastic life (*Letter* 46.10–12).

**"Repay her double" (18:6–8).**

- **By the sword of the word.** St. Augustine, preaching on Psalm 149, sees the saints armed with two-edged swords, and the vengeance done daily by speaking. When she had power to persecute, she killed the flesh and could not crush God; now the pagans are no more as pagans and the idols are broken. The chastisement is reproof. When a friend begins to blush at having worshiped what he made, the sword has reached his heart, and he "is about to die that he may live" (*Explanation of Psalm 149*, 9).
- **Less than she deserves.** Oecumenius recalls that God does not take vengeance twice (Nahum 1:9). "Double" does not mean twofold. God loves humankind and punishes far less than is deserved, so that a part repaid is counted as double, and the prophet can even say sevenfold (Psalm 79:12). "Widow" means deprived of rule. He ends with a prayer that we be freed from this judgment by the grace of him who called us (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.4–8).
- **Time and eternity.** Primasius places these things at the second coming. Those who sinned in time are punished in perpetuity, and those who took pleasure in passing delights suffer lasting torment, and he recalls the Lord's woe to those who laugh now (Luke 6:25). The soul dies when God abandons it, as the body dies when the soul leaves. We cannot avoid these plagues by our own strength, only by the goodness of God; but "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God" (Wisdom 3:1) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.6–8).
- **Body and soul; here and hereafter.** Andrew gives three readings of "double": sinners are punished both here and in the age to come; soul and body are punished for what they did together; or God's kindness counts as double what falls short of strict justice, and much is suffered in the conscience. Those who prosper without the fear of God say, "I shall never be shaken" (Psalm 30:7). "One day" is the suddenness of it (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.6–8).
- **Isaiah's Babylon.** Apringius of Beja sets beside the passage Isaiah's word to the virgin daughter of Babylon, who said she would be mistress forever, and to whom widowhood and the loss of children come in one day (Isaiah 47:1–9). He takes the two prophecies as one (*Tractate on the Apocalypse* 18.6–8).
- **A song kept for home.** Bede: she delighted in the luxury of the present and did nothing about what was coming. The citizens of the heavenly city do not want the Lord's song in a foreign land (Psalm 137:4), that is, the joy of the age to come in this one (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 18.6–8).

**The laments of kings, merchants and seamen (18:9–19).**

- **They weep for themselves.** Caesarius: the kings do not bewail the evil of wealth. They see that they are losing the prosperity that made them subject to her pleasures. They stand far off in their souls, not in their bodies, each fearing for himself. Can a city wear fine linen and purple? People do. Could every merchant and sailor be present at the burning of one city? It is all who loved the world, afraid at the ruin of their own hope (Homily 16).
- **Two senses.** Bede: either at the judgment, when only regret remains and they say, "What did our pride avail us?" (Wisdom 5:8), or in the present age, whenever abundance ceases and nations fall. The smoke of her burning is the evidence of her ruin: what is the rioting and crumbling of this world but "the smoke of Gehenna" close at hand? In the cargo he finds everything pleasant to the five senses, metals for sight, spices for smell, ointments for touch, wine and wheat and oil for taste; and when the harpers fall silent, hearing is added to the list (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 18.9–11, 22).
- **A hopeful reading.** Primasius hears in those who throw dust on their heads people reproaching the leaders who led them astray. To stand far off and cry out is not to fall with the lost. It can be the turning of a soul now firm in faith, a "fruitful penance," since no one is found good who was not first evil, and we were all once children of wrath (Ephesians 2:1–3) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.17–19).
- **The sea of this life, and the old reading of Rome.** Andrew reads the sea as the present life, heaving with waves, in which those who trade swim like fish. He allows that a city beside a physical sea may be meant. He adds that the merchants of the universal Babylon, "that is, Confusion," will weep at the end of the visible world, reproached by their conscience. Against that reading he reports the ancient teachers of the Church, who refer the passage to the Babylon of the Romans and to Daniel's fourth beast with its ten horns (Daniel 7:7–8, 19–20). He reconciles them: whoever takes this kingdom as one body through all its times, which has shed the blood of apostles, prophets and martyrs, will not be wrong. There is one kingdom, though it is divided into many times and places (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.9–10, 15–19, 22).

**"Rejoice over her, heaven" (18:20).** Apringius: the blood of the martyrs has been vindicated. Oecumenius: while the merchants weep, the angels and the souls of the righteous rejoice. Primasius: those who have converted are made glad. Caesarius asks whether Babylon is the only city that ever persecuted the saints, so that all are avenged when she falls; no, Babylon is throughout the world in evil people (Homily 17). Andrew: the apostles and prophets rejoice not in anyone's hardship. They desire the end of sin; and perhaps, he says, those who were slaves to sin will find "a milder punishment" in the age to come for having suffered something here (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.20). Bede hears the Lord's own word about the end, "stand erect and raise your heads" (Luke 21:28), and the judgment for which the souls under the altar cried out (Revelation 6:10).

**The millstone (18:21–24).**

- **The turning of time.** Caesarius: the revolving of the ages grinds down those who love the world and sends them in circles. This is the city of all the proud, which Cain founded on his brother's blood and named for his son (Genesis 4:17). The wicked in whom Babylon lives succeed one another and persecute the Church to the end, and so in her is found all the righteous blood from Abel onward (Matthew 23:35): "Woe to him who builds a city with blood" (Habakkuk 2:12) (Homily 17).
- **Sudden and complete.** Andrew: as a millstone sinks at once, no trace of her will be found. He allows that the Babylon of the Persians may be in view, which has received the blood of many saints up to his own day (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 18.21–24).
- **Two stones.** Bede: the city of this age is an unstable millstone, sunk by the weight of its sins. The Church is also a stone, the firm rock that withstands the storm (Matthew 7:24–25). Those who cause others to fall receive a like punishment (Matthew 18:6–7). The millstone can also stand for the grinding of the saints' sufferings, and he recalls St. Ignatius of Antioch on his way to the beasts, calling himself "the wheat of God" (*To the Romans* 4.1) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 18.21–22).

## The Church [[auth:church]]

The harlot is idolatry of wealth and power. Man commits idolatry "whenever he honors and reveres a creature in place of God," including "power, pleasure… the state, money" (CCC 2113); an economy that makes profit "the exclusive norm and ultimate end" is morally unacceptable (CCC 2424). Pope Leo XIV sets the same choice before our age: to "construct a new Tower of Babel" or to "build the city in which God and humanity dwell together," and he calls Babel "a project conceived without reference to God" (*Magnifica Humanitas* 7).

## Where interpreters differ [[auth:disputed]]

- **Rome:** the first meaning, in the NABRE notes and most modern commentary.
- **The earthly city in every age:** St. Augustine, St. Andrew of Caesarea, Primasius and St. Bede; every civilization that organizes itself without God.
- **Apostate Jerusalem:** some modern scholars, reading the harlot as the city that killed the prophets.
- **A world economic and commercial system:** many modern readers, reading chapter 18's merchants and cargo.

The Church has not decided which the text intends at the end.

<div class="response" markdown>

## The response asked of us

"Depart from her, my people, so as not to take part in her sins and receive a share in her plagues" (Revelation 18:4). Do not drink from her cup, and do not mourn her as the merchants do. The beast and the kings that served her destroy her in the end; the bride does not need her. "They will fight with the Lamb, but the Lamb will conquer them" (17:14).
