## A hymn that interprets the vision

Revelation often stops a vision to let heaven sing about it, and the song tells the reader what the vision meant. John has just seen a war (12:7–9). The hymn says what kind of war it was. It was not fought for territory. It was a case at law, and the prosecutor has been thrown out of court.

The Church sings this hymn herself. It is the canticle of Evening Prayer every Thursday in the Liturgy of the Hours. [[auth:church]]

## The words

- **Now.** The hymn begins with the time: salvation, power, the kingdom and the authority of God's Anointed have come *now*. This is why many read the war of 12:7–9 as the heavenly side of the Cross and Ascension; see [War in heaven](/war-in-heaven).
- **The accuser.** The Greek is *ho katēgōr*, a prosecutor. It translates the Hebrew *satan*. The notes of the New American Bible say: "The accuser: the meaning of the Hebrew word 'Satan'… he continues to accuse Christ's disciples" ([note on 12:10](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/12)). [[auth:theologian]]
- **Our brothers.** The voice belongs to those in heaven who call the faithful on earth their brothers: the angels, or the martyrs already there (6:9–11).
- **Day and night.** Without pause. The same phrase describes the worship of heaven (4:8; 7:15). The accuser had his own unceasing liturgy.
- **They conquered.** The verb is *enikēsan*, from *nikaō*, the word of the seven letters: "to the victor…" (2:7, 11, 17, 26; 3:5, 12, 21). Here John shows what that victory looks like.

## The accuser in the Old Testament

Two scenes stand behind the word.

**Job.** The satan comes among the sons of God and says of a just man: "Is it for nothing that Job is God-fearing? Have you not surrounded him and his family and all that he has with your protection?… But now put forth your hand and touch all that he has, and surely he will curse you to your face" (Job 1:9–11). The accusation is that no one loves God for his own sake.

**Joshua the high priest.** Zechariah sees "Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, while the adversary stood at his right side to accuse him." The priest is in filthy garments; the charge has something to it. And the angel answers, "May the LORD rebuke you, O adversary… Is this not a brand plucked from the fire?" Then: "Look, I have taken your guilt from you, and I am clothing you in stately robes" (Zechariah 3:1–4).

The second scene shows how the accuser is silenced. Not by proving the accused innocent, but by God taking the guilt away. [[auth:scripture]]

The Lord himself spoke of it on the night before he died: "Simon, Simon, behold Satan has demanded to sift all of you like wheat, but I have prayed that your own faith may not fail" (Luke 22:31–32). The accuser demands; the Advocate prays.

## How he is conquered

The hymn gives three things, in order.

**1. By the blood of the Lamb.** This comes first, and the other two depend on it. Christ "has freed us from our sins by his blood" (Revelation 1:5); the saints "have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb" (7:14). St. Paul describes the same victory in the language of a courtroom: Christ was "obliterating the bond against us, with its legal claims, which was opposed to us… nailing it to the cross; despoiling the principalities and the powers" (Colossians 2:14–15). And he draws the conclusion the hymn sings: "Who will bring a charge against God's chosen ones? It is God who acquits us. Who will condemn? It is Christ Jesus who died, rather, was raised, who also is at the right hand of God, who indeed intercedes for us" (Romans 8:33–34).

The accuser has lost his place because another now stands in it. "We have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous one" (1 John 2:1), who "lives forever to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25). The Catechism: "Victory over the 'prince of this world' was won once for all at the Hour when Jesus freely gave himself up to death to give us his life" (CCC 2853). [[auth:church]]

**2. By the word of their testimony.** The Greek word is *martyria*, from which "martyr" comes. The blood of the Lamb is not a charm; it is confessed. The faithful conquer by saying openly whose they are, which is the one thing the accuser said they would not do when it cost them.

**3. Love for life did not deter them from death.** This answers the charge made against Job. Here are people who do love God for nothing, and prove it. "Whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will preserve it for eternal life" (John 12:25). The Catechism calls martyrdom "the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith: it means bearing witness even unto death" (CCC 2473). [[auth:church]]

So the martyrs do not add a second victory to Christ's. They conquer "by the blood of the Lamb": his victory is theirs because they hold to it to the end.

## Rejoice, and woe

The hymn ends by dividing its hearers. Heaven is told to rejoice. Earth and sea are told, "woe to you… for the Devil has come down to you in great fury, for he knows he has but a short time."

Both halves are true at once, and the rest of chapters 12 and 13 unfolds the second. From the earth and the sea the two beasts will rise (13:1, 11). But the reason for the dragon's fury is given, and it is good news: his time is short, and he knows it. The Catechism says the same in plain terms: Christ's reign "is still under attack by the evil powers, even though they have been defeated definitively by Christ's Passover" (CCC 671). [[auth:church]]

## The Woman and the hymn

The hymn does not mention the Woman, but it explains her. Read as Mary, she is the first in whom the accuser found nothing: the Catechism says that "Mary benefited first of all and uniquely from Christ's victory over sin: she was preserved from all stain of original sin and by a special grace of God committed no sin of any kind during her whole earthly life" (CCC 411). Read as the Church, she is the mother of those who conquer, "the rest of her offspring… who bear witness to Jesus" (12:17); the word for their witness is the same *martyria*. [[auth:church]]

## What the Fathers said

- **"The accuser of our brothers."** Oecumenius marvels at the humility of the angels, who call human beings their brothers, and remembers that their Lord did so first: "I will proclaim your name to my brothers" (Psalm 22:23; Hebrews 2:12). The accuser has lost his rank, and there is no longer any place for him to accuse. Those he accused have had their answer: they overcame the one who seemed unconquerable, by the blood of Christ and by the word of their witness, which they held dearer than their lives (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.10–12). St. Andrew of Caesarea: the saints were slandered by him as Job was, and by what they suffered for Christ they conquered him.
- **"The authority of his Anointed."** Primasius reads the hymn of the Church. In her, salvation is accomplished by Christ's victory, and from it she has received the authority to bind and to loose, given to all in the one Peter (Matthew 16:19). When the Lord says that all authority has been given to him (Matthew 28:18), he speaks of the authority he began to hold in the Church as Head among his members. The angels rejoice for those who will be their fellow citizens and are now on the way. In the redeemed they see God's goodness, and in the lost his justice. The love by which the martyrs did not cling to life was not their own; it is poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit (Romans 5:5) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.10–11). [[auth:father]]
- **Why was he allowed to come down?** Oecumenius asks it outright, and answers as he does elsewhere in the book. For those who are sober and hope in God, it turned to their good. Trials train them, as a wrestling master trains an athlete, until they are proved and hardened like iron. The harm falls on the slothful, who would have turned to evil of themselves. "Earth" and "sea" are not places. They are people earthly in their thinking and unsteady in mind, and on these the enemy makes war.
- **"Rejoice, you heavens… woe to you, earth and sea."** St. Caesarius of Arles: the saints are the heaven from which the devil is cast out, and "the saints cannot become heaven" unless he has been expelled. He comes down to those who are earth by their attachments (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 9). Primasius recalls the scroll Ezekiel saw, written with lamentation, song and woe (Ezekiel 2:9–10): the weeping of the penitent, the joy of the saints, and the sentence on the wicked. Andrew says the angels, in imitation of God, rejoice at the devil's fall and grieve for those who cling to earthly things. Many on earth do defeat him, and will.
- **"His time is short."** Oecumenius: the whole time from Satan's fall to his final sentence is short, beside the unending ages that follow. Primasius hears the demons' own confession: "Have you come here to torment us before the appointed time?" (Matthew 8:29).

## Where readers differ

- **Who is speaking.** The "loud voice" is not identified. Some hear the martyrs under the altar (6:9), some the angels, some the twenty-four elders. [[auth:disputed]]
- **Whether Satan still accuses.** The hymn says he is cast out; the New American Bible's note says "he continues to accuse Christ's disciples." Both can be held: he has lost his standing before God, and he still accuses on earth, in consciences and through human mouths. What he can no longer do is make the charge stand.
- **Which martyrs.** Those of John's own day under Rome, those of every age, or those of the last persecution. The verb is in the past tense, as of a thing already sure.

## What it asks of us

Three things, in the hymn's own order. To rest our case on the blood of the Lamb and on nothing of our own: "Now there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). To say so aloud when it is asked of us. And to hold life loosely enough that fear of losing it cannot be used against us.

There is also a warning in the title. Accusation is the dragon's own work. A Christian who makes a habit of accusing his brothers has taken up the occupation of the one who was thrown down.
