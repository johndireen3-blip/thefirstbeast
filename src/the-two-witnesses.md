## The words

- **My two witnesses.** God calls them his. The Greek is *martyres*, from which "martyrs" comes.
- **Two.** The Law required it: "a charge shall stand only on the testimony of two or three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15). The Lord sent his disciples out two by two. The notes of the New American Bible give this as the reason for the number ([note on 11:3](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/11)).
- **Sackcloth.** The dress of mourning and repentance. Their message is a call to conversion.
- **When they have finished.** They are not killed before their work is done. The beast has no power over them until then.
- **The beast that comes up from the abyss.** This is the first mention of the beast in Revelation. He is introduced as the killer of God's witnesses two chapters before he is described.

[[auth:scripture]]

## The Old Testament behind it

**Zechariah's lampstand.** "These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth" (11:4). The prophet saw a lampstand with an olive tree on either side, and was told: "These are the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord of the whole earth" (Zechariah 4:14). In his day they were most likely Joshua the high priest and Zerubbabel the governor: priesthood and kingship, feeding the light of God's house.

**Elijah.** "They have the power to close up the sky so that no rain can fall" (11:6). Elijah shut the sky for three and a half years (1 Kings 17:1; Luke 4:25), the very length of the witnesses' prophesying, and called down fire: "divine fire came down from heaven and consumed him and his fifty men" (2 Kings 1:12). And "Elijah went up to heaven in a whirlwind" (2 Kings 2:11), as the witnesses go up in a cloud.

**Moses.** "They also have power to turn water into blood and to afflict the earth with any plague" (11:6; Exodus 7:17–20).

**Ezekiel's valley.** "A breath of life from God entered them. When they stood on their feet…" (11:11). The words are from the vision of the dry bones: "the breath entered them; they came to life and stood on their feet" (Ezekiel 37:10). There the dead who rise are the whole house of Israel. [[auth:scripture]]

So the two witnesses are drawn with the features of Moses and Elijah, the Law and the Prophets, the two who stood with Christ at the Transfiguration; and they are named with the image of a priest and a king.

## Who are they? The tradition's readings

The Church has not defined who the two witnesses are. The tradition offers two kinds of answer. [[auth:disputed]]

### Two prophets who will come

**Elijah is nearly always one.** Scripture promises his coming by name: "Now I am sending to you Elijah the prophet, before the day of the LORD comes" (Malachi 3:23). He is "destined… to put an end to wrath before the day of the LORD… and to re-establish the tribes of Israel" (Sirach 48:10). The Lord confirmed it, "Elijah will indeed come and restore all things," while teaching that in John the Baptist "Elijah has already come" (Matthew 17:11–12). St. Augustine calls Elijah's coming before the judgment "a familiar theme in the conversation and heart of the faithful," through whose teaching, he expects, the Jewish people will come to believe in Christ ([*City of God* XX.29](https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf102.iv.XX.29.html)). [[auth:father]]

**The second is less settled.**

| Reading | Who holds it |
| --- | --- |
| Enoch, who like Elijah did not die (Genesis 5:24) | St. Hippolytus, who speaks of "the two prophets Enoch and Elias" (*On Christ and Antichrist* 43, 47); a later work under his name, which adds John the Theologian; St. John Damascene; St. Robert Bellarmine; the Haydock commentary calls it "a very common interpretation" |
| Jeremiah | St. Victorinus, who gives it as the view of "all our ancients," and notes of other candidates: "Many think that there is Elisha, or Moses, with Elijah; but both of these died" |
| Moses | St. Hilary, as the Haydock commentary reports |

[[auth:father]]

The fuller accounts are on A Great Sign: [Hippolytus](https://agreatsign.si/hippolytus), [Victorinus](https://agreatsign.si/victorinus), [John Damascene](https://agreatsign.si/john-damascene), [Bellarmine](https://agreatsign.si/bellarmine).

### The witnessing Church

Others read the two as the Church herself, bearing witness through her whole history.

- **St. Cyprian** took them as two kinds of martyrs, and others, the Haydock commentary records, as the testimonies of the Old and the New Testament ([on Apocalypse 11](https://ecatholic2000.com/haydock/ntcomment277.shtml)). [[auth:father]]
- **St. Bede** reads them chiefly as the Church, made one out of two peoples, and the two olive trees as the two Testaments that feed her lamp; he also records the reading of Enoch and Elijah ([*Explanation of the Apocalypse*](https://pot-pourri.fltr.ucl.ac.be/files/aclassftp/textes/BEDA/explanatio_apoc_02_uk.txt)). [[auth:father]]
- **The notes of the New American Bible:** the witnesses "cannot readily be identified. Do they represent Moses and Elijah, or the Law and the Prophets, or Peter and Paul? Most probably they refer to the universal church, especially the Christian martyrs, fulfilling the office of witness" ([note on 11:3](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/11)). [[auth:theologian]]

### How the two kinds of reading relate

They are not rivals in the way they may seem. Those who expect Elijah in person do not deny that the Church bears witness in every age; those who read the Church do not deny that Elijah is promised. Bede holds both. A reader may see in the two witnesses the Church's prophetic witness through all her history, and expect that witness to be gathered, at the last, into persons whom God will send.

## The pattern of their witness

Whoever they are, John tells their story in the shape of another.

| The witnesses | Christ |
| --- | --- |
| They prophesy for 1,260 days, about three and a half years | His public ministry is traditionally counted as about three years |
| Killed in "the great city… where indeed their Lord was crucified" (11:8) | Crucified outside Jerusalem |
| The world rejoices over their bodies (11:10) | "You will weep and mourn, while the world rejoices" (John 16:20) |
| After three and a half days the breath of life enters them (11:11) | Raised on the third day |
| "They went up to heaven in a cloud" (11:12) | "A cloud took him from their sight" (Acts 1:9) |

The Catechism says this of the whole Church: she "will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection" (CCC 677). The two witnesses are that sentence told as a story. [[auth:church]]

## The great city

Their bodies lie in "the great city, which has the symbolic names 'Sodom' and 'Egypt,' where indeed their Lord was crucified" (11:8). John tells us himself that the names are symbolic. The notes of the New American Bible take the city as Rome, "called Babylon in Rev 16–18," "not the geographical but the symbolic Jerusalem that rejects God and his witnesses" ([note on 11:8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/11)). Others take it as Jerusalem itself; others as the whole world organized against God. It is every city that has killed the prophets. [[auth:disputed]]

## A different ending

One detail sets this passage apart from the trumpets before it. After the plagues of chapters 8 and 9, the survivors "did not repent" (9:20–21). After the witnesses are raised, "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13). To "give glory to God" is what the first angel of chapter 14 will ask of every nation (14:7).

What punishment did not do, the death and vindication of the witnesses does. Many readers have taken this as the heart of the passage: the world is converted not by plagues but by the faithful witness of those who are willing to die. [[auth:theologian]]

## What the tradition leaves open

This study names no one as a witness. It does say plainly what the Church has and has not settled, so that a reader knows where there is room to watch and to weigh; see [How to test a reading of Revelation](/how-to-test-a-reading). [[auth:disputed]]

- **Not settled:** whether the witnesses are two individuals, or the Church, or both. Bede and the modern notes show that all three positions are held within the Church.
- **Not settled:** who the second witness is, if they are individuals. The Fathers themselves name Enoch, Jeremiah and Moses.
- **Not settled:** whether Elijah's promised coming was wholly fulfilled in John the Baptist or is still to be completed. The Lord's words can be read either way, and Augustine expected a coming still to be.
- **Not settled:** whether the "two" must be persons of the past, or could be persons raised up by God in the last days "in the spirit and power of Elijah," as the angel said of John (Luke 1:17).
- **Settled by the text:** whoever they are, they are God's witnesses and not self-appointed; they preach repentance; they are given signs; they are killed by the beast and raised by God. Any reading has to meet all of these.
- **Settled by the Church:** the time is not ours to know, so no dates can be computed from their 1,260 days (CCC 673), and the Church's hope does not rest on identifying them.

A reader may hold an opinion here. It should be held as an opinion, tested against everything the text says, and never in a way that expects or wishes anyone's death.

## What the Fathers said further

The readings of the tradition are set out above. The commentators in the *Ancient Christian Commentary* volume add detail to both sides, and Tyconius argues openly against reading two individuals. [[auth:father]]

**Two prophets at the end.**

- **A third with them.** A later work that circulated under the name of Hippolytus, *On the End of the World*, expects Enoch and Elijah and also John the Theologian. It takes the half of Daniel's last week (Daniel 9:27) for the 1,260 days of their preaching. It sees in their sending the Lord's kindness: even in the last times he does not leave us without prophets (*On the End of the World* 21).
- **Why Jeremiah.** St. Victorinus gives his reason. God told Jeremiah that he had appointed him a prophet to the nations (Jeremiah 1:5), and Jeremiah never was one; so the word is still to be kept (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.3).
- **Why Enoch.** Oecumenius: Malachi foretells Elijah plainly (Malachi 3:23–24). Of another forerunner Scripture says only that Enoch pleased God and was taken (Genesis 5:24; Hebrews 11:5), and an old tradition in the Church holds that he will come with Elijah. They wear sackcloth because they mourn the disobedience of that time. Whether the number of days is a figure or exact, he leaves open (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.3–6).
- **Signs against signs.** Oecumenius and St. Andrew of Caesarea give the reason for their power. The antichrist will come with every false sign and wonder (2 Thessalonians 2:9). God will arm his witnesses with true ones, bringing, in Andrew's words, "a healing that is commensurate with the wound" (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.5–6).

**The Church and her two Testaments.**

- **Tyconius:** the two witnesses are the two Testaments by which the Church is governed. The Lord does not say he will make witnesses, as if they did not yet exist. He gives power to witnesses who have been with him from the beginning. The 1,260 days are the time of the last persecution; as before the flood there was one to announce God's wrath, so then there will be those who announce the kingdom and show the way of repentance. The olive trees are the Testaments, pouring the oil of knowledge into the one lampstand of the Church, as Zechariah saw (Zechariah 4:2–3, 14) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.3–4).
- **Primasius** stresses the tense: "these are," not "these will be." John is speaking of the present. He offers a second reading: two kinds of witness, that of a life known to God alone and that of public confession under persecution. St. Cyprian distinguished them, and the sons of Zebedee show them: James was killed by Herod (Acts 12:2), and John bore his witness otherwise, though the Lord told both that they would drink his cup (Matthew 20:23). The lampstands are two because of the two Testaments, or because the Church is gathered from two peoples and joined in the one cornerstone (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.3–4).
- **The fire and the closed sky.** Tyconius and St. Caesarius of Arles: whoever harms the Church is consumed through the prayers of her mouth, either now for his correction or hereafter for his condemnation. Tyconius allows a happier sense, that a person is "consumed" when he is turned from error and saved. The power over rain is the Church's power of binding and loosing (Matthew 16:19); heaven is shut when the rain of blessing is withheld, as the Lord said of his vineyard (Isaiah 5:6). Primasius adds that the same fragrance of Christ is life to some and death to others (2 Corinthians 2:15–16).

**The beast from the abyss.**

- **Victorinus** takes the beast for the antichrist and finds him in the prophets, in Ezekiel's great tree nourished by the deep (Ezekiel 31:3–4). St. Paul shows he was to be among the Caesars, already at work as a mystery of lawlessness, and to come only when God allows (2 Thessalonians 2:7–11) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.4).
- **Oecumenius:** the witnesses will testify that the one then present is not the Christ. He is called a beast for his savagery. The abyss is human life, bitter with sin, for he will be a man of our own nature and no other, whose coming is by the working of Satan. The city is called Sodom and Egypt in a spiritual sense, for its license and because it enslaves the servants of Christ (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.7–10).
- **Andrew:** by God's leave the antichrist will kill the witnesses and leave them unburied in Jerusalem, where he will set up his kingdom in imitation of David, to pass for the one who rebuilds David's fallen hut (Amos 9:11) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.7–8).
- **Tyconius and St. Bede** read the Church. "When they have finished their testimony" shows that she bears witness until the last persecution. The beast "conquers" in those who give way and "kills" those who suffer for Christ's name. Tyconius notes that his text speaks of one "body" of the two, for the Church is one. Bede adds that at that time the Church is believed to be left without the gift of miracles, while the adversary works false signs openly. Sodom and Egypt he takes to mean "silent" and "dark": a city with neither the light of faith nor the voice of confession (Romans 10:10) (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 11.7–8).

**Those who rejoice.** Tyconius: by refusing them burial, their enemies try to prevent any memorial of them among the living. The very sight of the just is a burden to the unjust (Wisdom 2:15; Psalm 112:10). Oecumenius: the prophets tormented them by reproof, not by force. Primasius hears the old cry against God's people, "let the name of Israel be remembered no more" (Psalm 83:5). Andrew turns it into a prayer: those who rejoiced did not know that the Lord reproves those he loves, and we should ask to be corrected now so as not to be condemned with the world (1 Corinthians 11:32).

**They stood on their feet.**

- **Tyconius** reads the general resurrection, when the just are caught up in the clouds to meet Christ (1 Thessalonians 4:17). This cannot happen to anyone before the Lord's coming, he says, and so he rejects the opinion that these are two men who ascend beforehand. How could the whole earth rejoice at two deaths in one city, or exchange gifts in three and a half days? The fear that falls on those who see is the fear of the living at the resurrection of the dead (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.11–12).
- **Andrew** keeps the two prophets: dead for as many days as the years of their prophecy, they will rise and go up to heaven on a cloud, as the Lord did (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 11.11–12).
- **The earthquake.** Tyconius: there are two buildings in the Church, one on the rock and one on sand (Matthew 7:24–27). In persecution the second falls, with all who did not keep the faith with a pure heart. Those on the rock are afraid, give glory to God, and keep the commandments more carefully. Andrew thinks these things may happen outwardly, and reads the earthquake as the change of what is unstable into something firm. The seven thousand are those who clung to the seven days of this present life and did not wait for the eighth day, the day of resurrection.

*Source: unless another source is linked, the Fathers in this section are cited from* Revelation, *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 11:3–13.*

## Where readers differ

- **Literal or symbolic,** as above.
- **Past or future.** The first-century reading sees the martyrs under Rome, and some scholars have proposed Peter and Paul, who died there. The Fathers who read two prophets place them at the end. The reading of the Church as witness covers all the time between.
- **The signs.** Whether the fire and the plagues are to be seen literally, or are the power of the prophetic word, as when the Lord tells Jeremiah that his words in the prophet's mouth will be a fire (Jeremiah 5:14).

## What it asks of us

Before it asks us to identify anyone, it asks us to be witnesses. Every Christian is one by Baptism and Confirmation, and the description holds: sent by God and not by oneself; in sackcloth, which is to say with repentance and without triumph; protected until the work is done; and not promised a better end in this world than the Lord's.

It also tells us what to expect of the world's reaction, and not to be shaken by it. The witnesses' death is a festival: people "gloat over them and be glad and exchange gifts" (11:10). Three and a half days later the same people are afraid. John wants his readers to know the order of those two scenes in advance.
