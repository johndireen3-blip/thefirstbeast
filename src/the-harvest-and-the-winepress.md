## Where this vision stands

It closes the section that began at 12:1. The notes of the New American Bible treat chapters 12 to 14 as one unit: the dragon, his two beasts, "followed by a vision of the Lamb and his faithful ones, and the proclamation of imminent judgment upon the world" ([note on 12:1–14:20](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/12)).

The first sign in heaven was a Woman, and her child was "caught up to God and his throne" (12:5). The last sight is that child returning as judge. Between the two lies everything the dragon was allowed to do.

## The words

- **A white cloud… one like a son of man.** These are Daniel's words: "I saw coming with the clouds of heaven one like a son of man… He received dominion, splendor, and kingship; all nations, peoples and tongues will serve him" (Daniel 7:13–14). Revelation opened with the same promise: "Behold, he is coming amid the clouds" (1:7). And the Lord applied it to himself before the high priest.
- **A gold crown.** A *stephanos*, the victor's wreath, as on the Woman's head (12:1). The dragon and the beast wear diadems (12:3; 13:1). The crown of victory belongs to the Son and to his mother.
- **A sharp sickle.** The word is used seven times in these seven verses.
- **Fully ripe.** The harvest is not hurried. It comes when the time is complete.

## The Old Testament behind it

**Joel.** Both images come from one verse: "Wield the sickle, for the harvest is ripe; come and tread, for the wine press is full; the vats overflow, for their crimes are numerous" (Joel 4:13). John has unfolded the prophet's single sentence into two scenes.

**Isaiah.** "Who is this that comes from Edom, in crimsoned garments?… Why is your apparel red, and your garments like one who treads the wine press? 'The wine press I have trodden alone, and from the peoples no one was with me'" (Isaiah 63:1–3). Revelation returns to this passage at the end, when the rider called Faithful and True comes with his cloak dipped in blood, and "he himself will tread out in the wine press the wine of the fury and wrath of God the almighty" (19:13, 15). The New American Bible's note connects them: the treading of the grapes symbolizes a doom "that will come in Rev 19:11–21."

**The Lord's own parables.** "The harvest is the end of the age, and the harvesters are angels" (Matthew 13:39). "When the grain is ripe, he wields the sickle at once, for the harvest has come" (Mark 4:29). [[auth:scripture]]

## Two harvests

The passage has two parts, and they are not the same.

| | The grain (14:14–16) | The grapes (14:17–20) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who reaps | One like a son of man, seated on a cloud | An angel from the temple |
| What follows | "The earth was harvested." Nothing more is said | The vintage is thrown into "the great wine press of God's fury" |
| Wrath mentioned | No | Yes |

**The common reading: the just and the unjust.** The New American Bible: "The reaping of the harvest symbolizes the gathering of the elect in the final judgment, while the reaping and treading of the grapes symbolizes the doom of the ungodly" ([note on 14:14–20](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/14)). This fits what has just come before: the 144,000 were called "firstfruits" (14:4), and here the rest of the harvest is brought in, by the Son of Man himself. It fits the Lord's parable of the wheat and the weeds (Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43). [[auth:theologian]]

**Another reading: one judgment told twice.** Joel's verse speaks of a single judgment under two images, and some commentators read John the same way: both scenes show the judgment of the world, the second with more detail. On this reading the passage does not describe the gathering of the saints at all. [[auth:disputed]]

The first reading is the more common among Catholic commentators and is followed here. On either reading, the Church's teaching is the same: "The Last Judgment will come when Christ returns in glory. Only the Father knows the day and the hour… Then through his Son Jesus Christ he will pronounce the final word on all history" (CCC 1040). [[auth:church]]

## An angel tells the Son of Man to reap

"Another angel came out of the temple, crying out in a loud voice to the one sitting on the cloud, 'Use your sickle and reap the harvest, for the time to reap has come.'"

It can seem strange that an angel gives the word to Christ. The angel comes "out of the temple," from the presence of the Father, and brings the Father's decision as to the time. The Lord had said, "Of that day and hour no one knows, neither the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father alone" (Matthew 24:36), and the Catechism repeats it: "only he determines the moment of its coming" (CCC 1040). The scene shows the Son, as man, receiving the hour from the Father. It also shows why no one on earth can compute it. [[auth:theologian]]

## The winepress outside the city

"The wine press was trodden outside the city."

"The city" is not named. In Joel the nations are judged outside Jerusalem, in the Valley of Jehoshaphat (Joel 4:12). And a Christian reader cannot hear "outside the city" without another memory: "Jesus also suffered outside the gate, to consecrate the people by his own blood" (Hebrews 13:12).

Some commentators draw the two together. Outside the city the Lord shed his own blood for sinners; outside the city those who refused it meet the judgment he bore. The one who treads the winepress in chapter 19 wears a cloak "dipped in blood" before the battle begins (19:13), and commentators differ on whether that blood is his enemies' or his own. This is offered as a theologian's reflection; the text itself does not say it. [[auth:theologian]]

## The blood and the number

"Blood poured out of the wine press to the height of a horse's bridle for two hundred miles." The Greek says "sixteen hundred stades."

This is not a measurement to be mapped. It is the language of the prophets for a judgment that is complete. The number is built from four, the number of the earth with its four corners and four winds (7:1): four times four, multiplied by a hundred. It says that the judgment reaches the whole world. The New American Bible's introduction to Revelation warns that "symbolic descriptions are not to be taken as literal descriptions," and that the book's vindictive language is "to be understood symbolically," as a device "to evoke in the reader and hearer a feeling of horror for apostasy and rebellion." [[auth:theologian]]

The horror is meant. The image is the grape harvest, a time of joy in Israel, turned into its opposite. Babylon made the nations drink "the wine of her licentious passion" (14:8); here is the vintage of it.

## Read with the Woman

The Woman's Son is the judge. That is the comfort of the passage for her other children. The one who comes on the cloud is the male child the dragon tried to devour, the Lamb who was slain, and the brother of those who "keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus." They are not judged by a stranger.

A reader who sees Mary in the Woman may add one thing more. The firstfruits belong to God (14:4), and Mary, taken up body and soul, is the first of the harvest after her Son: in her the Church contemplates "what she will be in the homeland at the end of her journey" (CCC 972). This is a reading by likeness, and the text does not make it. [[auth:theologian]]

## Where readers differ

- **One harvest or two,** as above.
- **Is the reaper Christ?** A few commentators have taken "one like a son of man" here as an angel, because another angel gives him the word. Most read him as Christ, since the title, the cloud and the crown are his (Daniel 7:13; Revelation 1:7, 13). [[auth:disputed]]
- **When.** The end of the age, by the Lord's own parable. The first-century reading sees also the judgment that fell on Jerusalem and on Rome as foreshadowings.
- **The Fathers** on this passage will be added from their own texts; see [The Commentators](/the-commentators).

## What it asks of us

"The message of the Last Judgment calls men to conversion while God is still giving them 'the acceptable time'… It inspires a holy fear of God and commits them to the justice of the Kingdom of God. It proclaims the 'blessed hope' of the Lord's return" (CCC 1041).

Both halves of that sentence are in the passage. There is a winepress, and it is not softened. And there is a harvest, gathered by the Lord's own hand, of people who were sown and grew and ripened in the same field as the weeds. The time between now and the sickle is the time given for ripening. The Lord's question about it is not when it will end but what we are becoming in it.
