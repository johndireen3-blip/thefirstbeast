## The same dragon

John names him exactly as he did in chapter 12.

| Revelation 12:9 | Revelation 20:2 |
| --- | --- |
| "The huge dragon, the ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan" | "The dragon, the ancient serpent, which is the Devil or Satan" |
| "who deceived the whole world" | "so that it could no longer lead the nations astray" |
| "was thrown down to earth" | "threw it into the abyss" |

The four names appear together only in these two places. John means the reader to set the passages side by side. See [The great red dragon](/the-great-red-dragon) and [War in heaven](/war-in-heaven).

The dragon's story in this book is a descent in three steps: from heaven to earth (12:9), from earth to the abyss (20:3), and from the abyss to the pool of fire (20:10). He never rises.

## The words

- **An angel.** Not Christ himself, and not even Michael by name. One angel with a key and a chain is enough. Scripture never treats the devil as God's rival. "He is only a creature" (CCC 395).
- **The key to the abyss.** At 9:1 the key was given so that the abyss could be opened. The risen Christ said at the beginning of the book, "I hold the keys to death and the netherworld" (1:18).
- **Seized, tied, threw, locked, sealed.** Five verbs, and the dragon does nothing in any of them.
- **Sealed.** The word used for God's servants in chapter 7. God's seal protects his own, and God's seal confines their enemy.
- **So that it could no longer lead the nations astray.** The text itself says what the binding is for. It is not said that he can do nothing. It is said that he can no longer deceive *the nations*.
- **A thousand years.** The notes of the New American Bible: "Like the other numerical values in this book, the thousand years are not to be taken literally; they symbolize the long period of time between the chaining up of Satan (a symbol for Christ's resurrection-victory over death and the forces of evil) and the end of the world" ([note on 20:1–6](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/20)).
- **A short time.** The same phrase as 12:12: the devil "knows he has but a short time."

## When was he bound?

The Lord himself used this image, and of his own ministry. Accused of casting out demons by the prince of demons, he answered: "How can anyone enter a strong man's house and steal his property, unless he first ties up the strong man? Then he can plunder his house" (Matthew 12:29). The verb "ties up" is the verb of Revelation 20:2.

He spoke the same way elsewhere. "I have observed Satan fall like lightning from the sky" (Luke 10:18). And on the eve of his Passion: "Now is the time of judgment on this world; now the ruler of this world will be driven out" (John 12:31).

The Catechism gathers these: "The coming of God's kingdom means the defeat of Satan's… Jesus' exorcisms free some individuals from the domination of demons. They anticipate Jesus' great victory over 'the ruler of this world.' The kingdom of God will be definitively established through Christ's cross" (CCC 550). And: "Victory over the 'prince of this world' was won once for all at the Hour when Jesus freely gave himself up to death to give us his life" (CCC 2853). [[auth:church]]

This is also what chapter 12 said in its own way. The dragon was thrown down when "salvation and power have come," and the brothers conquered him "by the blood of the Lamb" (12:10–11). Chapter 20 tells the same victory a second time under another image.

## Bound, and still prowling

St. Peter writes to Christians living after the resurrection: "Your opponent the devil is prowling around like a roaring lion looking for [someone] to devour" (1 Peter 5:8). How can he be bound and prowling?

St. Augustine, in the twentieth book of *The City of God*, answered from the text. The devil is bound in one respect: he can no longer hold the nations in deception as he did before Christ. Before the Gospel, the knowledge of the true God was nearly confined to one people. Since Pentecost it has gone to every nation, and he has not been able to stop it. He can still tempt, and he can persecute. He cannot prevent the Church from being gathered. [[auth:father]]

The Catechism says the same in its own words: "The power of Satan is, nonetheless, not infinite… He cannot prevent the building up of God's reign," though his action "may cause grave injuries" and "is permitted by divine providence" (CCC 395). [[auth:church]]

A chained animal is the old comparison. It is dangerous to anyone who goes within its reach, and it cannot go where it pleases.

## The thousand years

This study does not repeat the full treatment of the millennium. For that, see the topic pages on [The Lamb's Victory](https://thelambsvictory.org/). Three points belong here.

**The reign is now.** Those who reign with Christ for the thousand years are the martyrs and all who refused the beast (20:4). The notes of the New American Bible: "During this time God's people share in the glorious reign of God that is present to them by virtue of their baptismal victory over death and sin."

**Some early Fathers read it otherwise.** In the second and third centuries several respected writers, St. Justin, St. Irenaeus and St. Victorinus among them, expected a literal thousand-year reign of Christ and the saints on a renewed earth after his return. This view is called chiliasm or millenarianism. From the time of St. Augustine the reading given above became the common one in the Church. [[auth:fathers]]

**The Church has judged the question.** "The Antichrist's deception already begins to take shape in the world every time the claim is made to realize within history that messianic hope which can only be realized beyond history through the eschatological judgment. The Church has rejected even modified forms of this falsification of the kingdom to come under the name of millenarianism, especially the 'intrinsically perverse' political form of a secular messianism" (CCC 676). [[auth:church]]

The practical meaning is plain. Any promise of a perfected world inside history, whether religious or political, is to be refused. The kingdom comes fully only after the judgment.

## Released for a short time

"After this, it is to be released for a short time." John does not say why. He says "it is to be," literally "it must be": the word used throughout the book for what stands in God's plan.

The Catechism speaks of this as the Church's last trial: "Before Christ's second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers" (CCC 675). She "will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection" (CCC 677). As the Lord's own life ended with an hour when darkness was given power, so will the life of his Body. And as with him, it is short, and it is not the end. See [Gog and Magog, and the dragon's end](/gog-and-magog-and-the-dragons-end).

## Read with the Woman

In chapter 12 the dragon stood before the Woman to devour her child, pursued her into the desert, and poured out a river to sweep her away. Each time he failed. The Catechism reads these verses of Mary: "'He pursued the woman' but had no hold on her: the new Eve, 'full of grace' of the Holy Spirit, is preserved from sin and the corruption of death" (CCC 2853).

The promise in Eden was that the serpent's head would be struck by the woman's offspring (Genesis 3:15). The binding is that blow. What remains for him is what Genesis also foretold: to strike at the heel. [[auth:church]]

## What the Fathers said

Most of the Fathers cited here read the binding as this page does, of Christ's victory in his first coming. Two of the earliest read the thousand years literally, and one reads them in a way of his own. [[auth:father]]

**The angel, the key and the chain.**

- **Christ himself.** Primasius takes the angel for the Lord, the stronger one who came to bind the strong man (Matthew 12:29) and make vessels of mercy out of vessels of wrath. The key is the depth of God's judgments (Psalm 36:7): he alone knows whom he will call out of the mass of sinners (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.1–2). St. Bede likewise: the Lord came down into the flesh to make war on the ruler of the world and free his captives (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 20.1).
- **The Church's ministers.** Apringius of Beja begins with a prayer not to err about the thousand years. The Lord holds the keys of death (Revelation 1:17–18); the key here is the one who bears the office of ministry, and the chain is the unbreakable bond of God's commandment (*Tractate on the Apocalypse* 20.1).
- **An angel carrying out Christ's sentence.** St. Andrew of Caesarea recalls a saying of St. Justin, preserved by St. Irenaeus, that the devil first learned at Christ's coming that he was condemned. That an angel carries it out shows the devil to be weaker than God's servants.

**When he was bound.** At the Passion, says Andrew: the stronger one bound the one who seemed strong and freed us, his spoils. The demons who begged not to be sent into the abyss already knew it. The proof is before our eyes in the end of idol worship and the knowledge of God spread through the world (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.1–3). Apringius says the Lord shut him in by the power of the cross, and set on the abyss "the bolt of the cross" (*Tractate on the Apocalypse* 20.2–4). St. Irenaeus, much earlier, joins this verse to Genesis 3:15, as this page does: the offspring born of Mary treads down the serpent's head, tramples the lion and the dragon (Psalm 91:13), and gives Adam back his life, so that death is swallowed up in victory (1 Corinthians 15:54–55) (*Against Heresies* 3.23.7).

**The thousand years.**

- **St. Augustine** allows two readings. The thousand years may be the last part of the sixth age of the world, the whole named for a part, with the sabbath that has no evening to follow. Or they are all the years of the Christian era, because a thousand is the cube of ten, a number of fullness, as in the covenant commanded "for a thousand generations" (Psalm 105:8) (*City of God* 20.7).
- **Andrew of Caesarea** refuses a literal thousand, with the same psalm. The number means many, the time of the Gospel's preaching, or complete, the time in which we are called to full maturity in Christ. It runs from the Incarnation to the coming of the antichrist, and its real length is known to God alone.
- **St. Caesarius of Arles** counts it from the Passion. During it the devil may not do all he wishes, since God does not let his servants be tried beyond their strength (1 Corinthians 10:13) (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 17).
- **Oecumenius** stands alone. He rejects any millennium of the pagan kind. Since a thousand years in God's sight are as one day (Psalm 90:5; 2 Peter 3:8), and Scripture calls the Lord's coming a day (Isaiah 49:8; Psalm 118:24), he takes the thousand years for the time of the Lord's life on earth, when the demons cried out that he had come to torment them before the time (Matthew 8:29). The "little while" that follows is then the whole time from the Incarnation to the end, short beside what came before and what is to come (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.1–3). This is his reading alone, and it is not the Church's common one, which counts the thousand years from Christ's victory to the end of the age.
- **Apringius** finds the cross in the number itself. In Greek numerals a thousand is written with the letter alpha. Alpha is the beginning, which is Christ, and the name of Christ begins in Greek with the letter shaped like a cross. So the number says by whose power the enemy was bound, and that power has no end (*Tractate on the Apocalypse* 20.2).
- **St. Victorinus** reads it of the future: the devil shut up at the Lord's coming, a first resurrection of the just who reign with Christ over the nations for a thousand years, and then the last trumpet and the general judgment (1 Thessalonians 4:15–17; 1 Corinthians 15:52) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.1–2). This is the reading the Church did not keep; see "The thousand years" above.

**The abyss and the seal.** For Augustine the abyss is the hearts of the godless, where the devil is shut in more deeply the more he is shut out of the hearts of believers. The seal means that no one in this life knows who finally belongs to which side, since the one standing may fall and the one lying may rise. The devil still tempts those reborn in Christ, but he leads none of the elect to final ruin, for the Lord knows his own (2 Timothy 2:19) (*City of God* 20.7). Bede explains the four names: dragon for the harm he does, serpent for his cunning, devil for his fall, Satan for his stubborn opposition (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 20.2).

**Loosed for a little while.** Augustine gives the reason John does not. If the devil were never loosed, the full measure of his malice would never be known, nor the full constancy of the holy city, nor the good use God makes of so great an evil. The time is three years and six months, and those he attacks will be such as he cannot overcome. Augustine doubts whether any will come to the faith in that interval (*City of God* 20.8). Apringius takes "a little while" differently: only as far as Christ's command allows, and in a moment he is brought to nothing, since the resurrection and the thrones of judgment are already in view (Daniel 12:1–2). Andrew ends with a prayer that the merciful God will save us from the punishment prepared for the devil and his angels (Matthew 25:41).

**Those who reign with Christ (20:4).**

- **The Church now.** Augustine: the Church in this age is already Christ's kingdom, though mixed, with weeds among the wheat, and only those reign with him who are themselves his kingdom. The thrones are those of the bishops who govern her, and the judgment is the power of binding and loosing (Matthew 16:19; 18:18). The souls of the faithful departed reign with him too, which is why the Church remembers them at the altar (Romans 14:9). The beast is the godless city, and its image is the pretence of those who profess the faith and live as unbelievers (*City of God* 20.9).
- **Not the martyrs alone.** St. Cyprian notes that John joins to the beheaded all who did not worship the image. Whoever stands firm in faith and has left all for Christ is honored with the martyrs (Luke 18:29–30) (*Exhortation to Martyrdom* 12).
- **The apostles and the martyrs.** Oecumenius and Andrew see the twelve thrones promised to the apostles (Matthew 19:28). Andrew adds that the martyrs judge the demons even now, as their healings show. Apringius says the saints reign by the sign of the cross.

**The first resurrection and the second death (20:5–6).**

- **The soul's rising now.** Augustine reads it with John 5:25: the dead who hear the voice of the Son of God and live. Whoever does not pass from death to life in this age will rise in the body only to judgment (*City of God* 20.9). Caesarius says we rise by it in baptism (Colossians 3:1; Ephesians 2:1). Oecumenius calls it the resurrection of faith. Andrew sets out two lives, two deaths and two resurrections: the first resurrection is the rising from dead works, the second the change of our bodies to incorruption (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.5–6).
- **"The rest of the dead."** Oecumenius, keeping to his own reckoning, takes them for those who remained in unbelief while the Lord was on earth (Matthew 8:22). They came to life afterward, at the coming of the Holy Spirit. During the Lord's ministry only about a hundred and twenty are counted as believers (Acts 1:15); at Pentecost most of the Jewish people who heard believed. He compares the Lord's teaching to seed lying hidden in the ground until rain and sun bring it up. The Spirit, spoken of in the Old Testament but not yet seen at work, was then shown to be one in substance and power with the Father and the Son (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 20.4–8).
- **The second death.** It is the death of the soul by sin and its punishment (Oecumenius, with Matthew 10:28). Augustine describes it as a state where pain afflicts and never destroys (*Enchiridion* 92). Apringius notes that the rest "did not come to life," since existence without joy is not called life.
- **Baptized in the Spirit, or in fire.** Origen distinguishes the one who keeps the baptism of the Holy Spirit, and so shares the first resurrection, from the one who sins afterward and must be tried by fire (Luke 3:16) (*Homilies on Jeremiah* 2.3).
- **Priests of God and of Christ.** Augustine: this is said of all Christians and not of bishops and presbyters only. All are called priests as members of the one Priest (1 Peter 2:9), as all are called anointed. The verse also shows that Christ is God (*City of God* 20.10).

*Source: the Fathers are cited from* Revelation, *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 20:1–6.*

## Where readers differ

- **The sequence.** Read straight through, chapter 20 seems to follow chapter 19 in time. The common Catholic reading takes it as a new vision that goes back to the beginning, as chapter 12 did, and tells the whole age of the Church from another side. Revelation often retells in this way. [[auth:disputed]]
- **A future binding.** Some Christians, mostly outside the Catholic Church, expect the binding and the thousand years after Christ's return. The Church's rejection of millenarianism is given above. Among the Fathers, Victorinus placed the binding at the Lord's return, and Oecumenius placed it in the years of his earthly life.
- **How long "a short time" is.** The text does not say, and the Church sets no dates (CCC 673).

## What it asks of us

Not to fear him more than he deserves. One angel binds him. He acts only by permission. The Catechism quotes St. Ambrose: "One who entrusts himself to God does not dread the devil" (CCC 2852).

And not to go within reach of the chain. He is bound for the nations, and still a danger to the one who draws near. "Resist him, steadfast in faith" (1 Peter 5:9).
