## Hearing and seeing

The key to the passage is two verbs. "I *heard* the number" (7:4). "After this I had a *vision*" (7:9). John hears one thing and sees another.

He has done this before. In chapter 5 an elder tells him, "The lion of the tribe of Judah… has triumphed." John hears "lion." He looks, and sees "a Lamb that seemed to have been slain" (5:5–6). The Lion and the Lamb are not two figures. What he sees interprets what he heard.

So here. He hears a number, exact and ordered, drawn from the tribes of Israel. He looks, and sees a crowd beyond counting from every nation. Many commentators conclude that the second is the first seen truly. [[auth:theologian]]

## The number

- **Twelve times twelve times a thousand.** The notes of the New American Bible: "the square of twelve (the number of Israel's tribes) multiplied by a thousand, symbolic of the new Israel… that embraces people from every nation, race, people, and tongue" ([note on 7:4–9](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/7)). Twelve is the number of God's people: twelve tribes, twelve apostles. The new Jerusalem has twelve gates named for the tribes and twelve foundations named for the apostles, and its wall measures 144 cubits (21:12–17). A thousand is the number of immensity. The sum is the people of God, complete.
- **Counted as an army.** In the Old Testament a count of men by tribes is a census for war (Numbers 1). The sealed are numbered like the camp of Israel in the desert. This is the Church on earth in her time of struggle.
- **Judah first.** The list does not begin with Reuben, the firstborn. "Judah is placed first because of Christ," the note says, citing "the Lion of the tribe of Judah."
- **Not a limit.** No one is turned away because the number is full. The number is a symbol of fullness, and the very next verse shows a multitude "which no one could count."

The Catechism names them among those who take part in the worship of heaven: "the new People of God (the one hundred and forty-four thousand)" (CCC 1138). And the note on chapter 14 says plainly: "the 144,000 whose foreheads are sealed represent all Christian people." [[auth:church]]

## The multitude

- **From every nation.** "Every nation, race, people, and tongue": the promise to Abraham kept, that his descendants would be past counting (Genesis 15:5).
- **Standing before the throne.** The question at the end of chapter 6 was, "who can withstand it?" Literally, "who can stand?" Here is the answer: they are standing.
- **White robes and palm branches.** "Symbols of joy and victory," says the note. Palms were carried at the feast of Tabernacles, when Israel remembered her years in the desert under God's care.
- **Washed in blood.** "They have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb." The image is deliberately impossible. Nothing is made white by blood, except by this blood.
- **Out of the great distress.** They are not people who were spared the trial. They came through it.

## The promise

The elder's words are among the most tender in the book:

> "For this reason they stand before God's throne and worship him day and night in his temple. The one who sits on the throne will shelter them. They will not hunger or thirst anymore, nor will the sun or any heat strike them. For the Lamb who is in the center of the throne will shepherd them and lead them to springs of life-giving water, and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (7:15–17).

Every line answers something in the Woman's story. She was fed in the desert (12:6); they will not hunger. The serpent's flood pursued her (12:15); they are led to springs of living water. The child was to "shepherd" the nations with an iron rod (12:5); here "the Lamb… will shepherd them." The iron rod and the shepherd's care belong to the same hand. [[auth:scripture]]

"Shelter them" is literally "spread his tent over them": the word for the tabernacle, where the ark stood.

## One people, seen twice

| | The 144,000 (7:1–8) | The great multitude (7:9–17) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| John | hears a number | sees a crowd |
| They are | counted, tribe by tribe | beyond counting, from every nation |
| Where | on earth, before the winds are loosed | before the throne |
| When | before the trial | after it: "survived the time of great distress" |
| Marked by | the seal on the forehead | white robes and palms |

Read this way, the two visions show the Church in two conditions. First as God sees her on earth: every one of his servants known, counted and sealed, with none missing. Then as she will be in glory: victorious and innumerable. The Church herself speaks of her members in such states: "at the present time some of his disciples are pilgrims on earth… while still others are in glory" (CCC 954). [[auth:church]]

When the 144,000 appear again in chapter 14, the two pictures have come together: the counted ones stand with the Lamb on Mount Zion and sing before the throne. See [The Lamb on Mount Zion](/the-lamb-on-mount-zion).

## What the Fathers said

The Fathers divide on the question this page has asked: are the 144,000 and the multitude one people or two? [[auth:father]]

**Who are the 144,000?**

- **The whole Church.** St. Bede: the finite number stands for "the innumerable multitude of the whole church," born of the patriarchs by descent or by imitating their faith (Galatians 3:29). Twelve is three times four, faith in the Trinity held through the four quarters of the world, and the apostles were twelve to preach it (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 7.4).
- **Believers from Israel in the first century.** Oecumenius thinks of the "many thousands" of Jews who believed (Acts 21:20). He counts among the sealed those who had acted in ignorance, for whom the Lord prayed, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). The equal number from each tribe shows equal zeal and one faith (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 7.1–8).
- **Israel at the end.** St. Jerome reads the tribes with St. Paul: when the full number of the Gentiles has come in, "all Israel will be saved" (Romans 11:25–26). Like Paul, he sees in the delay the opening through which the nations entered (*Homilies on Mark* 82). St. Victorinus expects this through the preaching of Elijah.
- **Either.** Andrew of Caesarea thinks the second more likely and says of the two, "Either interpretation is acceptable." The equal thousands show the fruitfulness of the apostles' sowing (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 7.8).

**The names of the tribes.** Andrew and Bede both read the list as a map of the Christian life, by the meanings then given to the Hebrew names. Bede says John's purpose is not genealogy but to set out the virtues of the Church, which goes from confession, Judah, to the right hand of eternal life, Benjamin.

| Tribe | Andrew of Caesarea | St. Bede |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Judah | "confession": those saved by confessing Christ | "confession" or "praise": where every good work begins |
| Reuben | "son of vision": the pure of heart | "seeing the son": works, the fruit of confession |
| Gad | "temptation": those crowned for enduring it, like Job | "temptation": faith must be tested (Sirach 2:1) |
| Asher | "blessing": those worthy of the Lord's blessing | "blessed": the one who endures trial (James 1:12) |
| Naphtali | "prayer": those bound to God by unceasing prayer | "breadth": a heart enlarged (Psalm 119:32) |
| Manasseh | "forgetfulness": those who left the past behind for love of God | "forgetting": straining toward what lies ahead (Philippians 3:13) |
| Simeon | "obedience": those made just by keeping the commandments | "he has heard sorrow": sorrow turned to joy (John 16:20) |
| Levi | "taken up": the priestly life; eighth in the list, for the eighth day of the resurrection | "addition": those who buy eternal things with temporal |
| Issachar | "reward": those who live well for God's reward | "there is a reward" (Romans 8:18) |
| Zebulun | "abode of power," or "fragrance" (2 Corinthians 2:15) | "habitation of strength" (2 Corinthians 12:9) |
| Joseph | "addition": what is given besides the kingdom (Matthew 6:33) | "gifts of grace to be added" |
| Benjamin | "son of sorrow," "son of day" or "son of my right hand" | "son of the right hand": the elect, or the whole Church (Psalm 45:10) |

**The multitude: the same people, or another?**

- **The same.** St. Caesarius of Arles notes that John does not say he saw another people. It is the people of the 144,000, now seen without number, since by believing all nations have been grafted into the root (*Exposition on the Apocalypse*, Homily 6). Primasius agrees: the sacred number means the whole company of the elect (Romans 8:29), and those from the nations are made Israel, children of Abraham by faith (Galatians 3:7), in the one new man of Ephesians 2:14–16 (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 7.9). This is the reading followed on this page.
- **Another.** For Oecumenius the multitude are the countless believers from the nations, and Bede in his commentary says that after naming Israel John recalls the salvation of the Gentiles (*Explanation of the Apocalypse* 7.9). Andrew sees the martyrs of every tribe and tongue, those of the past and those of the last struggle (Psalm 139:18).

**White robes and palms.** The robes are purity of life (Oecumenius), baptism or the love poured out by the Spirit (Primasius), the gift of the Holy Spirit (Caesarius), or deeds made white by the shedding of one's blood (Andrew). The palms are victory, and for Primasius the triumph of the cross. Bede, preaching on the martyrs, says that those who once lay worn with pain before the thrones of earthly judges now stand crowned before the throne of God. Rachel weeps for each of them (Matthew 2:18): that is, the Church who bore them mourns as they are driven from this life by torments. But the heavenly Jerusalem, the mother of us all, at once receives them into the joy of their Lord. We should not mourn their death, he says, so much as rejoice that they have won the palm (*Homilies on the Gospels* 1.10).

**Their hymn.** Primasius hears one throne and one nature: where the Father and the Lamb are named, the whole Trinity is meant. The seven words of praise in 7:12 name gifts God shares with his Church, and in returning them the Church confesses that she received them (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 7.10–12). Bede adds that the martyrs sing because they overcame by God's help and not by their own strength.

**"Washed in the blood of the Lamb": martyrs only?**

- **The martyrs.** Tertullian takes them in the strict sense: baptism washes the stain away, and martyrdom turns it to dazzling white (*Scorpiace* 12).
- **All the baptized.** Oecumenius asks how blood can whiten. His answer is the sacraments: baptism into the Lord's death (Romans 6:3) and the receiving of his blood, poured out "for the forgiveness of sins" (Matthew 26:28). Caesarius is direct: these are "not, as some think, only martyrs," for the text says the blood of the Lamb, not their own (1 John 1:7).
- **Both.** Primasius gives the martyrs the first place and then widens it to anyone cleansed at the font, fed by the Lord's flesh and kindled by the Spirit. Some, he says, are martyrs before God by their inner life without any public act (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 7.13–14).

**"They worship him day and night."** In prosperity and in adversity, says Primasius, and those who serve in the temple are themselves the temple. For Oecumenius, God's dwelling among them is the unending remembrance of him in their souls. Andrew notes that there will be no night then, only one day lit by the Sun of righteousness, and that "his temple" is the whole creation renewed by the Spirit. Bede calls this service not a labor but one that is "pleasant and desirable."

**No hunger, no thirst, no scorching sun.** Tyconius reads the promise of the sacraments now: they do not hunger because they are fed by "the living bread" (John 6:51), and they do not thirst because of the water Christ gives (John 4:14); the strength of his sacraments shelters them from the heat of temptation (Isaiah 25:4). Oecumenius also takes the sun for temptation, as in the parable of the seed on rocky ground (Mark 4:5–6).

**The Lamb their shepherd, the springs, the tears.** Tyconius: the Lamb "in the midst of the throne" shows one throne of Father and Son, and in the midst of the Church the whole Trinity dwells by faith. The springs are the abundant stream of the Spirit (Andrew, with John 7:38), or the company of the saints, or the vision of God himself, for which the soul longs as the deer for running water (Bede, with Psalm 42:2). Tertullian notes that God wipes the tears from the same eyes that wept, which is a promise of the resurrection of the body (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 58). Tyconius adds that it begins already, spiritually, when sins are forgiven and we are filled with the joy of the Holy Spirit (Isaiah 65:18–19).

*Source: the Fathers are cited from* Revelation, *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 7:1–17.*

## Where readers differ

- **Two groups.** Some commentators, ancient and modern, distinguish them: the 144,000 as believers from Israel and the multitude as believers from the nations; or the 144,000 as a special company (martyrs, or the consecrated) within the larger Church. St. Paul does teach a special place for Israel in God's plan, and that "all Israel will be saved" (Romans 11:26; CCC 674). That truth does not depend on this passage. The Fathers are divided in the same way, as set out above. [[auth:disputed]]
- **A literal count.** Some groups outside the Catholic Church hold that exactly 144,000 persons form a heavenly class. The Church does not read it so.
- **"The time of great distress."** The note in the New American Bible refers it first to "fierce persecution by the Romans." The Lord uses the same words, in Greek, of the end: "great tribulation" (Matthew 24:21). The multitude is gathered from every persecution, and from the ordinary distress of every Christian life faithfully lived.
- **The missing tribe.** Dan is absent from the list and Manasseh is added beside Joseph. See [The seal of the living God](/the-seal-of-the-living-god).

## What it asks of us

Two kinds of confidence, and the passage gives both.

The first is for those who feel few. On earth the Church often looks small, scattered and outnumbered. John is told a number so that he will know God has counted her and that the count is full. No one who belongs to him has been overlooked.

The second is for those who fear that few are saved. John looks, and cannot count them.

Between the hearing and the seeing lies "the time of great distress." The promise is made to those who come through it, and it ends with a line the Church has never tired of repeating: "God will wipe away every tear from their eyes."
