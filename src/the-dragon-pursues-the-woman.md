## The words

- **Pursued.** The Greek *ediōxen* means both "pursued" and "persecuted." It is the ordinary New Testament word for persecution.
- **The woman who had given birth to the male child.** John names her by her motherhood. The dragon cannot reach the Son, so he attacks the mother. The New American Bible's cross-reference for this verse is Genesis 3:15.
- **Dragon and serpent.** In four verses John calls him "the dragon," "the serpent," "the serpent," "the dragon." The names of 12:9 are kept before us: this is the serpent of Eden.
- **Out of his mouth.** Said twice. The weapon is something that comes from a mouth.
- **The earth helped the woman.** The earth, to which he was thrown down and over which woe was just pronounced (12:12), takes the Woman's side.

The eagle's wings and the measured time are studied on [The wilderness and the 1,260 days](/the-wilderness-and-the-1260-days). [[auth:scripture]]

## The Old Testament behind it

**The first enmity.** "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers" (Genesis 3:15). The Catechism calls this "the first announcement of the Messiah and Redeemer, of a battle between the serpent and the Woman, and of the final victory of a descendant of hers" (CCC 410). Revelation 12:13–17 shows that battle. [[auth:church]]

**The Exodus.** Pharaoh, the "great dragon" (Ezekiel 29:3), pursued Israel to the sea, and the waters that should have destroyed her destroyed him: "When you blew with your breath, the sea covered them" (Exodus 15:10), and "the earth swallowed them" (15:12). Later, when Korah rebelled, "the earth opened its mouth and swallowed them" (Numbers 16:32). John uses the very phrase.

**The flood as an invading power.** The prophets speak of hostile empires as rivers in flood. Of Assyria: "The Lord is bringing up against them the waters of the River, great and mighty, the king of Assyria and all his glory. It shall rise above all its channels" (Isaiah 8:7). The psalmist gives thanks for deliverance in the same image: "Had not the LORD been with us, when people rose against us… then the waters would have engulfed us, the torrent overwhelmed us" (Psalm 124:2–4).

**The promise.** "When you pass through waters, I will be with you; through rivers, you shall not be swept away" (Isaiah 43:2). [[auth:scripture]]

## What is the flood?

The text does not say, and two readings have been given. They are not opposed. [[auth:disputed]]

**A flood of persecutors.** This follows the prophets, for whom a river in flood is an army. St. Victorinus reads it so: the water "signifies… the people who at his command would persecute her," and the earth's swallowing it "sets forth the vengeance for the present troubles" ([*Commentary on the Apocalypse*](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ENG1016/_PC.HTM), on chapter 12). [[auth:father]]

**A flood of lies.** This follows the detail John repeats, that it comes from the serpent's *mouth*. In Revelation what comes from a mouth is speech. From Christ's mouth comes "a sharp sword," his word (19:15). From the mouths of the dragon, the beast and the false prophet come "three unclean spirits like frogs" (16:13). The serpent's first act in Scripture was a question and a lie: "Did God really say…?" and "You certainly will not die!" (Genesis 3:1, 4). On this reading the torrent is false teaching, slander and deception, poured out to carry the Church off her feet. St. Paul expected such a thing: a coming "in every wicked deceit for those who are perishing because they have not accepted the love of truth" (2 Thessalonians 2:10), and the Catechism names the last trial "a religious deception" (CCC 675). [[auth:theologian]]

The dragon uses both. He has always worked by violence and by the lie, and the next chapter gives each a body: a beast that makes war (13:7) and a beast that deceives (13:14).

## The earth helped the woman

How does the earth swallow the flood? The text gives only the image, and readers have understood it in three ways.

- **As judgment on the persecutors,** as at the Red Sea and with Korah. This is Victorinus's reading.
- **As God's providence working through created things and human events.** Persecutions exhaust themselves; lies are forgotten; an empire that hunted the Church becomes the soil she grows in. The notes of the New American Bible speak of the Church as "protected by God's special providence (Rev 12:16)" ([note on 12:17](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/revelation/12)). [[auth:theologian]]
- **As a reversal.** The earth was cursed because of the serpent's work (Genesis 3:17) and has just heard a woe pronounced over it (12:12). Here creation itself sides against him.

In every reading the point is the same. The Woman does not stop the flood. It is stopped for her.

## The Woman: Mary and the Church

**The Church.** The promise to Peter says the same thing without images: "upon this rock I will build my church, and the gates of the netherworld shall not prevail against it" (Matthew 16:18). So does the parable: "The rain fell, the floods came, and the winds blew and buffeted the house. But it did not collapse; it had been set solidly on rock" (Matthew 7:25). The Church is not promised that the flood will not come. She is promised that it will not carry her away. [[auth:scripture]]

**Mary.** The Catechism reads the verse of her: "'He pursued the woman' but had no hold on her: the new Eve, 'full of grace' of the Holy Spirit, is preserved from sin and the corruption of death (the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption of the Most Holy Mother of God, Mary, ever virgin)" (CCC 2853). The serpent's flood, sin and death, did not touch her at the beginning of her life or at its end. What is promised to the Church as a whole was given to Mary in full. [[auth:church]]

The two readings meet where they always do. Because the dragon has no hold on the Woman, "the dragon became angry with the woman and went off to wage war against the rest of her offspring" (12:17). That is the next page.

## What the Fathers said further

St. Victorinus is quoted above. The other commentators read the pursuit either of Mary or of the Church, and one of them hesitates between saying and not saying. [[auth:father]]

**The pursuit read of Mary.**

- **St. Epiphanius of Salamis** (died 403) is the first writer known to raise the question. He is defending Mary's perpetual virginity. The Lord on the cross entrusted her to John (John 19:26–27), which he would not have done had she had a husband and other children. Then he observes that Scripture nowhere tells of her death, whether she died or was buried, and he will not go beyond it. Simeon told her a sword would pierce her soul (Luke 2:35); and John's Revelation says the dragon pursued the woman who bore the male child, and she was given wings and taken to the wilderness out of his reach. "Perhaps this can be applied to her," he writes; he cannot decide. He does not say she remained immortal, and he does not affirm that she died (*Panarion* 78.11). The Church has since defined that Mary was taken up body and soul into heaven, and has left open whether she first died (Pius XII, *Munificentissimus Deus*, 1950).
- **Oecumenius** reads the whole passage of her life. The dragon, fallen and envious that human beings should rise to the heaven he lost, turns on the woman who bore the Savior. The wings of the great eagle are the angel's visit to Joseph, by which mother and child reached Egypt as on wings. When that plot through Herod failed, the dragon devised another: the death of her Son. The river is the trial of the Lord's Passion, by which he meant to drown the Virgin in grief, and Simeon's sword is its measure. The earth swallowed the river when it received the crucified Lord into itself. But its real help was that it "gave the Lord back again": on the third day he rose, death being unable to hold the Author of life (Acts 3:15) (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.13–17).

**The pursuit read of the Church.**

- **The two wings.** For Primasius and St. Bede they are the two Testaments, by which the Church is taught to escape the enemy; Primasius adds the twofold love of God and neighbor. St. Andrew of Caesarea names all of these together, with a mind open to the Crucified. St. Methodius reads them as virginity, and calls his hearers to arm themselves against the seven-headed beast: each head is a vice, and whoever overcomes it wins the matching crown, temperance over self-indulgence, martyrdom over cowardice (*Symposium* 8.12–13).
- **The desert.** Primasius: it is the solitude of our present exile, while we are away from the Lord (2 Corinthians 5:6), and still more the heart that leaves the world by affection, as the psalmist longed to fly away and rest (Psalm 55:7–8). The eagle fits the Church because it soars far and yet stoops for its food, as she seeks what is above and still bears the needs of the body (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.14). Andrew: a way of life free of disordered desire. This is true always, and will be most true under the antichrist, when even those hidden in the mountains and caves of the real desert will flee him.
- **"A time, times and half a time."** Bede takes it for the whole time of the Church, the same span as the 1,260 days. Primasius: the time to the world's end, in which the Church grows and flees every error of the serpent.
- **The river and the earth.** Andrew: the river is a host of godless people, evil spirits and temptations sent to enslave her. The earth that swallows it may be the long dry distances of the desert, or the humility of the saints, who say "I am dust and ashes" and so undo the devil's snares. Primasius gives two readings. The earth may be the Church, founded on the saints, whose prayers and teaching expose the enemy's designs (Psalm 104:5). Better, he thinks, it is the human nature of Christ: truth sprung from the earth (Psalm 85:12), which appears before God for us and swallowed death in the abundance of its own life (*Commentary on the Apocalypse* 12.16).

*Source: unless another source is linked, the Fathers in this section are cited from* Revelation, *Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 12:13–16.*

## Where readers differ

- **Past or future.** The first-century reading sees the Church's escapes under Rome; Victorinus and Hippolytus see the last persecution; many see every age. As with the 1,260 days, the pattern recurs and will have a last instance (CCC 675–677). [[auth:disputed]]
- **A particular event?** Some have looked for one historical flight behind the image, such as the Christians of Jerusalem leaving the city before its fall in A.D. 70. Nothing in the text requires it, and the Church has not taught it.
- **The flood.** Armies, heresies, or both, as above.

## What it asks of us

The passage is honest about what to expect. Being hated by the dragon is not a sign that something has gone wrong; it follows from his defeat (12:13). And the form of the attack is named in advance: a torrent, something meant to sweep people along by its sheer volume. A flood of words works the same way as a flood of water. It does not argue. It carries.

The answer in the text is not the Woman's own strength. She is given wings, given a place, and given the earth's help. We are asked to stand where she stands: "When you pass through waters, I will be with you; through rivers, you shall not be swept away" (Isaiah 43:2).
