Revelation 12:7–9

War in Heaven

Michael and his angels fight the dragon, and the dragon is thrown down. Who Michael is, when this war is fought, and why the Church has put the passage into her prayer.

Home › The Study › War in heaven

✓ Reviewed by Fr. John How this site is made

The text
"Then war broke out in heaven; Michael and his angels battled against the dragon. The dragon and its angels fought back, but they did not prevail and there was no longer any place for them in heaven. The huge dragon, the ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan, who deceived the whole world, was thrown down to earth, and its angels were thrown down with it" (Revelation 12:7–9).
In one sentence
The dragon is defeated in heaven by an angel whose name is a question, "Who is like God?", and the hymn that follows says the victory was won "by the blood of the Lamb."
Authority
Scripture

The words

  • War broke out in heaven. It is the only place in Scripture that says so directly.
  • Michael and his angels. Michael acts. Elsewhere in Revelation God and the Lamb defeat their enemies by a word; here the dragon is not given the honor of fighting God himself. An angel is enough.
  • No longer any place. The dragon loses his standing in heaven. The next verse says what that standing was: he is "the accuser of our brothers… who accuses them before our God day and night" (12:10).
  • Thrown down. The verb comes three times in verse 9 and again in verses 10 and 13. The whole passage is about a fall.
  • Four names. The dragon, the ancient serpent, the Devil ("slanderer"), Satan ("adversary"). John piles them up at the moment of his defeat, so that the reader knows exactly who has lost.

Scripture

Michael

His name is Hebrew, Mi-ka-El, and it is a question: "Who is like God?" The notes of the New American Bible point out the echo in the next chapter, where the world says of the beast, "Who can compare with the beast?" (13:4). The beast's worshipers borrow the archangel's war cry and give it to a creature (note on 12:7). A theologian

In the Old Testament he is the guardian of God's people. The angel who speaks to Daniel calls him "one of the chief princes" and "your prince" (Daniel 10:13, 21), and foretells: "At that time there shall arise Michael, the great prince, guardian of your people; it shall be a time unsurpassed in distress since the nation began until that time. At that time your people shall escape, everyone who is found written in the book" (Daniel 12:1). In the New Testament he is "the archangel Michael," who did not revile even the devil, "but said, 'May the Lord rebuke you!'" (Jude 9). Scripture

Pope St. Gregory the Great explains why this angel is sent: "Whenever some act of wondrous power must be performed, Michael is sent, so that his action and his name may make it clear that no one can do what God does by his superior power" (Homilies on the Gospels 34, read in the Liturgy of the Hours on September 29). A Father or Doctor

The Church teaches that "the existence of the spiritual, non-corporeal beings that Sacred Scripture usually calls 'angels' is a truth of faith" (CCC 328), and that "Christ is the centre of the angelic world. They are his angels" (CCC 331). Michael fights as Christ's servant, not as his substitute. Church teaching

When is this war?

The text can be read of three moments, and the Church has not confined it to one. Disputed

1. At the beginning. Many Christians read the passage as the fall of the angels before human history. The Church does teach that fall: Satan "was at first a good angel," and the angels' sin was "the free choice of these created spirits, who radically and irrevocably rejected God and his reign" (CCC 391–392). The dragon sweeping down a third of the stars (12:4) is often read the same way. This reading is true as doctrine. Whether it is what John is narrating here is another matter, because of where he places it.

2. At the Cross and the Ascension. John places the war after the child is caught up to God's throne (12:5), and the hymn that follows dates it: "Now have salvation and power come, and the kingdom of our God and the authority of his Anointed. For the accuser of our brothers is cast out" (12:10). The martyrs "conquered him by the blood of the Lamb" (12:11). The Lord had said the same on the eve of his Passion: "Now is the time of judgment on this world; now the ruler of this world will be driven out" (John 12:31); and earlier, "I have observed Satan fall like lightning from the sky" (Luke 10:18). The Catechism reads it so: "Victory over the 'prince of this world' was won once for all at the Hour when Jesus freely gave himself up to death to give us his life. This is the judgment of this world, and the prince of this world is 'cast out'" (CCC 2853). On this reading, what Michael does in heaven is the heavenly side of what Christ did on Calvary: the accuser loses his place because there is nothing left to accuse. Church teaching

3. At the end. St. Victorinus reads the war as still to come: "this is the beginning of Antichrist," after Elijah has preached, when the dragon is cast down and stirs up his last assault (Commentary on the Apocalypse, on chapter 12). Daniel's prophecy of Michael arising in "a time unsurpassed in distress" (Daniel 12:1) supports him. A Father or Doctor

These need not be chosen between. The angels fell at the beginning; the accuser was cast out by the Cross; and what was won then will be finished at the end. John shows the decisive moment, and it is the one in the middle.

Thrown down to the earth

The defeat is real, and it has a consequence that the chapter does not hide: "Woe to you, earth and sea, for the Devil has come down to you in great fury, for he knows he has but a short time" (12:12). He has lost heaven, so he turns on the Woman (12:13) and then on "the rest of her offspring" (12:17).

The Catechism describes this state of things exactly. Christ's reign "is still under attack by the evil powers, even though they have been defeated definitively by Christ's Passover" (CCC 671). The fury of the dragon on earth is not a sign that he is winning. It is the fury of one who has already lost where it matters and knows his time is short.

How the Church prays it

  • The feast. On September 29 the Church keeps the feast of Saints Michael, Gabriel and Raphael, and this passage is one of the readings appointed for the Mass. Church teaching
  • The liturgy every day. "In her liturgy, the Church joins with the angels to adore the thrice-holy God. She invokes their assistance" (CCC 335). Church teaching
  • The prayer of Leo XIII. In 1886 Pope Leo XIII ordered a prayer to be said after Low Mass that takes its words from this chapter: "Saint Michael the Archangel, defend us in battle; be our protection against the wickedness and snares of the devil. May God rebuke him, we humbly pray; and do thou, O Prince of the heavenly host, by the power of God, cast into hell Satan and all the evil spirits who prowl about the world seeking the ruin of souls. Amen." The words "May God rebuke him" are Michael's own (Jude 9). The prayer is no longer required after Mass, and many of the faithful still say it. Church teaching

Where readers differ

  • The time of the war, as above.
  • Whether Michael is Christ. A few interpreters outside the Catholic tradition have identified Michael with Christ. The Church does not: Michael is an archangel, a creature, and Christ is "the centre of the angelic world" whom the angels serve (CCC 331).
  • How literally to picture it. Angels are "spiritual, non-corporeal beings" (CCC 328). A war among them is a real conflict of wills and of obedience, shown to John in the only images human eyes can take in.

What it asks of us

Michael's name is the whole of his strategy. He does not answer the dragon's pride with a greater pride of his own; he asks, "Who is like God?" Every temptation is, at bottom, an invitation to answer that question wrongly, and the worship of the beast in the next chapter is the wrong answer given by the whole world (13:4).

The passage asks us to give the right answer, and to give it without fear. "From infancy to death human life is surrounded by their watchful care and intercession. Beside each believer stands an angel as protector and shepherd leading him to life" (CCC 336).
