Revelation 11:3–14

The Two Witnesses

Two prophets in sackcloth, with the powers of Moses and Elijah, are killed by the beast and raised by God. Who the tradition has taken them to be, and what the Church has left open.

Home › The Study › The two witnesses

Draft · awaiting Fr. John's review

The text
"I will commission my two witnesses to prophesy for those twelve hundred and sixty days, wearing sackcloth." Fire comes from their mouths; they can close the sky and turn water to blood. "When they have finished their testimony, the beast that comes up from the abyss will wage war against them and conquer them and kill them." Their bodies lie in the street of the great city for three and a half days. "A breath of life from God entered them," and "they went up to heaven in a cloud as their enemies looked on" (Revelation 11:3–12).
In one sentence
God's witness in the world is given, opposed, killed and vindicated, in the pattern of Christ's own death and rising; the tradition has seen in the two both particular prophets, Elijah above all, and the witnessing Church.
Authority
Scripture

The words

  • My two witnesses. God calls them his. The Greek is martyres, from which "martyrs" comes.
  • Two. The Law required it: "a charge shall stand only on the testimony of two or three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15). The Lord sent his disciples out two by two. The notes of the New American Bible give this as the reason for the number (note on 11:3).
  • Sackcloth. The dress of mourning and repentance. Their message is a call to conversion.
  • When they have finished. They are not killed before their work is done. The beast has no power over them until then.
  • The beast that comes up from the abyss. This is the first mention of the beast in Revelation. He is introduced as the killer of God's witnesses two chapters before he is described.

Scripture

The Old Testament behind it

Zechariah's lampstand. "These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands that stand before the Lord of the earth" (11:4). The prophet saw a lampstand with an olive tree on either side, and was told: "These are the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord of the whole earth" (Zechariah 4:14). In his day they were most likely Joshua the high priest and Zerubbabel the governor: priesthood and kingship, feeding the light of God's house.

Elijah. "They have the power to close up the sky so that no rain can fall" (11:6). Elijah shut the sky for three and a half years (1 Kings 17:1; Luke 4:25), the very length of the witnesses' prophesying, and called down fire: "divine fire came down from heaven and consumed him and his fifty men" (2 Kings 1:12). And "Elijah went up to heaven in a whirlwind" (2 Kings 2:11), as the witnesses go up in a cloud.

Moses. "They also have power to turn water into blood and to afflict the earth with any plague" (11:6; Exodus 7:17–20).

Ezekiel's valley. "A breath of life from God entered them. When they stood on their feet…" (11:11). The words are from the vision of the dry bones: "the breath entered them; they came to life and stood on their feet" (Ezekiel 37:10). There the dead who rise are the whole house of Israel. Scripture

So the two witnesses are drawn with the features of Moses and Elijah, the Law and the Prophets, the two who stood with Christ at the Transfiguration; and they are named with the image of a priest and a king.

Who are they? The tradition's readings

The Church has not defined who the two witnesses are. The tradition offers two kinds of answer. Disputed

Two prophets who will come

Elijah is nearly always one. Scripture promises his coming by name: "Now I am sending to you Elijah the prophet, before the day of the LORD comes" (Malachi 3:23). He is "destined… to put an end to wrath before the day of the LORD… and to re-establish the tribes of Israel" (Sirach 48:10). The Lord confirmed it, "Elijah will indeed come and restore all things," while teaching that in John the Baptist "Elijah has already come" (Matthew 17:11–12). St. Augustine calls Elijah's coming before the judgment "a familiar theme in the conversation and heart of the faithful," through whose teaching, he expects, the Jewish people will come to believe in Christ (City of God XX.29). A Father or Doctor

The second is less settled.

The fuller accounts are on A Great Sign: Hippolytus, Victorinus, John Damascene, Bellarmine.

The witnessing Church

Others read the two as the Church herself, bearing witness through her whole history.

  • St. Cyprian took them as two kinds of martyrs, and others, the Haydock commentary records, as the testimonies of the Old and the New Testament (on Apocalypse 11). A Father or Doctor
  • St. Bede reads them chiefly as the Church, made one out of two peoples, and the two olive trees as the two Testaments that feed her lamp; he also records the reading of Enoch and Elijah (Explanation of the Apocalypse). A Father or Doctor
  • The notes of the New American Bible: the witnesses "cannot readily be identified. Do they represent Moses and Elijah, or the Law and the Prophets, or Peter and Paul? Most probably they refer to the universal church, especially the Christian martyrs, fulfilling the office of witness" (note on 11:3). A theologian

How the two kinds of reading relate

They are not rivals in the way they may seem. Those who expect Elijah in person do not deny that the Church bears witness in every age; those who read the Church do not deny that Elijah is promised. Bede holds both. A reader may see in the two witnesses the Church's prophetic witness through all her history, and expect that witness to be gathered, at the last, into persons whom God will send.

The pattern of their witness

Whoever they are, John tells their story in the shape of another.

The witnesses Christ
They prophesy for 1,260 days, about three and a half years His public ministry is traditionally counted as about three years
Killed in "the great city… where indeed their Lord was crucified" (11:8) Crucified outside Jerusalem
The world rejoices over their bodies (11:10) "You will weep and mourn, while the world rejoices" (John 16:20)
After three and a half days the breath of life enters them (11:11) Raised on the third day
"They went up to heaven in a cloud" (11:12) "A cloud took him from their sight" (Acts 1:9)

The Catechism says this of the whole Church: she "will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection" (CCC 677). The two witnesses are that sentence told as a story. Church teaching

The great city

Their bodies lie in "the great city, which has the symbolic names 'Sodom' and 'Egypt,' where indeed their Lord was crucified" (11:8). John tells us himself that the names are symbolic. The notes of the New American Bible take the city as Rome, "called Babylon in Rev 16–18," "not the geographical but the symbolic Jerusalem that rejects God and his witnesses" (note on 11:8). Others take it as Jerusalem itself; others as the whole world organized against God. It is every city that has killed the prophets. Disputed

A different ending

One detail sets this passage apart from the trumpets before it. After the plagues of chapters 8 and 9, the survivors "did not repent" (9:20–21). After the witnesses are raised, "the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13). To "give glory to God" is what the first angel of chapter 14 will ask of every nation (14:7).

What punishment did not do, the death and vindication of the witnesses does. Many readers have taken this as the heart of the passage: the world is converted not by plagues but by the faithful witness of those who are willing to die. A theologian

What the tradition leaves open

This study names no one as a witness, and it will not. But it is fair to say plainly what the Church has and has not settled, so that a reader knows where there is room. Disputed

  • Not settled: whether the witnesses are two individuals, or the Church, or both. Bede and the modern notes show that all three positions are held within the Church.
  • Not settled: who the second witness is, if they are individuals. The Fathers themselves name Enoch, Jeremiah and Moses.
  • Not settled: whether Elijah's promised coming was wholly fulfilled in John the Baptist or is still to be completed. The Lord's words can be read either way, and Augustine expected a coming still to be.
  • Not settled: whether the "two" must be persons of the past, or could be persons raised up by God in the last days "in the spirit and power of Elijah," as the angel said of John (Luke 1:17).
  • Settled by the text: whoever they are, they are God's witnesses and not self-appointed; they preach repentance; they are given signs; they are killed by the beast and raised by God. Any reading has to meet all of these.
  • Settled by the Church: no one may compute dates from their 1,260 days (CCC 673), and the Church's hope does not rest on identifying them.

A reader may hold an opinion here. It should be held as an opinion, tested against everything the text says, and never in a way that expects or wishes anyone's death.

Where readers differ

  • Literal or symbolic, as above.
  • Past or future. The first-century reading sees the martyrs under Rome, and some scholars have proposed Peter and Paul, who died there. The Fathers who read two prophets place them at the end. The reading of the Church as witness covers all the time between.
  • The signs. Whether the fire and the plagues are to be seen literally, or are the power of the prophetic word, as when the Lord tells Jeremiah that his words in the prophet's mouth will be a fire (Jeremiah 5:14).

What it asks of us

Before it asks us to identify anyone, it asks us to be witnesses. Every Christian is one by Baptism and Confirmation, and the description holds: sent by God and not by oneself; in sackcloth, which is to say with repentance and without triumph; protected until the work is done; and not promised a better end in this world than the Lord's.

It also tells us what to expect of the world's reaction, and not to be shaken by it. The witnesses' death is a festival: people "gloat over them and be glad and exchange gifts" (11:10). Three and a half days later the same people are afraid. John wants his readers to know the order of those two scenes in advance.
