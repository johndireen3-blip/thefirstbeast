Revelation 11:1–2

The Temple Measured

John is given a rod and told to measure the sanctuary and count those who worship there. The outer court is left out. The approach to chapter 12 begins with a promise and a warning.

The text
"Then I was given a measuring rod like a staff and I was told, 'Come and measure the temple of God and the altar, and count those who are worshiping in it. But exclude the outer court of the temple; do not measure it, for it has been handed over to the Gentiles, who will trample the holy city for forty-two months'" (Revelation 11:1–2).
In one sentence
God marks out what is his and will keep it, while allowing what is outward to be trampled for a limited time: the same thing chapter 12 says of the Woman and her children.
Authority
Scripture

Why the study begins here

Chapter 11 is the approach to chapter 12. It introduces, in order, three things the Woman's chapter will take up: a measured time of forty-two months or 1,260 days (11:2–3; 12:6, 14); "the beast that comes up from the abyss" who makes war on God's witnesses (11:7; 13:7); and the temple in heaven, opened at the end to show the ark (11:19). A reader who comes to chapter 12 from chapter 11 already knows the time, the enemy and the place.

The words

  • A measuring rod. In the prophets, to measure a thing is to claim it and to mark it for preservation or rebuilding.
  • The temple. The Greek is naos, the inner sanctuary, as in 11:19. John is told to measure the sanctuary, the altar, and the people: "count those who are worshiping in it." The worshipers are part of what is measured.
  • Exclude. Literally "cast out." The outer court is deliberately left unprotected.
  • Trample. The word the Lord used: "Jerusalem will be trampled underfoot by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled" (Luke 21:24).

Scripture

The Old Testament behind it

Ezekiel is carried in a vision to a high mountain and shown a man with a measuring rod, who measures a new temple in every part (Ezekiel 40–42). It is the plan of a restored dwelling of God with his people, given while the first temple lay in ruins.

Zechariah sees "a man with a measuring cord in his hand," going "to measure Jerusalem," and hears the promise that the Lord himself will be "an encircling wall of fire for it" (Zechariah 2:5–9).

In both, measuring is an act of ownership and care. What God measures, he means to keep. Scripture

What is measured, and what is not

When John wrote, the temple in Jerusalem had already been destroyed by the Romans (A.D. 70), or was about to be. He is not measuring a building of stone.

The sanctuary is the Church at prayer. The notes of the New American Bible: "The temple and altar symbolize the new Israel… The worshipers represent Christians. The measuring of the temple… suggests that God will preserve the faithful remnant… who remain true to Christ" (note on 11:1). The New Testament speaks the same way throughout: "Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwells in you?" (1 Corinthians 3:16); you are "built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ Jesus himself as the capstone… a temple sacred in the Lord" (Ephesians 2:20–21); "like living stones, let yourselves be built into a spiritual house" (1 Peter 2:5). A theologian

The outer court is the same Church, seen from outside. Here readers differ, and the difference matters. Disputed

  • On one reading, the sanctuary and the court are two groups: faithful Christians within, and those outside, whether unbelievers or Christians in name only.
  • On the other, they are one people seen in two ways. In her inner life, her worship and her union with God, the Church cannot be touched. In her outward life, her members' bodies, goods, good name and peace, she is given over to be trampled for a time.

The second reading fits what follows. The two witnesses are protected while they prophesy (11:5) and are then killed (11:7). The Woman is kept safe in the desert while the dragon makes war on her children (12:14–17). The notes of the New American Bible say of that passage what could be said of this one: "Although the church is protected by God's special providence… the individual Christian is to expect persecution and suffering" (note on 12:17).

Forty-two months

This is the first appearance in Revelation of the measured time. It comes back at once as 1,260 days (11:3), and again in chapter 12 (12:6, 14) and chapter 13 (13:5).

The notes of the New American Bible give its origin: it is "the duration of the vicious persecution of the Jews by Antiochus IV Epiphanes (Dn 7:25; 12:7); this persecution of three and a half years (half of seven, counted as 1260 days in Rev 11:3; 12:6) became the prototype of periods of trial for God's people… The reference here is to the persecution by the Romans" (note on 11:2). A theologian

The number is studied on The wilderness and the 1,260 days. What matters here is where it is first placed: on the trampling. The time of the Church's outward suffering is measured from its first mention. It has a length, and God has set it.

Read with the Woman

The Church calls herself by the names this passage uses: "the house of God," "the dwelling-place of God among men," "the holy temple" (CCC 756). And she calls Mary the place where that dwelling was first made: "the ark of the covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord dwells" (CCC 2676). Church teaching

So the section that begins with a sanctuary measured on earth ends with the sanctuary opened in heaven and the ark seen within it (11:19), and then the Woman (12:1). What John is told to measure at the beginning of chapter 11, he is shown in its fullness at the end.

Where readers differ

  • A temple to be rebuilt? Some Christians expect a third temple of stone in Jerusalem and read this passage of it. Several of the Fathers expected the Antichrist to seat himself in a rebuilt temple, and others, with St. Augustine, thought it uncertain which temple is meant. The Church has not taught that a temple must be rebuilt, and the New Testament's own use of "temple" for the Church weighs against reading these verses that way. Disputed
  • Which trampling. The fall of Jerusalem in A.D. 70; the persecutions under Rome; every persecution; the last one. As elsewhere in this part of the book, the pattern recurs.
  • The outer court, as above.

What it asks of us

To know what has been promised and what has not. God has not promised that the Church will be spared in her outward life. He has promised that what is measured will be kept: those who worship, the altar, the sanctuary where he dwells.

The passage also asks where we are standing. John is told to count "those who are worshiping." The line between the sanctuary and the court does not run between one building and another. It runs through the life of each Christian, between what is given to God in worship and what is merely outward.
