Revelation 20:7–10

Gog and Magog, and the Dragon's End

The dragon is released for one last deception, surrounds the camp of the holy ones, and is destroyed by fire from heaven. The enemy of the Woman since Genesis is gone from the story.

The text
"When the thousand years are completed, Satan will be released from his prison. He will go out to deceive the nations at the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them for battle; their number is like the sand of the sea. They invaded the breadth of the earth and surrounded the camp of the holy ones and the beloved city. But fire came down from heaven and consumed them. The Devil who had led them astray was thrown into the pool of fire and sulfur, where the beast and the false prophet were" (Revelation 20:7–10).
In one sentence
At the end the Church is surrounded and does not save herself; God does, and the deceiver who first appeared in Eden is removed for ever.
Authority
Scripture

Where the vision stands

The dragon was bound "so that it could no longer lead the nations astray" (The dragon bound). Released, he does the one thing he was kept from doing: "He will go out to deceive the nations." It is his only work. From the garden to this verse, he has never had any power but the lie.

The words

  • Released from his prison. He does not escape. He "will be released," as at 20:3 "it is to be released." Even his last act is by permission.
  • The four corners of the earth. Everywhere. This is not one nation or one region.
  • Gog and Magog. The notes of the New American Bible: "symbols of all pagan nations; the names are taken from Ez 38:1–39:20" (note on 20:8).
  • Like the sand of the sea. The dragon took his stand on the sand of the sea to call up the beast (12:18). The phrase also echoes the promise to Abraham, whose descendants were to be as countless as "the sands of the seashore" (Genesis 22:17). The devil's host is a counterfeit of God's people to the last.
  • The camp of the holy ones. The word for Israel's camp in the desert. The Church at the end is still a people on pilgrimage, living in tents.
  • The beloved city. She is a camp and a city at once: on the way, and already the place God loves. This is the city that will be shown as the Bride in the next chapter.
  • Fire came down from heaven. No army goes out. No battle is fought.
  • Where the beast and the false prophet were. The three who stood together at the end of chapter 12 and through chapter 13 are together again.

Scripture

The Old Testament behind it

Ezekiel 38–39 tells of "Gog of the land of Magog," a prince from the far north who leads "many nations" against Israel when she is living securely, and is destroyed by God alone: "flooding rain and hailstones, fire and brimstone, I will rain down on him, on his troops and on the many nations with him. And so I will show my greatness and holiness" (Ezekiel 38:22–23).

The notes of the New American Bible on Ezekiel say that these oracles "describe a mythic attack of God against a final enemy of his people sometime in the future," and that "the name is symbolic." Their purpose "is to strengthen Israel's hope in God, since they end with God's triumph on behalf of the people." A theologian

John makes two changes. In Ezekiel, Gog is a prince and Magog his land; in Revelation they are two names for all the deceived nations. In Ezekiel they come from the north; in Revelation from "the four corners of the earth." Both changes widen the picture. John has taken a prophecy about one invader and made it a name for the last gathering of the whole world against the people of God.

Not a nation on a map

In almost every century, readers have matched Gog and Magog to a people of their own day: Goths, Huns, Mongols, and in modern times one great power or another. Every one of those identifications has failed, and the text gives the reason. Gog and Magog are "the nations at the four corners of the earth." The name cannot belong to one country, because John has given it to all of them.

This study names no nation. The enemy in this passage is not a people. It is the deceiver, and those he has deceived can be found anywhere. What a reader can watch for is not a flag but a deception wide enough to take in the world; see How to test a reading of Revelation.

The Church surrounded

The picture is of a small encampment and an army without number around it. Nothing in the text suggests the holy ones can win.

This is how the Catechism describes the end. "Before Christ's second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers" (CCC 675). "The Church will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection. The kingdom will be fulfilled, then, not by a historic triumph of the Church through a progressive ascendancy, but only by God's victory over the final unleashing of evil, which will cause his Bride to come down from heaven. God's triumph over the revolt of evil will take the form of the Last Judgment" (CCC 677). Church teaching

The Catechism's footnote to "the final unleashing of evil" cites this passage, Revelation 20:7–10. The Church reads Gog and Magog as her own last trial, and reads its outcome as God's doing alone.

It is the pattern of the Lord's Passion. On Good Friday he was surrounded, and no one fought for him, and he did not save himself. The Church at the end is asked to resemble him.

The dragon's end

"The Devil who had led them astray was thrown into the pool of fire and sulfur."

Follow him back through the book and through the Bible. He is "the ancient serpent" (20:2), the one who deceived the woman in the garden (Genesis 3:13). He stood before the Woman to devour her child (12:4). He was thrown out of heaven (12:9), pursued her (12:13), made war on her children (12:17), and raised up the beast and the false prophet (13:1, 11). He was bound (20:2). Here he is ended.

The first promise in Scripture was made to him, as a sentence: "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; They will strike at your head, while you strike at their heel" (Genesis 3:15). St. Paul wrote to the Romans, "the God of peace will quickly crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20). This verse is that promise kept. Scripture

Nothing is said of his being heard, or answering. In Genesis God questions the man and the woman, and does not question the serpent. So here.

What follows

With the deceiver gone, John sees the judgment: "a large white throne," the dead "great and lowly" standing before it, the scrolls and the book of life (20:11–15). Then the last enemy: "Death and Hades were thrown into the pool of fire" (20:14), as St. Paul had said, "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26).

Of the judgment the Catechism says: "Only the Father knows the day and the hour… We shall know the ultimate meaning of the whole work of creation and of the entire economy of salvation… The Last Judgment will reveal that God's justice triumphs over all the injustices committed by his creatures and that God's love is stronger than death" (CCC 1040). Church teaching

On the pool of fire, the notes of the New American Bible call it a "symbol of God's punishment," and "the second death" is "the eternal death, when sinners will receive their final punishment." The Church teaches that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035). She has never taught that any particular human being is there.

Read with the Woman

Her enemy has been in the story since its third chapter. After this verse he is not mentioned again. When the next chapter opens, "the sea was no more" (21:1): the sea from which the beast rose, "home of the dragon," as the notes say. And a woman appears, a bride, with no dragon standing before her.

The Church sings of Mary in the words of Genesis: she is the Woman whose offspring strikes the serpent's head. This is where the blow is finished.

Where readers differ

  • One battle or two. Whether this is the same gathering as 16:14–16 and 19:19, seen again from another side, or a later one. Those who read chapter 20 as a retelling of the Church's whole age take them as one. Both passages draw on Ezekiel's Gog. Disputed
  • How literal. The notes of the New American Bible call it "a description of the symbolic battle to take place when Satan is released at the end of time." The Church teaches a real final trial (CCC 675) without saying what outward form it will take.
  • "The beloved city." The notes identify it as Jerusalem and refer to Mount Zion, the gathering-place of "the Israel of faith." Most Catholic commentators read it as the Church throughout the world.
  • When. No one knows, and the Church forbids no hope and sets no date (CCC 673, 1040).

What it asks of us

To expect that the Church may look defeated at the end, and not to conclude from this that God has abandoned her. The camp of the holy ones is small and surrounded when the fire falls.

And to place hope where the passage places it. Not in the Church's numbers, and not in any power that offers to defend her. "Fire came down from heaven." The same hope is in every Our Father: "deliver us from evil." The Catechism ends its commentary on that petition with this very scene: "Therefore the Spirit and the Church pray: 'Come, Lord Jesus,' since his coming will deliver us from the Evil One" (CCC 2853).
