Revelation 20:7–10

Gog and Magog, and the Dragon's End

The dragon is released for one last deception, surrounds the camp of the holy ones, and is destroyed by fire from heaven. The enemy of the Woman since Genesis is gone from the story.

The text
"When the thousand years are completed, Satan will be released from his prison. He will go out to deceive the nations at the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them for battle; their number is like the sand of the sea. They invaded the breadth of the earth and surrounded the camp of the holy ones and the beloved city. But fire came down from heaven and consumed them. The Devil who had led them astray was thrown into the pool of fire and sulfur, where the beast and the false prophet were" (Revelation 20:7–10).
In one sentence
At the end the Church is surrounded and does not save herself; God does, and the deceiver who first appeared in Eden is removed for ever.
Authority
Scripture

Where the vision stands

The dragon was bound "so that it could no longer lead the nations astray" (The dragon bound). Released, he does the one thing he was kept from doing: "He will go out to deceive the nations." It is his only work. From the garden to this verse, he has never had any power but the lie.

The words

  • Released from his prison. He does not escape. He "will be released," as at 20:3 "it is to be released." Even his last act is by permission.
  • The four corners of the earth. Everywhere. This is not one nation or one region.
  • Gog and Magog. The notes of the New American Bible: "symbols of all pagan nations; the names are taken from Ez 38:1–39:20" (note on 20:8).
  • Like the sand of the sea. The dragon took his stand on the sand of the sea to call up the beast (12:18). The phrase also echoes the promise to Abraham, whose descendants were to be as countless as "the sands of the seashore" (Genesis 22:17). The devil's host is a counterfeit of God's people to the last.
  • The camp of the holy ones. The word for Israel's camp in the desert. The Church at the end is still a people on pilgrimage, living in tents.
  • The beloved city. She is a camp and a city at once: on the way, and already the place God loves. This is the city that will be shown as the Bride in the next chapter.
  • Fire came down from heaven. No army goes out. No battle is fought.
  • Where the beast and the false prophet were. The three who stood together at the end of chapter 12 and through chapter 13 are together again.

Scripture

The Old Testament behind it

Ezekiel 38–39 tells of "Gog of the land of Magog," a prince from the far north who leads "many nations" against Israel when she is living securely, and is destroyed by God alone: "flooding rain and hailstones, fire and brimstone, I will rain down on him, on his troops and on the many nations with him. And so I will show my greatness and holiness" (Ezekiel 38:22–23).

The notes of the New American Bible on Ezekiel say that these oracles "describe a mythic attack of God against a final enemy of his people sometime in the future," and that "the name is symbolic." Their purpose "is to strengthen Israel's hope in God, since they end with God's triumph on behalf of the people." A theologian

John makes two changes. In Ezekiel, Gog is a prince and Magog his land; in Revelation they are two names for all the deceived nations. In Ezekiel they come from the north; in Revelation from "the four corners of the earth." Both changes widen the picture. John has taken a prophecy about one invader and made it a name for the last gathering of the whole world against the people of God.

Not a nation on a map

In almost every century, readers have matched Gog and Magog to a people of their own day: Goths, Huns, Mongols, and in modern times one great power or another. Every one of those identifications has failed, and the text gives the reason. Gog and Magog are "the nations at the four corners of the earth." The name cannot belong to one country, because John has given it to all of them.

This study names no nation. The enemy in this passage is not a people. It is the deceiver, and those he has deceived can be found anywhere. What a reader can watch for is not a flag but a deception wide enough to take in the world; see How to test a reading of Revelation.

The Church surrounded

The picture is of a small encampment and an army without number around it. Nothing in the text suggests the holy ones can win.

This is how the Catechism describes the end. "Before Christ's second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers" (CCC 675). "The Church will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection. The kingdom will be fulfilled, then, not by a historic triumph of the Church through a progressive ascendancy, but only by God's victory over the final unleashing of evil, which will cause his Bride to come down from heaven. God's triumph over the revolt of evil will take the form of the Last Judgment" (CCC 677). Church teaching

The Catechism's footnote to "the final unleashing of evil" cites this passage, Revelation 20:7–10. The Church reads Gog and Magog as her own last trial, and reads its outcome as God's doing alone.

It is the pattern of the Lord's Passion. On Good Friday he was surrounded, and no one fought for him, and he did not save himself. The Church at the end is asked to resemble him.

The dragon's end

"The Devil who had led them astray was thrown into the pool of fire and sulfur."

Follow him back through the book and through the Bible. He is "the ancient serpent" (20:2), the one who deceived the woman in the garden (Genesis 3:13). He stood before the Woman to devour her child (12:4). He was thrown out of heaven (12:9), pursued her (12:13), made war on her children (12:17), and raised up the beast and the false prophet (13:1, 11). He was bound (20:2). Here he is ended.

The first promise in Scripture was made to him, as a sentence: "I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; They will strike at your head, while you strike at their heel" (Genesis 3:15). St. Paul wrote to the Romans, "the God of peace will quickly crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20). This verse is that promise kept. Scripture

Nothing is said of his being heard, or answering. In Genesis God questions the man and the woman, and does not question the serpent. So here.

What follows

With the deceiver gone, John sees the judgment: "a large white throne," the dead "great and lowly" standing before it, the scrolls and the book of life (20:11–15). Then the last enemy: "Death and Hades were thrown into the pool of fire" (20:14), as St. Paul had said, "The last enemy to be destroyed is death" (1 Corinthians 15:26).

Of the judgment the Catechism says: "Only the Father knows the day and the hour… We shall know the ultimate meaning of the whole work of creation and of the entire economy of salvation… The Last Judgment will reveal that God's justice triumphs over all the injustices committed by his creatures and that God's love is stronger than death" (CCC 1040). Church teaching

On the pool of fire, the notes of the New American Bible call it a "symbol of God's punishment," and "the second death" is "the eternal death, when sinners will receive their final punishment." The Church teaches that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035). She has never taught that any particular human being is there.

Read with the Woman

Her enemy has been in the story since its third chapter. After this verse he is not mentioned again. When the next chapter opens, "the sea was no more" (21:1): the sea from which the beast rose, "home of the dragon," as the notes say. And a woman appears, a bride, with no dragon standing before her.

The Church sings of Mary in the words of Genesis: she is the Woman whose offspring strikes the serpent's head. This is where the blow is finished.

What the Fathers said

The Fathers cited here agree with this page on the main point: Gog and Magog are no single nation, and the city they surround is the Church. They differ on when the devil is loosed and on what the fire is. A Father or Doctor

Why is he loosed at all?

  • Oecumenius keeps to his own reckoning, in which the devil was restrained during the Lord's earthly life and loosed after the Ascension (see The dragon bound). His answer to the question holds on any reckoning. Athletes need an opponent, and without one the victories of the patriarchs, prophets, apostles, martyrs, pastors and ascetics would never have been seen. Against his will the devil serves as "an athletic trainer" for the human race. The slothful lose nothing by it, since they would sin without him. And had he been free during the Lord's ministry, no one would have been left to hear the teaching (1 John 3:8) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 20.4–8).
  • St. Andrew of Caesarea knows that reading and does not follow it. He also knows the opinion that the saints will rise first and enjoy a thousand years of plenty on this earth, and answers that the Church receives none of it. The just will be like the angels (Matthew 22:30), and the kingdom of God is not food and drink (Romans 14:17). The thousand years are the full harvest of the Gospel, as a thousand stands for fullness in Song of Songs 8:11–12. After it comes the man of lawlessness (2 Thessalonians 2:3, 12), of whom the Lord said that another would come in his own name and be received (John 5:43) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 20.7–8).
  • Apringius of Beja reads the release as the devil's final dissolution. When the saints have received their reward and all things are gathered under Christ (Colossians 1:17), he goes out into eternal perdition (Tractate on the Apocalypse 20.7).

Who are Gog and Magog?

  • Not a people on a map. St. Augustine says they are not some barbarian tribe in one corner of the earth, such as the Getae and Massagetae, whom some proposed because of the first letters of the names, or any nation outside the Roman Empire. John says they are in the four corners of the earth. He has been told that Gog means roof and Magog from the roof. The nations are the roof under which the devil is now shut, and he is the one who comes out from it when hidden hatred breaks into open persecution. This will be the last persecution, suffered by the worldwide city of Christ from the worldwide city of the devil (City of God 20.11). Apringius uses the same meanings for the pride that shelters the wicked, and adds that the battle is in one sense already going on, in the hostility of the wicked to good works.
  • Nations at the end. Oecumenius thinks they are peoples that do not yet exist, or exist under other names (Commentary on the Apocalypse 20.8–10). Andrew reports that some in his day took them for the Scythians of the far north, called Huns, and that others read Ezekiel's prophecy of the Assyrians under Sennacherib, of the enemies of those who rebuilt Jerusalem, or of the armies of Antiochus. He judges that it fits the last times best, since Ezekiel himself says Gog will come at the end of the years (Ezekiel 38:8). From the Hebrew he gives other meanings: Gog the one who gathers, Magog the one who is exalted (Commentary on the Apocalypse 20.7–8).

The camp of the saints and the beloved city. For Augustine this is not one place. It is the Church spread through all nations, hemmed in everywhere by her enemies and never giving up her camp, that is, her fight (City of God 20.11). St. Caesarius of Arles says the nations cannot be gathered into one city from the four corners of the earth: in every place each people rises against the Church. He connects this with the gathering at Armageddon (Revelation 16:16) (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 18). Apringius and Caesarius read "the height of the earth" in their Latin text, and take it for arrogance. Apringius hears Psalm 59:7 of those who prowl around the city. Andrew compares the enemy to wild animals coming out of their dens, and says they do not know that many angels encamp around those who fear God (Psalm 34:8). He adds that the antichrist will then seat himself in the temple of God (2 Thessalonians 2:4), whether that means the temple in Jerusalem or the Church.

The fire from heaven.

  • The constancy of the saints. Augustine does not take this fire for the last punishment, which begins only with the words "Depart from me" (Matthew 25:41). It is the firmness of the saints, given by God's grace, which torments their enemies because they cannot be won over (City of God 20.12).
  • The Holy Spirit, or their own sins. Caesarius offers two readings. Either they are consumed by the Church, that is, brought to faith by the fire of the Spirit and made part of her, or they are consumed by the fire of their sins and perish.
  • Real fire, or the coming of Christ. Andrew: either fire like that which fell on the companies sent against Elijah (2 Kings 1:9–12), or the glorious coming of Christ, who will slay them with the breath of his mouth (2 Thessalonians 2:8). He ends by urging the prayer the Lord taught, that we not be led into temptation (Commentary on the Apocalypse 20.9–10).

The devil's end. Oecumenius counts three figures in the book, the dragon, the beast from the sea and the false prophet, whom he takes for the antichrist. Now the dragon joins the other two, and the punishment is not for a thousand years but forever. Augustine asks why the Church has refused the opinion that the devil will at last be cleansed or pardoned, an opinion associated with Origen. It is not from any lack of feeling. The Lord's own sentence speaks of everlasting fire prepared for the devil and his angels (Matthew 25:41), and this verse says "forever and ever" (City of God 21.23).

The judgment that follows (20:11–15).

  • Heaven and earth fled. Tertullian sees here proof that what was made from nothing can return to nothing (Against Hermogenes 34). The others speak of change and not of destruction. Augustine: it is the outward form of this world that passes (1 Corinthians 7:31), and not its nature (City of God 20.14). Oecumenius quotes 2 Peter 3:10 and Psalm 102:26–27. Andrew cites St. Irenaeus and St. Methodius: creation, subjected to corruption for our sake, will be set free with us (Romans 8:20–21) and renewed (Psalm 104:30; Isaiah 65:17–18; 66:22).
  • The books and the book of life. For Augustine the books are the Scriptures of both Testaments, which show what God commanded. The other book is an act of divine power by which each person recalls all his deeds at once, so that conscience accuses or excuses (City of God 20.14). Apringius says the book of life is Christ himself, made manifest to every creature (Psalm 112:6). Oecumenius notes that there are many books and one book of life, because many walk the broad way and few find the narrow one (Matthew 7:13–14). Counting the "little scroll" of chapter 10, he finds three kinds of book: one for the most impious, the book of life for the most just, and the books for all who stand between. St. Bede adds that the wicked will read in the deeds of the just the good they chose not to do.
  • The sea, death and Hades gave up their dead. No one is beyond the reach of the resurrection, however the body perished (Apringius). Oecumenius answers those who think it impossible for scattered elements to be gathered again: you do not know the power of God (Matthew 22:29), who made all things from nothing. Oecumenius then reads the verse by the four elements of which bodies were thought to be made. The sea gives back what was water in us; death, which he takes for the earth, the dust of death (Psalm 22:16), gives back what was earth; and Hades, the unseen, gives back what was air and fire. We can sometimes separate wine from water, and the sun draws what is sweet out of the salt sea. It is easier for God, whose voice divides the flames of fire (Psalm 29:7), to give each body what belongs to it. Augustine takes the sea for this present world, which gives up those whom Christ finds still living. Andrew notes that death and Hades are not living beings: death is the separation of soul and body, and Hades is the unseen place of souls. The souls of the just are in the hand of God (Wisdom 3:1). Bede reports the reading of Tyconius: the dead of the sea are the nations Christ will find still living, and the dead of death and Hades are the nations already dead and buried. Bede himself takes "death" for the good souls, who suffered only the parting from the body, and "Hades" for the wicked.
  • The second death. Augustine calls it the death of the whole person, in which the soul lacks the life of God and yet gives the body feeling enough to suffer; it is the death the Lord warned of in Matthew 10:28 (City of God 13.2). Oecumenius and Andrew read the casting of death into the fire as the end of death itself (Hosea 13:14; 1 Corinthians 15:26), since God did not make death (Wisdom 1:13). Aphrahat, the Syriac Father, says it is the death rightly to be feared, and that the good servant looks forward to the dawn when he will receive his Lord's gifts (Demonstrations 8.19).
  • Not all alike. Oecumenius and Andrew both say that there are degrees of punishment, as there are many dwelling places with the Father for those who are saved.
  • Written in the book. Augustine and Bede explain that the book of life is not a reminder for God. It is his unfailing foreknowledge of those to whom eternal life will be given (City of God 20.15).
  • Lactantius (died about 325) describes the same end: the world renewed, the just made like the angels and always in the sight of God, the second and public resurrection of all, and the devil condemned with his ministers (Divine Institutes 7.26; the volume labels it the Epitome). He places it after a literal thousand years, the expectation the Church did not keep; see The dragon bound.

A note on the word "predestined." One Father on this passage uses a word that is often misunderstood. St. Fulgentius of Ruspe (died about 533), a follower of Augustine, says that the wicked are predestined to the second death. He does not mean that God destines anyone to sin, or creates anyone in order to condemn him. He says the opposite in the same place. There are two deaths. The first is the death of the soul by sin, and to this no one is predestined: a person brings it on himself, as desire conceives and gives birth to sin, and sin when it is full-grown brings forth death (James 1:15). The second death is the sentence of the just Judge on that sin. What God has decreed from eternity is the justice, that sin persisted in to the end will be punished. He has not decreed the sin. The first death, says Fulgentius, is the cause of the second, and the second is the punishment of the first (Letter to Monimus 1.27).

The Church has guarded this distinction. In 529 the Council of Orange, at which St. Caesarius of Arles presided, condemned the opinion that anyone is predestined to evil by the power of God. The Catechism teaches: "God predestines no one to go to hell; for this, a willful turning away from God (a mortal sin) is necessary, and persistence in it until the end" (CCC 1037). And of God's plan as a whole: "To God, all moments of time are present in their immediacy. When therefore he establishes his eternal plan of 'predestination,' he includes in it each person's free response to his grace" (CCC 600). God wills everyone to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4). So predestination, in the Church's sense, is God's plan to bring us to glory by grace, a plan that includes our free answer. It is never a decree that anyone must sin or be lost. The book of life in this passage says the same in a picture: God knows his own, and that knowledge forces no one. Church teaching

Where readers differ

  • One battle or two. Whether this is the same gathering as 16:14–16 and 19:19, seen again from another side, or a later one. Those who read chapter 20 as a retelling of the Church's whole age take them as one. Both passages draw on Ezekiel's Gog. Among the Fathers, Caesarius connects this gathering with Armageddon. Disputed
  • How literal. The notes of the New American Bible call it "a description of the symbolic battle to take place when Satan is released at the end of time." The Church teaches a real final trial (CCC 675) without saying what outward form it will take.
  • "The beloved city." The notes identify it as Jerusalem and refer to Mount Zion, the gathering-place of "the Israel of faith." Most Catholic commentators read it as the Church throughout the world, as St. Augustine did.
  • When. No one knows, and the Church forbids no hope and sets no date (CCC 673, 1040).

What it asks of us

To expect that the Church may look defeated at the end, and not to conclude from this that God has abandoned her. The camp of the holy ones is small and surrounded when the fire falls.

And to place hope where the passage places it. Not in the Church's numbers, and not in any power that offers to defend her. "Fire came down from heaven." The same hope is in every Our Father: "deliver us from evil." The Catechism ends its commentary on that petition with this very scene: "Therefore the Spirit and the Church pray: 'Come, Lord Jesus,' since his coming will deliver us from the Evil One" (CCC 2853).
