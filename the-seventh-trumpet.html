Revelation 11:15–18

The Seventh Trumpet

The last trumpet sounds, and heaven announces that the kingdom of the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Anointed. Then the temple opens, and the ark is seen.

Home › The Study › The seventh trumpet

Draft · awaiting Fr. John's review

The text
"Then the seventh angel blew his trumpet. There were loud voices in heaven, saying, 'The kingdom of the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Anointed, and he will reign forever and ever.' The twenty-four elders… worshiped God and said: 'We give thanks to you, Lord God almighty, who are and who were. For you have assumed your great power and have established your reign. The nations raged, but your wrath has come, and the time for the dead to be judged, and to recompense your servants, the prophets, and the holy ones and those who fear your name, the small and the great alike, and to destroy those who destroy the earth'" (Revelation 11:15–18).
In one sentence
Heaven announces the end of the story before chapter 12 tells it: the kingdom belongs to God and his Christ, and everything the dragon does afterward happens inside that fact.
Authority
Scripture

Where this stands

An angel had sworn that "at the time when you hear the seventh angel blow his trumpet, the mysterious plan of God shall be fulfilled, as he promised to his servants the prophets" (10:7). Now it sounds. What follows is not another plague. It is a hymn.

And at once the scene changes. "Then God's temple in heaven was opened, and the ark of his covenant could be seen" (11:19), and the Woman appears (12:1). The seventh trumpet is the doorway to the Woman's chapters. The notes of the New American Bible: "The seventh trumpet proclaims the coming of God's reign after the victory over diabolical powers" (note on 11:15–19).

Many readers, ancient and modern, hold that Revelation tells the same history more than once, from different sides. On that reading the seventh trumpet reaches the end, and chapter 12 goes back to the beginning, to the birth of the Messiah, and tells the story again from the inside. St. Victorinus taught this way of reading the book. The Church has not prescribed one account of its structure. A Father or Doctor

The words

  • The kingdom of the world. One kingdom, in the singular. The world's many powers are treated as a single dominion, and it changes hands.
  • Our Lord and his Anointed. The Father and his Christ; "Anointed" is the word "Messiah."
  • He will reign. Singular: the Father and the Son reign as one.
  • Who are and who were. At the opening of the book God is "the one who is and who was and who is to come" (1:8). Here the third phrase is missing. Heaven no longer says "who is to come," because in this hymn he has come.
  • You have assumed your great power. God's power was always his. What is new is that he now exercises it openly.

Scripture

The psalm behind the hymn

The hymn is Psalm 2 sung at its fulfillment.

Psalm 2 Revelation 11
"Why do the nations protest and the peoples conspire in vain?" (2:1) "The nations raged" (11:18)
"Against the LORD and against his anointed one" (2:2) "Our Lord and his Anointed" (11:15)
"I myself have installed my king on Zion" (2:6) "You have… established your reign" (11:17)
"I will give you the nations as your inheritance" (2:8) "The kingdom of the world now belongs to our Lord" (11:15)

This is the same psalm that gives chapter 12 its male child, "destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod" (12:5; Psalm 2:9), and chapter 14 its Lamb on Mount Zion (14:1; Psalm 2:6). The psalm binds the whole section together; see The male child.

Daniel had seen the same transfer: "the kingship and dominion and majesty of all the kingdoms under the heavens shall be given to the people of the holy ones of the Most High" (Daniel 7:27). Scripture

Already, and not yet

How can heaven sing that the kingdom has come, when the dragon and the beasts are still to appear?

The Church's answer is that the kingdom has come and is still coming. The Catechism: "The Kingdom of God lies ahead of us. It is brought near in the Word incarnate, it is proclaimed throughout the whole Gospel, and it has come in Christ's death and Resurrection. The Kingdom of God has been coming since the Last Supper and, in the Eucharist, it is in our midst. The kingdom will come in glory when Christ hands it over to his Father" (CCC 2816). And of the time in between: Christ's reign "is still under attack by the evil powers, even though they have been defeated definitively by Christ's Passover" (CCC 671). Church teaching

Heaven sings from the standpoint of the end, where the outcome is not in doubt. Chapter 12 will say the same thing in a different key: "Now have salvation and power come, and the kingdom of our God and the authority of his Anointed" (12:10). The two hymns frame the vision of the Woman and the dragon. Before the war is shown and after it, heaven declares who has won.

When we pray "thy kingdom come," we ask for what this hymn celebrates: the petition "refers primarily to the final coming of the reign of God through Christ's return" (CCC 2818). Church teaching

What the hymn says will happen

Four things, in one sentence (11:18).

  • "Your wrath has come." The raging of the nations is answered. God's wrath in Scripture is not a passion; it is his settled opposition to evil, made effective.
  • "The time for the dead to be judged." "The Last Judgment will come when Christ returns in glory. Only the Father knows the day and the hour" (CCC 1040).
  • "To recompense your servants." The prophets, the holy ones, and "those who fear your name, the small and the great alike." No one who feared God is too small to be remembered.
  • "To destroy those who destroy the earth." Judgment falls on destroyers. The phrase recalls the flood, when "the earth was corrupt" and God resolved to destroy those who had corrupted it (Genesis 6:11–13). In Revelation the great destroyer is named: the angel of the abyss, "Abaddon," "Apollyon," the Destroyer (9:11).

The order is worth noticing. Reward is named beside judgment, and it is named for the servants first.

Then the temple opens

The hymn is followed by the verse with which this study began: the sanctuary in heaven is opened and the ark is seen, with lightning, thunder, an earthquake and hail (11:19). The seventh in each series of seven ends this way (8:5; 16:18–21).

At the beginning of the chapter John measured a sanctuary on earth that was about to be trampled (11:1–2). At the end he sees the sanctuary in heaven standing open. Between the two lies the witness, death and rising of God's prophets. Continue with The ark in heaven.

Where readers differ

  • Is this the end? Some read the seventh trumpet as the very end of history, told here in brief and then retold in later chapters. Others read it as the announcement of a final phase, the "third woe" (11:14), which the following chapters unfold in order. The first reading goes with the view that Revelation repeats; the second with the view that it runs in sequence. The Church has not chosen. Disputed
  • "The last trumpet." St. Paul speaks of the dead being raised "at the last trumpet" (1 Corinthians 15:52). Many readers connect the two. The connection is natural; it has not been defined.
  • "Those who destroy the earth." Corruption by sin and idolatry, as in Genesis 6; violence against God's people; and, in the judgment of some modern readers, the ruin of creation itself. The first is the sense nearest the text.

What it asks of us

To learn to sing before we see. The elders give thanks for a reign that, on earth, is still contested. This is what the Church does at every Mass, when she joins the song of heaven before the world has changed at all.

The hymn also sets the measure for everything that follows in this study. A reader will shortly meet a dragon, a war, a flood, two beasts and a mark. All of it takes place after this announcement and inside it. "The kingdom of the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Anointed, and he will reign forever and ever."
