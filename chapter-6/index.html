REVELATION 6:01–08

Come forward.

Come forward.

01John watches as the Lamb breaks the first of the seven seals, and one of the four living creatures cries out with a voice like thunder, “Come forward.”

02A white horse appears. Its rider carries a bow and is given a crown, and he rides out conquering and to conquer.

03When the Lamb breaks the second seal, the second living creature cries, “Come forward.”

04A red horse comes out. Its rider is allowed to take peace from the earth, so that people slaughter one another, and he is given a great sword.

05When the Lamb breaks the third seal, the third living creature cries, “Come forward.” A black horse appears, and its rider holds a pair of scales.

06A voice from among the living creatures says, “A ration of wheat for a day’s pay, three rations of barley for a day’s pay, but do not harm the oil and the wine.”

07When the Lamb breaks the fourth seal, the fourth living creature cries, “Come forward.”

08A pale green horse appears. Its rider is named Death, and Hades follows him. They are given power over a quarter of the earth, to kill by sword, famine, plague and wild beasts.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The Lamb who received the scroll in chapter 5 now opens it. The USCCB notes read chapter 6 as a symbolic description of the scroll’s contents. It begins the book’s three cycles of seven (seals, trumpets and bowls), each interrupted by scenes of God’s triumph and his people’s safety. Nothing is loosed on the earth except at the Lamb’s command. The imagery comes from the prophet Zechariah’s colored horses (Zech 1:8–10; 6:1–8). In the USCCB reading the riders are conquest, war, famine and Death with Hades following. The bow may allude to the Parthians, expert archers on Rome’s eastern border who defeated a Roman army in A.D. 62. The black horse’s prices describe famine: a whole day’s wage buys one day’s ration of wheat. These are the sufferings that follow human violence in every age, as Jesus foretold: wars, famines and plagues (Luke 21:9–11). Some early Christian writers, such as Victorinus of Pettau, read the first rider instead as Christ or the Gospel going out to conquer, since Christ later rides a white horse (19:11). The Church has not settled the question; both readings agree that the Lamb, not the riders, is in control.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 6:09–11

How long?

How long?

09When the Lamb breaks the fifth seal, John sees beneath the altar the souls of those who had been slain for the word of God and for the witness they gave.

10They cry out in a loud voice, “How long, holy and true Master, before you judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?”

11Each of them is given a white robe, and they are told to rest a little longer, until the number of their fellow servants and brothers who are to be killed as they were is complete.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The fifth seal shifts from the earth to heaven. The souls of the martyrs lie beneath the altar like the blood of sacrifice poured at the base of the temple altar. Their cry, “How long?”, echoes the psalms of lament (Ps 13:2; 79:5–10). They receive white robes, the sign of victory, and are asked to wait. The Church remembers this vision in her ancient custom of placing relics of martyrs and saints beneath the altar where the Eucharist is offered.

REVELATION 6:12–17

Who can stand?

Who can stand?

12When the Lamb breaks the sixth seal, there is a great earthquake. The sun turns black as sackcloth, and the whole moon becomes like blood.

13The stars of the sky fall to the earth like unripe figs shaken loose by a strong wind.

14The sky splits apart like a scroll rolling up, and every mountain and island is moved from its place.

15Kings, nobles, commanders, the rich and the powerful, slave and free alike, hide in caves and among the rocks of the mountains.

16They call to the mountains and rocks, “Fall on us and hide us from the face of the one seated on the throne and from the wrath of the Lamb,

17for the great day of their wrath has come, and who can stand?”

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The USCCB notes call these cosmic signs “symbolic rather than literal”: the traditional language of the day of the Lord used by the prophets and by Jesus himself (Joel 3:3–4; Isa 34:4; Matt 24:29). Every rank of society, from kings to slaves, hides from “the wrath of the Lamb,” echoing Jesus’ words on the way to Calvary (Luke 23:30). The chapter ends with a question, “Who can stand?”, which chapter 7 will answer.

REVELATION 6:1–2

The white horse and its rider

A crowned archer rides out to conquer. The USCCB notes see a conquering power, perhaps the feared Parthian archers; some early writers saw Christ and the Gospel (compare 19:11). Either way, he goes out only when the Lamb opens the seal.

Read Zechariah 6
02

REVELATION 6:3–8

The red, black and pale green horses

War, famine and Death follow one another, as they did in the ancient world and still do. Their power is limited to “a quarter of the earth”: real, terrible, but not unlimited.

Read Luke 21
03

REVELATION 6:9

The souls beneath the altar

The martyrs’ lives were poured out like sacrificial blood at the foot of the altar. Christ’s sacrifice and theirs are joined, which is why the Church places the relics of saints beneath her altars.

Read Revelation 6
04

REVELATION 6:11

The white robe

White signals victory and resurrection (3:5; 7:14). The martyrs appear defeated on earth, yet heaven already clothes them as victors.

Read Revelation 7
05

REVELATION 6:16–17

The wrath of the Lamb

A startling phrase: the gentle Lamb is also the judge. His “wrath” is not rage but the truth of God’s justice that no one can hide from. Those washed in his blood will be able to stand (7:14).

Read Isaiah 2
READ WITH CARE

The four horsemen and the sixth seal are often matched to particular wars, pandemics or news events as proof that the end is near. The USCCB notes call these signs “symbolic rather than literal,” describing troubles that recur in every age. Jesus himself warned against such date-setting: “it will not immediately be the end” (Luke 21:9).

WHERE IT FITS

The Lamb breaks the seals.

The Lamb who took the scroll in chapter 5 now breaks its seals. Chapter 6 opens six of the seven; before the seventh, chapter 7 pauses to answer the question “Who can stand?” with the sealing of God’s servants and a great multitude in white. The seventh seal (8:1) then opens into the seven trumpets. The martyrs’ cry, “How long?”, is finally answered in the judgment of chapters 19–20.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or go on to the next chapter and come back to it.

The Fathers read the seals in two main ways. St. Victorinus and St. Andrew of Caesarea see the history of the Church from the apostles to the end. Oecumenius sees the saving works of Christ, from his birth to his cross. Tyconius and those who follow him see the Church and her enemies in every age. The readings below also draw on Primasius, St. Caesarius of Arles and St. Bede, and for the fifth seal on Tertullian, St. Cyprian, St. Fulgentius and Cassiodorus.

01

REVELATION 6:1–2

The first seal: a white horse

What the seals are.

  • St. Victorinus: to break the seals is to disclose the prophecies of the Old Testament and to foretell what will happen at the end. The announcement runs its full course only when all the seals are open. His text reads “Come and see”: “come” is said to one invited to faith, and “see” to one who has not yet seen (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.1).
  • Oecumenius: the sealed scroll is our estrangement from God, our mouths closed from any plea. Each seal loosed is a work the Lord did for our salvation and against our spiritual enemies. The first is his birth in the flesh, which began our return from the exile caused by Adam’s sin and made human birth holy (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.1–4).
  • Primasius: the one living creature that speaks is the whole Church, preaching with a great voice and inviting to faith: “Let anyone who thirsts come to me and drink” (John 7:37); look to him and be radiant (Psalm 34:6); “Awake, O sleeper, and arise from the dead, and Christ will give you light” (Ephesians 5:14) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.1).
  • St. Andrew of Caesarea: the first living creature, the lion, is the royal courage of the apostles against the demons (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.1).

The white horse and its rider.

  • Victorinus: this is what happened first. After the Lord ascended he sent the Holy Spirit, whose words, through preachers, went into human hearts like arrows and conquered unbelief. The white horse is the word of preaching sent into the world, as the Lord said: this Gospel will be preached to all nations, and then the end will come (Matthew 24:14) (6.1).
  • Oecumenius: the white horse is the Gospel, and it goes out to bring the crown to Christ, who had begun to conquer the devil and would conquer him completely.
  • Primasius, St. Caesarius and St. Bede, probably following Tyconius: the white horse is the Church, made whiter than snow by grace, and its rider is Christ. The bow is the preaching of the word, which pierces hearts so that they bear the fruit of faith (Psalm 45:6). Caesarius finds this horse promised in Zechariah (Zechariah 10:3–4), and sees in the rider the Holy Spirit as well (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 5).
  • Andrew: the generation of the apostles, who drew the Gospel like a bow against the demons and brought to Christ those wounded by saving arrows. “Conquering and to conquer” names two victories: the conversion of the nations, and their own willing death for it (6.2).
  • Bede: the rider has a bow because he came to make war on the powers of the air, and a crown because by dying he overthrew the reign of death (Hebrews 2:9) (On the Tabernacle 1.25.11).
02

REVELATION 6:3–4

The second seal: a red horse

Oecumenius: the second work of Christ is his temptation and his victory over the tempter. The one who boasted that he would set his throne above the clouds (Isaiah 14:13–14) was sent away like a slave by a man: “Get away, Satan!” (Matthew 4:10). The sword is given to cut apart the agreement in evil and idolatry among those who dwell on earth, for the Lord came to bring not peace but a sword (Matthew 10:34–35) (6.1–4).

Victorinus: the wars to come, as the Gospel says: nation will rise against nation (Luke 21:10–11) (6.2).

Tyconius and Caesarius: the red horse is an evil people with the devil for its rider, red with the blood of others, and it comes out against the victorious Church. Zechariah saw a red horse of the Lord (Zechariah 1:8); that one is red with his own blood. The peace taken away is the peace of the earth. The Church keeps the peace Christ left her, which passes all understanding (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.4; Homily 5).

Primasius: after joy at the Church’s victory comes a warning. The Church is told beforehand of the evil she will face (6.3).

Andrew: the second living creature is the calf, for the sacrifices of the martyrs. This seal is the age after the apostles, of martyrs and teachers. As the preaching spreads, the peace of the world is taken away, and the martyrs are offered on the heavenly altar. The horse is red for their blood or for their burning zeal. That the rider “was given” power shows God’s wise permission, by which his servants are tested (6.3–4).

03

REVELATION 6:5–6

The third seal: a black horse

The horse and the scales.

  • Victorinus: famine, as the Lord foretold (Matthew 24:7), and above all the great famine in the time of the Antichrist (6.2).
  • Oecumenius: the third mercy of Christ is his teaching and his miracles, by which we learned who is truly God and stopped honouring what our hands had made. As yeast draws the flour, his teaching drew people to itself. The horse is black for the devil’s shame and grief; the scales are the just judgment the Lord gave in our favour (Psalm 9:5) (6.5–8).
  • Caesarius: an evil people who work with the devil. They pretend to hold the scales of justice, and so deceive many (Homily 5).
  • Bede: the company of false brothers, who hold the balance of a right confession and harm their fellows by works of darkness. “Do not damage” shows that someone is doing damage (Explanation of the Apocalypse 6.5–6).
  • Andrew: the third living creature is the man, and the seal shows human beings falling through the misuse of free will. The black horse is the grief of those who fell away from Christ under torture; the scales test those who fell through inconstancy, vainglory or weakness of body (6.5–6).

Wheat and barley.

  • Tyconius: the wheat and barley are the Church, her great and her least, or her leaders and her people. One measure and three cost the same: though one may exceed another in merit, all have been redeemed by one price. The three measures recall the leaven hidden in three measures of flour (Matthew 13:33) (6.6).
  • Primasius: the one denarius is eternal life, common to all though there are many dwelling places (John 14:2). One may receive a greater reward than another, yet where each has immortality all share one eternity (6.6).
  • Bede: take care not to scandalize by bad example a brother for whom Christ died, who bears the signs of the sacred blood and the chrism. Whether his merit is great or small, he was redeemed at the same price (6.5–6).
  • Oecumenius: the wheat is the preaching of the Gospel, food for the mature; the barley is the teaching of the law of Moses. The seal warns of a time when both are scarce, “a famine… for hearing the word of the Lord” (Amos 8:11).
  • Andrew: the wheat is those who contended lawfully and kept the divine image; the barley is those who bowed to their persecutors out of cowardice and later washed the image clean with tears (6.5–6).

“Do not damage the olive oil or the wine.” Primasius and Caesarius: the wine is the Blood of Christ and the oil the anointing with chrism; the power of the sacraments is not to be dishonoured. Oecumenius: leave alone those who despise the teaching, for there is still mercy for them, and hope that the preaching of the Son will gladden their hearts like wine (Psalm 104:15). Andrew: the wine and oil are the healing Christ gives through repentance, as to the man who fell among robbers. Let us bring to brothers who have fallen the wine of consolation mixed with the oil of compassion, so that what is lame may be healed (Hebrews 12:13).

04

REVELATION 6:7–8

The fourth seal: a pale horse

Oecumenius: with this seal the undoing of Adam’s sin begins. It is the scourging of Christ. We were condemned through pleasure and are healed by its opposite: disobedience by obedience, pleasure by pain, the hands that reached for the forbidden tree by the hands stretched out on the cross. The horse is pale, the colour of gall, for wrath. He reads Death and Hades as sent against the demons, to end their tyranny. Only a fourth is struck, because at this point in the vision the saving Passion is not yet complete. The “beasts of the earth” are the demons’ own passions of pride and vainglory, which devour them as they are driven from their rule over us (6.5–8).

Victorinus: the Lord foretold these things among other disasters. “Hades accompanied him” means the devouring of the souls of many of the impious (6.3).

Andrew: the fourth living creature is the eagle, whose swift descent on its prey suggests the plagues that come from above, to vindicate the devout and punish the impious unless they turn back. It has happened before. Eusebius tells how, under the emperor Maximinus, famine and plague killed more than could be buried, and how the Christians buried the dead and by their kindness led many to the truth. In the war with the Armenians at that time so many fell by the sword that the dead lay unburied (Ecclesiastical History 9.8). The like has been seen in his own day (6.7–8).

Tyconius: there are two parts in the world, God’s and the devil’s. The devil’s has three divisions, inside the Church and outside: the pagans, false brothers, and those cut off by open error or schism. These make war on the one Church. She is called “three fourths” because she believes in the Trinity, or because she has three callings, the virgins, the widows and the married, built together on the foundation of the apostles and prophets with Christ as the cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20). The three horses that follow the white one are these three enemies, with sword, famine and death. Whether they are meant literally or spiritually may be left open: there is a sword against the body and one against the soul, a famine of bread and one of the word. Through it all the full number of the elect is kept: “none of them was lost” (John 17:12) (6.8).

Caesarius: the pale horse is evil people who never stop stirring up persecutions. The three horses are one, and their rider is the devil, who is death. They are war, famine and pestilence, which the Lord foretold in the Gospel. They have already happened, and will happen more as the day of judgment draws near (Homily 5).

Bede: heretics who dress as Catholics and carry death within them, drawing the lost after them. He points to the madness of Arius, which rose in Alexandria and reached as far as the ocean of Gaul, pursuing the faithful with a famine of God’s word and also with the sword (6.8).

05

REVELATION 6:9–11

The fifth seal: the souls under the altar

Under the altar.

  • Tertullian argues from this verse that paradise before the resurrection holds only the martyrs, who die as Christ died: “The only key that unlocks the gates of paradise is your own blood” (On the Soul 55.4–5).
  • St. Victorinus: the law had two altars, a golden one within and a bronze one outside. The golden altar is heaven, to which our prayers rise, and which the high priest approached once a year as a sign that Christ would suffer once for all. The bronze altar is the earth; beneath it he places the abode of the dead, where the saints rest, away from pain and fire (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.4).
  • A note on Tertullian and Victorinus. Both write before the Church had spoken clearly on the state of the dead. She teaches that the souls of all who die in God’s grace and are fully purified, and not the martyrs only, see God face to face at once, before the resurrection of the body (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1023).
  • Oecumenius: the fifth work of Christ is the chains and blows he bore before and under Pilate. His wounds healed ours, the wounds of the man who went down from Jerusalem to Jericho (Luke 10:30), and loosed the cords of sin (Psalm 119:61). When the holy ones who had suffered for God’s word under the old covenant saw their Master scourged, they cried out. Martyrs do not die for themselves alone; their courage encourages others, and by the blood of the saints the knowledge of God is built up (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.9–11).
  • St. Bede: after the Church’s afflictions John shows the glory of the souls. “Under the altar” is the hidden place of eternal praise. The golden altar within does not receive flesh and blood, only incense. Those who now offer their bodies as a living sacrifice will, once the bonds of the body are broken, offer the sacrifice of praise (Explanation of the Apocalypse 6.9).

“How long, holy and true master?” The Fathers agree that the martyrs do not ask out of hatred.

  • Tertullian: this is the prayer of every Christian: “your kingdom come.” We long to reign and no longer be slaves, and would ask for it even if we had not been told to (On Prayer 5.1–4).
  • Primasius: it is not to be taken in a fleshly way, as if they burned with resentment; we are to love our enemies. They pray against the kingdom of sin, and for the kingdom for which we say, “your kingdom come” (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.10).
  • Cassiodorus: the vengeance they ask aims at the conversion of the enemy. Those who are chastised in this world escape eternal ruin. Holy people do not seek a cruel revenge; they keep the command to pray for enemies, and the Lord himself said on the cross, “Father, forgive them” (Luke 23:34) (Exposition of the Psalms 79.10).
  • Oecumenius: their plea is against the demons, who lead people to destruction, and not against their own kind.
  • Bede: they pray from love of justice, standing near the Judge and agreeing with him, that the day may come when the kingdom of sin is destroyed and their bodies rise. We too, who are told to pray for our enemies, still say, “your kingdom come” (6.10).

The white robe, and “a little while longer.”

  • St. Cyprian cites the verse to those who wanted Christians who fell under persecution restored at once on the word of the martyrs. Even the martyrs under the altar are told to be patient. No one can make himself good by pardoning sins at random. What the martyrs ask is to be done by the priest of God, if it is just and lawful and not against the Lord (The Lapsed 18).
  • St. Fulgentius of Ruspe: when the time of judgment comes, conversion will no longer help the evildoer. That time is put off by God’s patience, so that the number of the saints may be filled (On the Forgiveness of Sins 2.5.2–3).
  • Victorinus: they await the vindication of their bodies at the end of time, and as a consolation they receive white robes, the gift of the Holy Spirit.
  • Oecumenius: the white robe shows that they were cleansed by their own blood. They wait so that others equal to them in courage may not lose the martyr’s crown.
  • Primasius: they learned that the fountain of grace will flow to the end of the world and many more will join them. The white robe is the inexpressible joy this gave them; by the perfection of charity they are content to wait for their brothers (6.11).
  • St. Andrew of Caesarea: they pray for the consummation of the world and are asked to be patient, for “without us they should not be made perfect” (Hebrews 11:40). The robes are the brightness of their virtues. They rest in the bosom of Abraham, rejoicing in what they already see in spirit (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.11).
  • Bede: each soul now has one white robe, its own blessedness. They will have two when the number of their brothers is complete and they receive their immortal bodies (Homilies on the Gospels 2.12).
06

REVELATION 6:12–17

The sixth seal: the great day

The cross. Oecumenius: the sixth seal is the cross and death of the Lord, followed by his Resurrection and Ascension, which complete our salvation and open “the new and living way” (Hebrews 10:20) for the living and for those who had gone before, since he preached also to the spirits in prison (1 Peter 3:19). The vision shows the signs of the crucifixion: the earthquake, the darkened sun, and the moon, which was full at Passover. Joel had foretold it (Joel 3:4). The sky is the angelic powers, shaken at the abuse of their Master. The kings and the mighty who hide are the demons, in flight from the punishment of Christ (Matthew 8:29) (6.12–17).

The last persecution. Tyconius: the sixth seal is the sixth age of the world, at whose end the last persecution comes and shakes the whole earth. The sun and moon are Christ and the Church. The sun goes dark when the power of Christ is hidden, or teaching is obscured for a time, or help is delayed while the impious attack the saints, so that even the elect might be deceived, if that were possible (Matthew 24:24). The moon turns to blood because the Church will shed her own blood more than before (Commentary on the Apocalypse 6.12). Bede says the same, and notes that John writes the “whole” moon because the last upheaval will be over all the earth (6.12).

Before the false Christ. Andrew: the vision passes from the age of the persecutions to the time just before the coming of the false Christ, when there will be tribulations such as have never been. In Scripture an earthquake often means a change of things (Hebrews 12:26–27). The darkness of sun and moon is the darkness that falls on those overtaken by the wrath of God (6.12–13).

The stars and the fig tree. Tyconius: the stars are those in the Church who for a time seemed to stand out among the elect and who fall when sharp persecution comes (Matthew 24:21–22). The tree is the Church; the unripe figs are people shaken loose by the winds of persecution (6.13). Andrew: they are those who think themselves lights of the world and are crushed by the events of that time. The devil’s wind shakes down fruit not yet ripened by the heat of trials or sweetened by grace. Scripture uses the fig in a good sense and a bad (Jeremiah 24:1–5; Matthew 21:19–20). Whether these things will also be seen with the eyes when Christ comes in glory, he alone knows.

The sky like a scroll.

  • Tyconius: the sky is the Church. She withdraws and prudently avoids persecution, hidden from those outside and fully known to her own. She is careful with the stranger, as St. John wrote: “do not trust every spirit but test the spirits” (1 John 4:1) (6.14).
  • Andrew: either the time of Christ’s second coming is unknown, or the heavenly powers grieve for those who fall from the faith. It also shows that heaven does not cease to exist. It is changed into something better, as St. Irenaeus says: the substance of creation is not destroyed, but “the world in its present form is passing away” (1 Corinthians 7:31). To unroll a scroll does not destroy it; it shows what is written inside (6.14).

Mountains and islands. Tyconius: these too are the Church, a city set on a mountain (Matthew 5:14). Persecution moves her from her place: the good by a prudent flight, the evil by a will turned to something worse (Revelation 2:5). Andrew: the Lord foretold these things to the disciples. In part they came to pass in the siege under Vespasian and Titus, as Josephus tells. At the end they will come on the whole world: the great, whether leaders in the Church or holders of worldly power, and the churches of the faithful will flee from place to place because of the false Christ (6.14–17). Oecumenius reads the mountains as the ranks of proud demons.

“Fall on us and hide us.”

  • St. Caesarius of Arles: the good in all the world will take refuge in the Church, and under her protection be made firm for eternal life (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 5).
  • Bede: the mountains are the highest in the Church. The weak will look to their example, counsel, encouragement and prayers, as if begging the mountains to fall on them in compassion; for “the high mountains are for wild goats” (Psalm 104:18). The prayer is that the Lord, when he comes, may find us steadfast in faith, our sins covered by the intercession of the saints and the mercy of God. And who can stand? The one who has taken care now to be watchful, firm in faith and brave in living. If the earthquake is taken of the day of judgment itself, it is no wonder that the kings of the earth then look to the holy mountains for refuge, as the rich man in purple looked to Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) (6.16–17).
  • Andrew: those who hold power on earth and own nothing in heaven will beg to be hidden from the anger that comes either in the plagues of the Antichrist’s time or in the judgment after the resurrection. It will consume those who built on the foundation of faith with wood and straw.
