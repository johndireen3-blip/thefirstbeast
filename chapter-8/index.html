REVELATION 8:01–02

Silence in heaven.

Silence in heaven.

01When the Lamb breaks the seventh seal, there is silence in heaven for about half an hour.

02John sees the seven angels who stand before God, and they are given seven trumpets.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The seventh seal does not bring the end. It opens into a new cycle of seven: the trumpets. The USCCB notes describe the pattern: silence, then a “minor liturgy” of incense framed by the seven angels, then four trumpet blasts that form a unit, just as the first four seals did. The half hour of silence echoes the prophet Zephaniah: “Silence in the presence of the Lord God! for near is the day of the Lord” (Zeph 1:7). Before God acts, heaven holds its breath.

REVELATION 8:03–06

The prayers of the saints rise.

The prayers of the saints rise.

03Another angel comes and stands at the altar with a gold censer. He is given a great deal of incense to offer with the prayers of all the holy ones on the gold altar before the throne.

04The smoke of the incense, together with the prayers of the holy ones, rises before God from the angel’s hand.

05Then the angel fills the censer with burning coals from the altar and hurls it down to the earth. There are peals of thunder, rumblings, lightning and an earthquake.

06The seven angels with the seven trumpets prepare to blow them.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

An angel offers incense “along with the prayers of all the holy ones” on the golden altar, the heavenly counterpart of the altar of incense in Jerusalem’s temple. The prayers of God’s people on earth are the hinge of the chapter: they rise to God, and the fire that comes down in answer sets the trumpets in motion. In the Roman Canon (Eucharistic Prayer I), the priest asks God to command that the gifts “be borne by the hands of your holy Angel to your altar on high,” the same picture of the Church’s worship rising to heaven.

REVELATION 8:07–13

A third, not all.

A third, not all.

07The first angel blows his trumpet, and hail and fire mixed with blood are hurled to the earth. A third of the land is burned, a third of the trees, and all the green grass.

08The second angel blows his trumpet, and something like a great burning mountain is thrown into the sea. A third of the sea turns to blood,

09a third of the creatures in the sea die, and a third of the ships are wrecked.

10The third angel blows his trumpet, and a great star, burning like a torch, falls from the sky onto a third of the rivers and springs.

11The star is called Wormwood. A third of the waters turn bitter, and many people die from the water.

12The fourth angel blows his trumpet, and a third of the sun, moon and stars are struck, so that a third of the day and a third of the night are without light.

13Then John sees an eagle flying high overhead and hears it cry in a loud voice, “Woe! Woe! Woe to those who live on the earth, because of the trumpet blasts the three angels are about to blow!”

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The first four trumpets are modeled on the plagues of Egypt: hail and fire (Exod 9:23–24), water turned to blood (Exod 7:20–21) and darkness (Exod 10:21–23). As the plagues were meant to bring Pharaoh to let God’s people go, these blasts call the world to repentance. Each strikes only “a third,” the traditional prophetic fraction (compare Ezek 5:12). The judgment is real but partial: there is still time to turn back. Wormwood is a very bitter plant. The prophet Jeremiah uses it as a symbol of the punishment that follows unfaithfulness (Jer 9:14; 23:15). The USCCB notes read it the same way here: bitterness poisoning the springs of life.

Before the Lord acts, all creation falls silent (Zeph 1:7; Hab 2:20). The Church keeps this kind of sacred silence in the liturgy, making room for God to speak.

REVELATION 8:2, 6

The seven trumpets

Trumpets announced battle, feasts and the coming of God (Exod 19:16; Josh 6). Here they sound alarms that call the world to wake up and repent.

Read Joshua 6
03

REVELATION 8:3–4

The golden censer

The angel offers incense with “the prayers of all the holy ones” (compare Tob 12:12; Ps 141:2). Our prayers are not lost: they rise before God’s throne and shape what happens next.

Read Psalm 141
04

REVELATION 8:7–12

A third of the earth

Each trumpet strikes only a third, echoing the plagues of Egypt. The disasters are limited: warnings meant to bring people to conversion while there is still time.

Read Exodus 9
05

REVELATION 8:10–11

Wormwood

A bitter plant that Jeremiah used as an image of the punishment that follows unfaithfulness (Jer 9:14; 23:15). Turning from God makes the very springs of life bitter.

Read Jeremiah 9
READ WITH CARE

“Wormwood” is often linked online to the Chernobyl disaster, to asteroids or to a hidden planet, as if John were predicting a specific modern event. The USCCB notes read Wormwood as a symbol of God’s judgment drawn from the prophet Jeremiah. Revelation’s trumpets warn every generation to repent; they are not a forecast to be matched to the headlines.

WHERE IT FITS

From seals to trumpets.

Chapter 8 links the seals to the trumpets: the seventh seal opens into seven trumpets, just as the seventh trumpet will later open into the seven bowls (chapters 15–16). The golden altar and the prayers of the holy ones continue the worship of chapters 4–5 and the martyrs’ cry beneath the altar in 6:9–10. The eagle’s three woes (8:13) announce the fifth, sixth and seventh trumpets, which follow in chapters 9 and 11.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or go on to the next chapter and come back to it.

The Fathers read the trumpets in three ways. Tyconius and the Latin writers who follow him, Primasius, St. Caesarius of Arles and St. Bede, hear in them the preaching of the Church in every age and see her struggle with false brothers and false teachers. Oecumenius reads them of the end: the coming of Christ, the renewal of creation and the grief of the lost. St. Andrew of Caesarea reads them of the trials that come before the end, and of the war the devil wages on souls now. St. Victorinus adds a rule for the whole book: the trumpets and the bowls tell the same things twice.

01

REVELATION 8:1

The seventh seal: silence in heaven

Tyconius: “in heaven” means in the Church. The silence for half an hour shows the beginning of the eternal rest (Commentary on the Apocalypse 8.1).

Oecumenius: the loosing of the seventh seal is the second coming of Christ and the giving of his rewards: to be called children of God, heirs of God and co-heirs with Christ, his brothers and friends, to reign with him and be glorified with him, and to receive “what eye has not seen, and ear has not heard” (1 Corinthians 2:9). Some are handed over to punishment, yet the aim of Christ and the purpose of his incarnation is that everyone should become an heir of his kingdom. There is silence because the King of creation is coming, and every angelic power falls silent, astounded at the greatness of his glory (Commentary on the Apocalypse 8.1–2).

St. Andrew of Caesarea: the number seven belongs to this age and to the sabbath rest of the saints, so the seventh seal signifies the dissolution of the earthly city. The silence shows the reverent good order of the angels, and also that the second coming of Christ is unknown even to them. The half hour is the shortness of the time: once the plagues have come and the things of the end are happening on earth, the kingdom of Christ will appear (Commentary on the Apocalypse 8.1–2).

St. Bede: it is believed that after the destruction of the Antichrist there will be a short rest in the Church. Daniel calls blessed the one who waits and reaches the 1,335 days (Daniel 12:12). St. Jerome explains that this is forty-five days beyond the 1,290, that is, beyond the three and a half years. Why there should be such a silence after the Antichrist’s death is known to God, unless the delay of the kingdom of the saints is a test of patience. Bede adds that the greatest afflictions of the Church are placed in the sixth period and her rest in the seventh, for the Lord was crucified on the sixth day and rested on the seventh, awaiting the resurrection (Explanation of the Apocalypse 8.1).

02

REVELATION 8:2

Seven angels and seven trumpets

Tyconius: the seven angels are again the Church, since seven stands for the whole. She has received a powerful trumpet of proclamation, by which every age comes to faith: “lift up your voice like a trumpet blast” (Isaiah 58:1) (8.2).

Oecumenius: the trumpets sound the signal that the King is arriving. The same trumpets will wake the dead, for the Lord will come down “with the trumpet of God” (1 Thessalonians 4:16), and “the trumpet will sound, the dead will be raised incorruptible” (1 Corinthians 15:52) (8.1–2).

03

REVELATION 8:3–4

The angel, the altar and the incense

Tyconius: John saw all this at one time. The seven received their trumpets as the other angel was coming: when Christ the Lord came, his Church received the power to preach. The angel is Christ himself. He came over the altar, that is, over the Church, which is taken up whole as the body of his priesthood, “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation” (1 Peter 2:9). The golden censer is his immaculate body, conceived by the overshadowing of the Holy Spirit, which he offered to God as a sweet-smelling sacrifice for the redemption of the world, and by which he cleansed the conscience of all from dead works. He receives the prayers of the saints and offers them, because through him the prayers of all can come to God and be pleasing to him (8.3).

Bede: he “stood before the altar,” that is, he appeared in the sight of the Church. He was himself made the censer from which God received the sweet savour and was made favourable to the world. Another version reads “upon the altar,” because on the altar of the cross he offered to the Father his own golden censer, his sinless body conceived by the Holy Spirit. In offering himself he made acceptable the sorrow of the hearts of the saints, which rises from the fire within and draws tears, as smoke does (8.3–4).

Oecumenius: the prayers of the saints are fragrant by nature, and are made more fragrant by the cooperation of the holy angels. The angels who govern us bring these prayers to Christ at his appearing like the first offerings of homage. The care of human beings was given to the angels by God so that they might make our prayers acceptable to him (8.3–6).

Andrew: what is shown to the saints in material form and colour, whether altar or censer, is in truth invisible and spiritual. The altar is Christ, on whom every holy and ministering power is founded and on whom the sacrifices of the martyrs are offered. It was prefigured by the altar shown to Moses on Mount Sinai with the tabernacle. The incense is the prayers of the saints. The altar is “before the throne,” that is, before the highest of the holy powers, who stand there through the abundance of divine love and wisdom in them (8.3).

04

REVELATION 8:5

Fire cast on the earth

Tyconius: the Lord received his body, the Church, and filled her with fire from the altar to accomplish the Father’s will. This is the power of loosing and binding: “You make the winds your messengers; flaming fire, your ministers” (Psalm 104:4). In this she received all power in heaven and on earth, and she completes the sacrifice of God: first the Lord offering himself, and then the saints presenting their own bodies as a living and holy sacrifice (Romans 12:1). The fire is cast on the earth because through the preaching of the Church the knowledge of the coming judgment reaches the world (Zechariah 12:6). All that follows is spiritual. The voices are warnings: “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand!” (Matthew 3:2). The thunder is the proclamation of the Christian faith. The lightning is the virtues of those who have been made whole. The earthquakes are the persecutions foretold and suffered at various times (8.5).

St. Caesarius of Arles: Jesus received his body, the Church, and filled her with the fire of the Holy Spirit so that the Father’s will might be done. The voices, thunder, lightning and earthquakes are the spiritual proclamations and the virtues of the Church (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 6).

Oecumenius: an angel once cast this heavenly fire on Mount Sinai, and there were thunder and voices and trumpets and lightning, and smoke covered the mountain, because God had come (Exodus 19:16–19). As fearful wonders went before him then, so they will go before the glorious coming of the Lord (8.3–6).

Andrew: the prayers of the saints, offered by the ministering angel, cause the censer of fire to be poured out on the earth, as Ezekiel saw fire taken from among the cherubim (Ezekiel 10:6–7). Every high priest is like this angel. As a mediator between God and the people, he carries up their petitions and brings down God’s mercy, and he turns sinners to repentance by his word or by sterner correction (8.4–5).

Bede: the censer is rightly said to be filled with fire, for God “does not ration his gift of the Spirit” (John 3:34). This was fulfilled above all in the humanity of Christ, in whom “dwells the whole fullness of the deity bodily” (Colossians 2:9) (8.5).

05

REVELATION 8:6

The angels make ready

St. Victorinus: God sends these seven great archangels to strike the kingdom of the Antichrist, as the Lord said that the Son of Man will send his angels to gather his elect from the four winds (Matthew 24:31). Victorinus finds them in the seven shepherds of Micah, where he takes Asshur to be the Antichrist (Micah 5:4–5), in the keepers of the house in Ecclesiastes (12:3), and in the reapers of the parable of the weeds (Matthew 13:27–30). The trumpet is the word of power.

  • His rule for reading the book. The scenes are repeated in the bowls, but the events do not happen twice. What is decreed by God to happen is told twice. What the trumpets say briefly, the bowls say more fully. We should not press the order of what is said, for the sevenfold Holy Spirit, when he has gone through to the last time and the very end, returns to the same times and supplies what he had left unsaid. We are to seek the meaning more than the order. Together the trumpets and the bowls tell of the plagues sent on the world, the madness of the Antichrist, the blasphemies of the peoples, the hope of the kingdom of the saints, the ruin of cities, and the ruin of Babylon, “that is, of the city of Rome” (Commentary on the Apocalypse 8.1–2).

Tyconius: the Church, so often meant by the number seven, made herself ready for faithful preaching (8.6).

Oecumenius: the angels blow their trumpets to announce the coming of God, as trumpets were used to announce great occasions (8.3–6).

Bede: made zealous by the sevenfold Spirit, the Church prepares to preach faithfully and to cast down the pomp of the world with heavenly trumpets, as the walls of Jericho fell. The seven days of marching around those walls stand for the whole time of the Church (8.6).

06

REVELATION 8:7

The first trumpet: hail and fire mixed with blood

Tyconius: the fire and blood are the wrath of God, which devours the multitude of the impious (8.7).

  • Earth, trees and grass. The earth is everything earthly. The trees are those who sway through unfaithfulness, carried about by “every wind of teaching” (Ephesians 4:14), the “fruitless trees in late autumn” of St. Jude (Jude 12). The green grass is flesh fattened with luxury, for “All flesh is grass” (Isaiah 40:6).
  • The thirds. Earlier three parts were set against the one that is the Church (6:8). Here her opponents are two thirds: the false brothers mixed among the good within the Church, and those separated from her by pagan error, by heresy or by open schism. The Church, the one third, has to struggle against a double evil, simplicity against duplicity, like the king in the Gospel who goes out with ten thousand against twenty thousand (Luke 14:31). God promised this through Zechariah: two thirds will perish, and the third he will bring through fire, refining them as silver is refined and testing them as gold is tested, and he will say, “They are my people” (Zechariah 13:8–9).
  • The separation. The third that is burned is the heretics. Through Zechariah the Lord promised to strike the false shepherds and to free his sheep (Zechariah 13:7). Before this separation all are regarded as the people of God. After it, it will be plain who belongs to God and who to the devil. Tyconius says here that anyone found outside the true Church will be condemned with the devil, who is the author of division.

A note on Tyconius. The Church states this more carefully. All salvation comes from Christ the Head through the Church, which is his Body. Those who know that the Catholic Church was founded by God as necessary and still refuse to enter or remain in her cannot be saved. This is not said of those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ and his Church (Catechism of the Catholic Church 846–848). Those born today into communities separated from the Church cannot be charged with the sin of separation, and she accepts them as brothers and sisters in the Lord (CCC 818). Tyconius was himself a Donatist. The Catholic writers who borrowed from him, Primasius, Caesarius and Bede, used his words against those who knowingly divide the Church.

Bede: the first trumpet of the Church’s preaching tells of the destruction of the impious by fire and hail. The warning is rightly compared to a trumpet, the signal for battle: “lift up your voice like a trumpet blast; Proclaim to my people their transgression” (Isaiah 58:1); “Put the trumpet to your lips!” (Hosea 8:1). That Gehenna is the wage of works that shed blood, the preachers say in the words of Job, in Bede’s Latin text: he will pass from the waters of the snow to great heat (Job 24:19). The blood may also mean the spiritual death of the soul (8.7).

Oecumenius: the righteous have been counted worthy to be caught up in the clouds to meet the Lord at his coming (1 Thessalonians 4:17). Now the vision turns to the rest of humankind. Most will be consumed by fire, for that Day will be revealed with fire (1 Corinthians 3:13). As there are many dwelling places for rest (John 14:2), so there are different places of punishment. The trumpets that bring death to those on earth are the same that afterwards raise the dead. One who takes the hail and fire literally is not wrong. One who takes them as a figure is not wrong either: the fire is the distress and deep grief of sinners who see the saints taken up to meet the Lord while they remain on the earth without honour. The trees and the grass are sinners, whose folly and hardness of soul make them like dry wood, fit for burning (8.7).

Andrew: some take this as the punishment of sinners in Gehenna, described under the figure of bodily torments. He thinks it more likely to be the plagues that come before the end, since the third is not the whole number of those who will be punished in the age to come, for the road that leads to destruction is broad (Matthew 7:13). The hail is the scourges that come from heaven by just judgment. The fire mixed with blood is the burning and slaughter at the hands of barbarians, which happen daily. Wars destroy not only human beings but everything the earth produces, so that a third of all creatures on earth perish. Joel confirms it: blood, fire and columns of smoke before the day of the Lord (Joel 3:3–4) (8.7).

07

REVELATION 8:8–9

The second trumpet: a burning mountain cast into the sea

Tyconius: the burning mountain is the devil, who consumes those near him like a fire. He is called “great” because he is an angel among the others, and yet a creature. The sea is the world, where John saw the devil, cast down from heaven, burning (8.8–9).

  • The third that died. Another edition reads a third “of those who have souls.” These have died a spiritual death, like the widow of whom the Apostle says that the self-indulgent one is dead while she lives (1 Timothy 5:6). One third has killed another by a poisonous tradition and by the imitation of useless teaching.
  • The ships. Tyconius’s Latin text read “a third of the birds,” which the editor of the volume takes to be a copyist’s slip for “ships.” He applies it to the devil and those of one mind with him, who flit about like birds and deceive, or wish to deceive, everyone by their trickery (8.9).

Primasius: the burning mountain is the devil, sent against the peoples. The blood is the wisdom of the flesh, which is hostile to God and destroys the soul: “The concern of the flesh is death” (Romans 8:6), and “flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God” (1 Corinthians 15:50) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 8.8–9).

Bede: the second trumpet shows the devil driven out of the Church, to burn more hotly in the sea of the world. As the Christian religion grew, the devil, swollen with pride and burning with his own anger, was cast into that sea, as the Lord said of the mountain cast into the sea (Matthew 21:21). He was there before, but once thrown out of the Church he rages more against his own followers, bringing spiritual death on them through the arrogance of fleshly wisdom. Flesh and blood did not teach the apostles, but the Father in heaven (Matthew 16:17). They steered the ship of faith over a sea that had shown itself fit to be walked on to reach the feet of the Lord (8.8).

Oecumenius: creation was made subject to futility in hope, and will be set free from slavery to corruption (Romans 8:20–21) when there are “new heavens and a new earth” (2 Peter 3:13). The sea is on the earth and shares its renewal, and it is purified by fire. This may be taken literally. By analogy the sea is also the present life, with its turbulence and distractions. The fish and the boats are people fouled by salty and bitter sins, and worn out by useless regret for what they did in life (8.8–9).

Andrew: he knows the opinion that the sea is purified by fire after the resurrection, and thinks the “third” does not suit it, since, as it is said, those who are punished are more than those who are saved. In the spiritual sense the present life is a sea. With some of his teachers he takes the great mountain to be the devil, who burns with wrath against us and will be bound in Gehenna. In the time allowed him he destroys a third of what is in the sea, as he once did to Job, for “a person is a slave of whatever overcomes him” (2 Peter 2:19). The death of the soul comes on those who, in the sea of this life, blaspheme the Trinity by works and words. Andrew is the first Greek commentator known to read the mountain as the devil (8.8–9).

08

REVELATION 8:10–11

The third trumpet: the star called Wormwood

Tyconius: the great star is the devil, of whom the Lord said that he saw Satan fall like lightning from the sky (Luke 10:18). It may also mean people of the Church who lived the spiritual life, forgot themselves, bent down like animals to the things of earth, and fell from their place (Psalm 49:21). The rivers and springs are the teachers of the divine Scriptures who instruct others and turn away from the way of truth themselves. Wormwood is the bitterness of sins, or their sweetness: they are sweet for the moment to those who desire them and turn bitter afterwards (8.10–11).

Primasius: these are people who have fallen from heaven, that is, from the Church, though they had a public name for shining with good merits. They are compared to stars and torches as St. Jude speaks of “wandering stars” (Jude 13), since they lead astray by a surface brightness. The Lord compared such people to “whitewashed tombs” (Matthew 23:27). The ruin of the great confuses many, who are weakened by their fall and corrupted by imitating their teaching. A little bitterness mixed with what is sweet makes the whole bitter. So the Lord asks the choice vine he planted how it turned into a wild one (Jeremiah 2:21) (8.10–11).

Bede: the third trumpet shows the heretics leaving the Church and corrupting the rivers of the holy Scriptures. They fall from the height of the Church and try to pollute the springs of Scripture with the flame of their own iniquity, and are not afraid to falsify its meaning, and often even its words. They deserve the name Wormwood, for a little of it turns great sweetness bitter (8.10–11).

Caesarius: many die from the waters. He applies this to those who are rebaptized (Homily 6).

A note on Caesarius. Baptism seals the Christian with an indelible spiritual mark, and so it cannot be repeated (CCC 1272, 1280). The Donatists rebaptized Catholics who came to them. Caesarius has in view those who do the same in his own day. The note on Primasius in chapter 4 says more on Baptism and the unity of the Church.

Oecumenius: Naomi asked to be called “Bitter” because of her many troubles (Ruth 1:20). The bitterness here is what sinners suffer when they see the glory of the saints: such good things were prepared for humankind, and they traded what is to come for the things of the present. He thinks the falling star is a wrath of God that makes the waters bitter. The waters are peoples, as in the psalm of the floods that lift up their voice (Psalm 93:3–4). These things are spoken in figures, and yet such things may also happen at that time (8.10–11).

Andrew: some say the bitterness is the torment of sinners in Gehenna, who are called “waters” for their number. He thinks it means sufferings in time. The star shows that these come from heaven, or it is the devil, the fallen Morning Star (Isaiah 14:12), who through pleasure gives people a foul and bitter destruction to drink. God in his patience allows this to reach only a third. We should examine ourselves so that we are not condemned with the world (1 Corinthians 11:31–32), and receive the sufferings that come to us with thanksgiving. The sick bear the knife and the cautery of the physician because they want to be healed. So may we be healthy in spirit, bring no wood to the fire of Gehenna, and reign with Christ for ever (8.10–11).

09

REVELATION 8:12

The fourth trumpet: the lights darkened

Tyconius: the sun, moon and stars are the Church, a third of which is struck. This third is “a designation, not a quantity.” There are two peoples within the Church: the part that belongs to God, compared to light, and the part that belongs to the devil, wrapped in darkness. That part is struck so that it may become apparent who is of God and who is of the devil. It is given over to its own sins and desires, so that faults long hidden and unknown may be revealed to all (8.12).

Bede: the fourth trumpet shows the falling away of false brothers. The glory of the Church shines like a star and is often dimmed by them. In good times and in hard times their defection makes her shine less brightly (Explanation of the Apocalypse 8.2, 12).

Oecumenius: Joel foretold that the sun will be darkened and the moon turned to blood before the day of the Lord (Joel 3:4). St. Peter says that the heavens will pass away and the elements be dissolved by fire (2 Peter 3:10). The Lord says that after the tribulation the sun will be darkened and the stars will fall (Matthew 24:29). Why does only a third of earth, sea, rivers and heavenly lights suffer? It is proof of the goodness of God. He calls the people of that time to repentance by a partial dissolution of the elements and holds back their total destruction. The psalm says that he made a path for his anger (Psalm 78:50). His wrath advances by stages, as on a road, and so he opens a door for repentance and calls people to a change of heart through fear of what is coming. Complete destruction comes at last on those who do not repent (8.12–13).

10

REVELATION 8:13

The eagle: “Woe! Woe! Woe!”

Victorinus: the eagle is the Holy Spirit, who through two prophets proclaims that the great wrath of the plagues is near. This is done so that, even at the end of time, someone may still be saved (8.13).

Tyconius: the eagle flying in mid-heaven is the word of God, which runs freely in the midst of the Church and announces openly the plagues of the last time (8.13).

Bede: the voice of this eagle flies every day through the mouths of the great teachers of the Church, when they announce the evil of the heretics, the rage of the Antichrist and the day of judgment, which will come in all its severity on those who love the earth. He cites St. Paul on the terrifying times of the last days (2 Timothy 3:1–2, 8), on the lawless one who exalts himself above every so-called god and object of worship (2 Thessalonians 2:3–4), and on the day of the Lord that comes like a thief in the night when people are saying, “Peace and security” (1 Thessalonians 5:2–3) (8.13).

Primasius: the eagle is the Church, who moves through the heavens in swift flight out of contempt for the earth, and says, “our citizenship is in heaven” (Philippians 3:20). She flies “in the middle” because she is to possess the whole world, and because God is in the midst of his own: all around him bring gifts (Psalm 76:12), he works salvation in the midst of the earth (Psalm 74:12, in the old Latin wording), and “the LORD surrounds his people” (Psalm 125:2) (8.13).

Oecumenius: the eagle is a holy angel, who looks with sorrow on the sufferings of those on earth and feels for humankind in its plight (8.12–13).

Andrew: the passage shows the sympathy and goodness of the holy angels. Like God, they are merciful toward sinners who are being chastised, and most of all toward those who do not see that their suffering is meant for their conversion. The “woe” fits these especially: they live on the earth, think earthly thoughts, and breathe dust instead of the perfume that has been poured out for us. For those whose citizenship is in heaven (Philippians 3:20), sufferings become the cause of unfading crowns and rewards (8.13).
