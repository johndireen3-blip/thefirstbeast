Revelation 21:10–22:5

The City, the Temple and the Garden

The study began with the ark seen in heaven. It ends with a city shaped like the room where the ark stood, with no temple in it, and the tree of life growing in its street.

The text
"He took me in spirit to a great, high mountain and showed me the holy city Jerusalem coming down out of heaven from God. It gleamed with the splendor of God… I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God almighty and the Lamb… Then the angel showed me the river of life-giving water, sparkling like crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb down the middle of its street. On either side of the river grew the tree of life… They will look upon his face, and his name will be on their foreheads" (Revelation 21:10–11, 22; 22:1–2, 4).
In one sentence
The last vision gathers the whole Bible into one place: Jerusalem, the Holy of Holies and Eden are a single city, and its life is to see God's face.
Authority
Scripture

From the ark to the city

The central part of this study opened with one verse: "Then God's temple in heaven was opened, and the ark of his covenant could be seen in the temple" (11:19). The ark stood in the Holy of Holies, the inner room that only the high priest entered, once a year. See The ark in heaven.

Now look at the shape of the city. "The city was square… its length and width and height are the same" (21:16). It is a cube. The notes of the New American Bible point to the one other cube in Scripture: "The city is shaped like a gigantic cube, a symbol of perfection (cf. 1 Kgs 6:19–20)" (note on 21:15–17). That passage of Kings describes the inner sanctuary of Solomon's temple, built to hold the ark, equal in length, width and height.

The city is the Holy of Holies. What was one small room, entered by one man, has become a city fifteen hundred miles on a side, with gates that are never shut. At 11:19 John saw into the sanctuary. Here the whole people of God live in it. A theologian

The city

John's guide is Ezekiel, who was also set "on a very high mountain" and shown "something like a city" with a man holding a measuring rod (Ezekiel 40:2–3). The notes: "Most of the images are borrowed from Ez 40–48."

  • Twelve gates, with the names of the twelve tribes (21:12), as in Ezekiel 48. Israel is not replaced. Her names are on the doors, and everyone who enters comes in through her.
  • Twelve foundations, with the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb (21:14). St. Paul: "built upon the foundation of the apostles and prophets" (Ephesians 2:20). The Catechism quotes this verse to explain why the Church is called apostolic (CCC 865).
  • The measurements. Twelve thousand stadia each way; a wall of 144 cubits. "Multiples of the symbolic number twelve," say the notes. These are the numbers of the 144,000: the city is the size of the people. See The 144,000 and the great multitude.
  • Measured. In chapter 11 John was told to measure the temple and leave the outer court to be trampled (The temple measured). Here everything is measured, and nothing is left outside to be trampled.
  • Gold and precious stones. They "symbolize the beauty and excellence of the church," say the notes. The twelve stones recall the twelve on the high priest's breastpiece (Exodus 28:17–21). The harlot too was "adorned with gold, precious stones, and pearls" (17:4). Hers were worn; these are what the city is made of.

Scripture

No temple

"I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God almighty and the Lamb" (21:22).

For a reader of Ezekiel this is the surprise. Ezekiel's last nine chapters are almost entirely about a new temple. John follows him closely and then leaves the temple out.

A temple exists so that God can be present in one place among a people who cannot bear his presence everywhere. It has walls, courts and a veil. In the city there is nothing left to veil. Ezekiel's vision ended by giving the city a new name: "The LORD is there" (Ezekiel 48:35). John shows what that name means.

The Lord said to the Samaritan woman that the hour was coming when worship would be neither on her mountain nor in Jerusalem (John 4:21), and of his own body, "Destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19). The notes of the New American Bible cite both.

And no sun. "The city had no need of sun or moon to shine on it, for the glory of God gave it light, and its lamp was the Lamb" (21:23), as Isaiah had promised: "No longer shall the sun be your light by day… the LORD will be your light forever" (Isaiah 60:19). In chapter 12 the Woman was clothed with the sun and stood on the moon. Sun and moon were her garments. Here even these are unnecessary, because the one they signified is present.

The garden

The last scene returns to the first pages of Genesis.

"The tree of life in the middle of the garden" (2:9) "On either side of the river grew the tree of life"
The ground cursed (3:17) "Nothing accursed will be found there anymore"
The man and woman hide from God's face (3:8) "They will look upon his face"
Cherubim and a fiery sword "to guard the way to the tree of life" (3:24) Gates never shut (21:25); "the right to the tree of life" (22:14)
A serpent in the garden No serpent

The river also comes from Ezekiel, who saw "water flowing out from under the threshold of the temple," and trees along its banks: "Every month they will bear fresh fruit… Their fruit is used for food, and their leaves for healing" (Ezekiel 47:1, 12). John adds one phrase: the leaves are "for the nations."

It is not simply a return. Eden was a garden with two people in it. This is a garden inside a city, with the nations walking by its light and bringing their treasure into it (21:24–26). Nothing good in human history is lost. The Second Vatican Council, quoted by the Catechism, says of the fruits of human nature and work that "we will find them once again, cleansed this time from the stain of sin, illuminated and transfigured" (CCC 1050). Church teaching

In chapter 12 the serpent "spewed a torrent of water out of his mouth" to sweep the Woman away (12:15). The river of the last chapter flows from the throne. See The dragon pursues the woman.

Face to face

"They will look upon his face, and his name will be on their foreheads" (22:4).

In the Old Testament no one could see God's face and live (Exodus 33:20). The high priest entered the Holy of Holies in a cloud of incense, so as not to see. This single line says that the oldest barrier is gone.

The Church calls this the beatific vision. "Those who die in God's grace and friendship and are perfectly purified live for ever with Christ. They are like God for ever, for they 'see him as he is,' face to face" (CCC 1023). She adds that it is "beyond all understanding and description," and that "Scripture speaks of it in images: life, light, peace, wedding feast, wine of the kingdom, the Father's house, the heavenly Jerusalem, paradise" (CCC 1027). Church teaching

Nearly every image in that list is in this passage. John has not given a map of heaven. He has piled up every picture Scripture has, the city, the bride, the sanctuary, the garden, the light, because no one of them is enough.

And the name on the forehead is the seal of chapter 7, now seen for what it was. See Two marks on the forehead.

Read with the Woman

The ark held the presence of God for Israel. Mary bore him in her body. The Church carries him through history. In each reading the Woman is the place where God dwells among his people, hidden: in a tent, in a womb, under sacramental signs.

In the city nothing is hidden. That is why there is no ark in it and no temple. The Woman's whole work, in every reading, was to bring God's presence to his people. Here it is finished, and what remains is the presence itself. The Church believes Mary already lives there, body and soul, "a sign of certain hope and comfort to the pilgrim People of God" (CCC 972). Church teaching

What the Fathers said

The Fathers cited here read the city as the Church, and almost every detail of it as Christ or his apostles. A Father or Doctor

The city on the mountain (21:10). St. Caesarius of Arles says the mountain is Christ, and the city is the Church set on him, carried back like the lost sheep on the Shepherd's shoulders (Luke 15:5). She cannot be a different bride from the Church, he adds, or there would be two brides (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 19). Primasius hears the Lord's "city set on a mountain" (Matthew 5:14) and Isaiah's mountain of the Lord's house (Isaiah 2:2). She comes down from heaven because her beauty is from the Spirit, by whom her Bridegroom was conceived and born (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.9–10). St. Bede remembers the stone cut from the mountain without hands, which broke the image of the world's glory and grew to fill the whole earth (Daniel 2:34–35).

Her radiance (21:11). The light is not her own. Apringius quotes Isaiah: the sun will no longer be your light by day, for the Lord will be your everlasting light (Isaiah 60:19). Oecumenius and Andrew say her radiance is Christ. Jasper is green, for he is life-giving and feeds every living thing (Psalm 145:16); it is clear as crystal, for he did no wrong and no deceit was in his mouth (Isaiah 53:9). Bede quotes the Lord's prayer: the glory you gave me I have given them (John 17:22).

The wall, the gates, the angels and the names (21:12–13).

  • The wall is Christ. All say so. Apringius quotes Zechariah, where the Lord is a wall of fire around the city (Zechariah 2:9); Primasius quotes Isaiah's song of the strong city with its wall and rampart (Isaiah 26:1–3).
  • The gates. For most they are the apostles, through whom we came to the faith. Apringius adds that Christ alone is the door (John 10:9), and the names on the gates show that the whole company of the patriarchs belonged to faith in him. The Church is built on Peter's confession, that is, on faith in the Lord's Incarnation, Passion and Resurrection (Matthew 16:18) (Tractate on the Apocalypse 21.12).
  • The tribes. Primasius takes the gates themselves for the twelve patriarchs, from whose stock Israel came. He quotes St. Paul: there is a remnant chosen by grace; God has not rejected his people; and it is the root that supports you (Romans 11:2, 5, 18) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.12). Oecumenius reads the names of spiritual Israel, all who follow the faith of Abraham (Romans 4:12), and the twelve tribes as the full number of the saints.
  • The angels. Oecumenius and Andrew think holy angels worked with the apostles in spreading the Gospel, as the law was given through angels (Hebrews 2:2). If each of the faithful has a guardian angel, says Andrew, how much more were the highest angels given to those who founded the Church. Bede takes the angels for the Church's teachers, who follow in the apostles' steps.
  • Three gates on each side. The four quarters of the world have received faith in the Trinity (Apringius, Caesarius, Bede), which the apostles preached when they baptized in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19) (Oecumenius). Andrew sees in the arrangement the shape of the cross, and recalls the twelve oxen, three facing each direction, that bore the bronze sea in Solomon's temple (1 Kings 7:23–25): a figure of baptism, which cleanses the world.

Twelve foundations, one foundation (21:14). Primasius explains how there can be twelve when St. Paul says there is only one (1 Corinthians 3:11). The apostles are foundations in Christ, as they are the light of the world (Matthew 5:14) only because he is the true light (John 1:9). He is the Light that enlightens, and they are a light that is enlightened. He is called the foundation even without them; without him they are not foundations at all (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.14). Oecumenius says that Christ the wall rests on the apostles' preaching as its cornerstone (Isaiah 28:16), and ends with a prayer that we remain in their teaching. Andrew: their names are written up as on a public notice, for the instruction of those who read. Bede: the foundations may also be the patriarchs, and in the singular the foundation is the Lord himself, "the foundation of foundations."

The golden measuring rod (21:15). Apringius reads the rod as faith in the Lord's Incarnation, by which alone the city is measured. Primasius takes the angel for Christ, the Wisdom of God, who reaches from end to end and orders all things well (Wisdom 8:1), in measure and number and weight (Wisdom 11:20). He alone measures the city, because he gives each of the faithful the gifts of grace. Caesarius sees in the golden rod the members of the Church, weak in the flesh and founded on golden faith: we hold this treasure in earthen vessels (2 Corinthians 4:7).

Foursquare (21:16). A cube stands firm, say Oecumenius and Andrew: the blessings of the saints are permanent and cannot change. Oecumenius adds that the size of the city shows that the saints, though fewer than sinners, are not few. Apringius reads the four sides as the fourfold Gospel, in which nothing is out of proportion, nothing added and nothing taken away. Primasius: this is the city built on the Rock. "The Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4), and Peter is named from the Rock as the Christian is named from Christ (Matthew 16:18). Bede: her length is faith, her breadth is love and her height is hope, and if one were lacking she would not stand (Explanation of the Apocalypse 21.16). Andrew offers a reckoning of the twelve thousand stadia, by sevens, as the rest of soul and body.

The wall of a hundred and forty-four cubits (21:17). Apringius: the wall is Christ, measured by the measure of a man because the humanity he assumed is the protection of the saints; and of an angel, because he is the messenger of the covenant (Malachi 3:1) and the Wonder-Counselor (Isaiah 9:5). Oecumenius notes that Scripture often calls angels men, as Gabriel's name means "man of God" (Daniel 9:21; Luke 12:35–36). What is divine is beyond measure, but by contemplating the greatness of God, men become like the angels. Primasius: the Church, gathered from the human race and lifted up by Christ's promises, hopes for equality with the angels; and the city is made up of both, angels already at home and human beings on the way.

Pure gold, clear as glass (21:18). Apringius of Beja reads the gold as the company of the saints, tried in the furnace of suffering, and the glass as the transparent holiness that is in them. The jasper of the wall he takes for the Lord's body, taken from the Virgin, raised up to protect the whole (Tractate on the Apocalypse 21.18). Primasius asks why gold should be compared to glass, which it does not resemble. With glass nothing is seen outside that is not within. In that city the thoughts of each will be open to all, when the Lord brings to light what is hidden (1 Corinthians 4:5). Now no one knows a person's thoughts but his own spirit (1 Corinthians 2:11) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.18). For St. Andrew of Caesarea the jasper shows the ever-green life of the saints.

The twelve foundations and their stones (21:19–20).

  • A spiritual building. Clement of Alexandria says the gems stand for the spotless brilliance of spirit, not for earthly ornament (Christ the Educator 2.12.118). Primasius finds the city already in Tobit's song (Tobit 13:16) and in Isaiah, who adds at once that all her children will be taught by the Lord (Isaiah 54:11–13), so that no one will think of an earthly building. St. Paul says the same (Ephesians 2:20–21).
  • The faith of the apostles. Apringius: the foundations are the apostolic faith and preaching, on which Christ, the "Foundation of foundations," builds his Church. The stones differ because the gifts of the Spirit differ in each apostle. St. Caesarius of Arles says the same (1 Corinthians 12:11).
  • Old covenant and new. Andrew counts eight of the twelve stones among those on the high priest's breastplate and four that are new. This shows the agreement of the two covenants and the greater brightness of the second (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.19).

Andrew assigns each stone to an apostle, and St. Bede reads each as a grace of the Christian life, drawing on what was then said of the stones.

Bede adds that the stones are given one to each foundation because each saint has received his own gift (1 Corinthians 12:8–10). All of them together are found whole in Christ, the one mediator, on whose breast as High Priest the twelve stones were placed (Explanation of the Apocalypse 21.20).

Twelve gates, each a single pearl (21:21). All agree that the gates are the apostles, and that the one pearl is Christ, from whom they have their brightness. As Christ is the door, says Apringius, so the apostles are the doors through which we are taught and enter the faith. Oecumenius notes that the pearl is new: it was not among the stones of the old breastplate. The apostles knew the law, and knew better the Gospel, in which the shadow gave way to the reality; they bring out of their treasure things new and old (Matthew 13:52). Bede remembers the pearl of great price, for which the merchant sold all he had.

The street of gold. Apringius sees in the streets the saints of lesser merit, who gathered into one company shine with no lesser light. Primasius sees the little ones and the simple. No one there will be thought unworthy, small or weak, though one will differ from another in glory as star differs from star (1 Corinthians 15:41–42). Andrew admits that no single image can hold what the city is: John saw it as gold for its worth and as crystal for its purity, and the reality surpasses hearing, sight and mind (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.21).

No temple (21:22).

  • Apringius: a temple was given so that people within its walls might call on him whom no temple can contain, and reach by faith what cannot be seen. Where he shows himself openly, no temple is wanted.
  • Oecumenius: our knowledge of God is now as in a mirror, and then will be face to face (1 Corinthians 13:12). He asks why the Lamb is named beside the Lord God Almighty, whom he takes for the Trinity. It is to show that one of the Holy Trinity became flesh and is not without his flesh even now in heaven (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.15–22).
  • Primasius: the Church herself, with the weeds removed, will rest in God as in a temple. Her wholeness is in three things, in being, in knowing and in loving: God made her, enlightened her and makes her blessed (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.22).
  • Caesarius: the Church is in God and God is in the Church. Andrew: God is both the temple of the saints and the one who dwells in them; and the Spirit is not left out, for he appears a little later as the river. Bede: God himself will be their house, their light and their rest.

Its lamp is the Lamb (21:23–24). The glory of God is the presence of his majesty, says Apringius, and those who see him as he is (1 John 3:2) need no sun. Primasius: the city is lit by the Sun of justice, who has healing in his wings (Malachi 3:20), the true light who enlightens everyone (John 1:9). Bede: the Church is not ruled by the lights or elements of this world, but led through its darkness by Christ, the eternal Sun. Apringius adds that the nations walk in his light because what our weakness had hidden was made clear when the Lord took a body (Psalm 36:10; Romans 13:12; John 1:4–5).

The kings and the glory of the nations. Oecumenius takes the kings of the earth for all the saints, and the nations for believers both from the Gentiles and from Israel. The glory they bring in is the honor that comes from virtue. Primasius: the Lamb who will be the life of the citizens is the way for those still travelling, and whatever dignity they have received they return in praise to the giver.

Gates never shut; no night (21:25). Oecumenius gives two meanings. There will be such peace that the city needs no watch and no bars. Or the gates are the apostles, whose teaching will not fall silent even there, since the divine light is never interrupted. Primasius: the open gates mean complete security. There it is no longer said, watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation (Matthew 26:41), but be still and know that I am God (Psalm 46:11). His summary is one of the finest lines in these pages: there "what is seen is truly loved," and what is loved is praised without weariness (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.25).

St. Victorinus reads these verses of the earthly kingdom he expected: the hundredfold promised to those who left all (Matthew 19:29), the wine the Lord will drink new with his disciples (Matthew 26:29), and the lands promised to Abraham made level and cleansed (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.2, 5–6). The volume's editor notes that he renders these chapters freely to fit that reading. The Church did not keep it; see The dragon bound.

Nothing unclean will enter (21:27). Apringius: no one enters who refuses to be cleansed of sin, or who, once cleansed and soiled again, refuses the washing of humility and tears. Oecumenius: light has no fellowship with darkness (2 Corinthians 6:14). Primasius: John is describing the Church of the future, when the wicked will no longer be mixed with the good. Those who enter are the ones to whom the Lord said that their names are written in heaven (Luke 10:20).

The river of life-giving water (22:1).

  • Baptism, in this life. Apringius of Beja: the living water is the Lord himself, who promised it to the Samaritan woman (John 4:10). It is the washing of the font and the brightness of faith, and it flows from the throne because the cleansing, the life and the holiness of baptism all come from him (Romans 6:4) (Tractate on the Apocalypse 22.1). St. Caesarius of Arles sees the font of baptism flowing from God and from Christ in the midst of the Church. St. Andrew of Caesarea agrees for the present life: the river is the baptism of rebirth, made effective by the Spirit.
  • The Holy Spirit, in the life to come. In the Jerusalem above, Andrew says, the river is the Holy Spirit, flowing from God the Father through the Son, among the highest powers of heaven, and filling the streets of the city, that is, its people, who are more in number than the sand (Psalm 139:18) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.1–2).
  • The fruit of baptism. Primasius: here the river is no longer the giving of the sacrament but what the sacrament leads to. We sow now in tears and shall reap in joy; the Church sows to the Spirit to reap eternal life (Galatians 6:8) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.1).
  • Christ himself. Primasius adds a second reading. Christ is the fountain of life (Psalm 36:10), and he is "the Fountain who is also the River": a fountain because he remains inexhaustible in himself, and a river because he gives himself generously and without end. It runs down the middle of the street because it is the common good of all the saints, an inheritance that is not divided (Psalm 122:3) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.2–3).
  • From the throne of God and of the Lamb. St. Jerome notes that there is one throne, for the kingship of the Trinity is one. The river of graces does not flow from the throne unless the Lamb stands before it: unless we believe in the Incarnation of Christ, we do not receive those graces (John 1:29) (Homilies on the Psalms 1).

The tree of life (22:2).

  • Christ, on both banks. Jerome asks how one tree can stand on both sides of a river. The river is the grace of the Holy Spirit, found in the Scriptures. Its two banks are the Old Testament and the New, and the one tree planted on both is Christ. Its fruit is the meaning of Scripture, and its leaves are the words. Whoever reads only the letter has the leaves, and these are not useless, for the leaves never fade (Psalm 1:3) and are medicine for the nations. Whoever reads with spiritual insight, coming to the tree through the apostles, gathers the fruit (Homilies on the Psalms 1). Apringius reads it the same way: the two banks are the two Testaments, the tree is Christ, in whom we live and move and have our being (Acts 17:28), and the leaves are his words, for the health of all nations. The twelve months are the apostles, because the Lord himself is the year of favor (Isaiah 61:2) (Tractate on the Apocalypse 22.2).
  • Wisdom. Oecumenius recalls that Wisdom is called a tree of life to those who lay hold of her (Proverbs 3:18), and Christ is the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24). The saints have his gifts and also have him, living in them and among them (2 Corinthians 6:16), which is the height of blessedness. The leaves are all who hang from Christ and cling to him: patriarchs, prophets, apostles, evangelists, martyrs, confessors and pastors, who heal souls even now (Commentary on the Apocalypse 21.26–22.5).
  • The cross. Caesarius: no tree bears fruit in every season except the cross of the Lord. The faithful, watered by the Church's river, eat of it, by which he means baptism and the Eucharist, and they in turn bear lasting fruit in every season (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 19).
  • Immortality and praise. Primasius: the twelve months mean all of time and all of eternity, where no dryness and no infirmity enters, and sorrow and sighing flee away (Isaiah 35:10). This is the tree planted near streams of water (Psalm 1:3; Jeremiah 17:8). Its fruit is the reward of a blessed immortality, and its leaves are the song of unending praise.
  • The saints. Andrew: those planted beside the river are themselves called the tree of life, because they share in him who is the Tree of Life and imitate him. There will be no winter of sin to strip their leaves. Or the fruits are the fuller knowledge to be given in the age to come, and the leaves the understanding of God's judgments, which heals the ignorance of those who were weaker in virtue; for star differs from star in glory (1 Corinthians 15:41), and in the Father's house there are many dwelling places (John 14:2). He offers a third reading from the parable of the sower. The twelve fruits are the apostles, who share in the Tree of Life himself, since by sharing our flesh he gave us a share in his divinity. Their fruit is those who yield a hundredfold. The leaves are those who yield sixtyfold, who pass on to later generations the light they received from the first. And the healing is for those from the nations who yield thirtyfold. The saved will differ as fruit differs from leaves, some glorified more and some less. The tree is spoken of as one because the saints share one life in harmony (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.2).

Nothing accursed; one throne (22:3). No temptation will come near, says Apringius, and all will serve God in the happiness of that city, with his name engraved on their foreheads in the sign of his cross (Tractate on the Apocalypse 22.3–4). Oecumenius: the saints will be clean of every stain of soul and body. Their worship will not be toil; it will be joy, and it comes from seeing his face. Primasius notes that John says "throne" and not "thrones." There is one indivisible rule of the one Godhead, which the Arian error cannot divide or the Sabellian error confuse (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.3). Andrew explains the word for "accursed" as whatever is set aside for the devil and condemned with him. Nothing of that kind will be in the city.

They will look upon his face (22:4).

  • Primasius: this is the reward of faith, the sight John himself spoke of: we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is (1 John 3:2). "Face" does not mean a bodily face. It means the showing of what God is. He will be seen in the spirit, by each within himself and in one another; he will be seen in himself; and he will be seen in the new heavens and the new earth and in every creature. Primasius is here quoting St. Augustine (City of God 22.29) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.4).
  • Oecumenius: they will see as far as human nature can. The name on their foreheads is their unbroken remembrance of God and union with him: we shall always be with the Lord (1 Thessalonians 4:17).
  • Andrew: they will see him no longer in shadows but face to face, as the apostles saw him on the holy mountain. For this he appeals to "the great Dionysius," the writer known as Dionysius the Areopagite (The Divine Names 1.4). In place of the gold plate on the high priest's forehead, which this study recalls on The seal of the living God, they will bear the divine name on their foreheads and in their hearts, the sign of a firm and fearless love (Commentary on the Apocalypse 22.3–4).
  • St. Bede: the vision of God is the highest good, as Philip understood when he said, "show us the Father, and that will be enough for us" (John 14:8) (Explanation of the Apocalypse 22.4).

No night; they will reign forever (22:5). Apringius: neither the night of sin nor the darkness of injustice will appear again. The saints will not live by the words of any teacher or need the light of another's understanding, for all the knowledge they desire is given in the brightness of his face (Tractate on the Apocalypse 22.5). Oecumenius quotes Daniel: the holy ones of the Most High shall receive the kingship and possess it forever (Daniel 7:18). Andrew: if the just will shine like the sun (Matthew 13:43), what need have they of a lamp? They will be ruled by him, or rather they will reign with him (2 Timothy 2:12). Bede: the body will need neither the rest of night nor the light of a fire, for God will be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:28). Read as a figure, the prophets and the law, which are now a lamp shining in a dark place (2 Peter 1:19), will no longer be needed, when every promise is completed in the sight of God (Explanation of the Apocalypse 22.5).

Source: the Fathers are cited from Revelation, Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 21:9–22:5.

Where readers differ

  • The Church now, or heaven to come. The notes of the New American Bible read the city as "the new Jerusalem, the church," and take even "no temple" of Christ's presence in the Church today. The Catechism reads the same verses of the final consummation (CCC 1044–1045). The letter to the Hebrews holds both: "you have approached Mount Zion and the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" (Hebrews 12:22). We have come to it already, and we are not yet inside. Disputed
  • Literal dimensions. The numbers are symbolic. A cube fifteen hundred miles high is not architecture.
  • "The kings of the earth." They were the beast's allies at 19:19, and here they bring their treasure into the city. Some see in this the breadth of God's mercy toward the nations. The verse must be read with the next: "nothing unclean will enter it" (21:27).

What it asks of us

The book does not end with the vision. It ends with a prayer. "The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come.' Let the hearer say, 'Come'" (22:17). And the last words before the blessing: "Amen! Come, Lord Jesus!" (22:20).

The notes of the New American Bible call this "a liturgical refrain," the Marana tha of the first Christians. The Catechism says Christians pray it "above all in the Eucharist" (CCC 671). Every Mass is the Bride saying "Come" and being answered.

That is what the whole study asks. The Woman in labor, the flight to the desert, the seal on the forehead, the refusal of the mark, the endurance of the holy ones: all of it is the life of someone who is waiting for a person and wants him to come. "On this way of perfection," the Catechism says, "the Spirit and the Bride call whoever hears them to perfect communion with God" (CCC 2550).
