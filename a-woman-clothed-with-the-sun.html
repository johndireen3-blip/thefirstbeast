Revelation 12:1

A Woman Clothed with the Sun

The great sign in heaven: what John's words say, where the images come from, and why the Church sees Mary here first, with Israel and the Church beside her.

Home › The Study › A woman clothed with the sun

✓ Reviewed by Fr. John How this site is made

The text
"A great sign appeared in the sky, a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars" (Revelation 12:1).
In one sentence
The Woman is the mother of the Messiah: the liturgy reads her as Mary, and in Mary the Church sees both Israel, from whom Christ came, and herself.
Authority
Scripture

The words

  • A great sign. The Greek is sēmeion mega. A sign points beyond itself. It is the first of three "signs" in this part of the book: the Woman (12:1), the dragon (12:3), and the seven angels with the last plagues (15:1). Only the Woman's is called simply "great" at its first appearing.
  • Appeared. The same verb as in the verse before, where the ark "could be seen" (11:19). See The ark in heaven.
  • Clothed with the sun. She does not shine by her own light. She is wrapped in it, as one puts on a garment.
  • A crown. The word is stephanos, the wreath given to a victor. The dragon wears diadēmata, the diadems of kings (12:3). Her crown is won; his are seized.

Scripture

The Old Testament behind it

  • Joseph's dream. "The sun and the moon and eleven stars were bowing down to me" (Genesis 37:9). The sun, moon and stars are Jacob, his wife and his sons: the family of Israel. Twelve stars are the twelve tribes complete.
  • The bride of the Song. "Who is this that comes forth like the dawn, beautiful as the white moon, pure as the blazing sun, fearsome as celestial visions?" (Song of Songs 6:10).
  • God's own garment. The psalmist says of the LORD, "You are clothed with majesty and splendor, robed in light as with a cloak" (Psalm 104:1–2). The Woman wears what belongs to God.
  • The sign of the virgin. Isaiah told King Ahaz, "Ask for a sign from the LORD, your God; let it be deep as Sheol, or high as the sky!" When Ahaz refused, the prophet said, "The Lord himself will give you a sign; the young woman, pregnant and about to bear a son, shall name him Emmanuel" (Isaiah 7:11, 14). John sees a sign, high as the sky: a woman with child (Revelation 12:2).

Scripture

Read first as Mary

The child the Woman bears is the one "destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod" (Revelation 12:5; Psalm 2:9). He is the Messiah, and the mother of the Messiah is Mary. The Church's prayer and teaching read the sign of her:

  • The liturgy. Pope St. Paul VI begins his letter on Mary with these words: "The great sign which the Apostle John saw in heaven, 'a woman clothed with the sun,' is interpreted by the sacred Liturgy, not without foundation, as referring to the most blessed Mary, the mother of all men by the grace of Christ the Redeemer" (Signum Magnum, 1967). The verse is the entrance antiphon and part of the first reading of the Mass of the Assumption. Church teaching
  • Pope St. Pius X. "Everyone knows that this woman signified the Virgin Mary, the stainless one who brought forth our Head" (Ad Diem Illum 24, 1904). Church teaching
  • Pius XII. "The scholastic Doctors have recognized the Assumption of the Virgin Mother of God as something signified, not only in various figures of the Old Testament, but also in that woman clothed with the sun whom John the Apostle contemplated on the Island of Patmos" (Munificentissimus Deus 27, 1950). Church teaching
  • Pope St. John Paul II. The enmity foretold in Genesis 3:15 "is confirmed in the Apocalypse," where "there recurs the sign of the 'woman,' this time 'clothed with the sun' (Rev. 12:1). Mary, Mother of the Incarnate Word, is placed at the very center of that enmity" (Redemptoris Mater 11, 1987). Church teaching
  • The Catechism names among those who take part in the liturgy of heaven "the all-holy Mother of God (the Woman)" (CCC 1138), and says of the dragon: "'He pursued the woman' but had no hold on her: the new Eve, 'full of grace' of the Holy Spirit, is preserved from sin and the corruption of death" (CCC 2853). Church teaching

Israel beside her

The images are Israel's. The sun, moon and stars come from Joseph's dream, and the twelve stars are the twelve tribes. The prophets spoke of Zion as a mother in labor (Isaiah 66:7–9; Micah 4:10). The notes of the New American Bible read the Woman this way: she "symbolizes God's people in the Old and the New Testament. The Israel of old gave birth to the Messiah" (note on 12:1–6). A theologian

Mary does not replace this reading. She is the daughter of Zion in whom it comes true: the one woman of Israel who in fact gave birth to the Messiah.

The Church beside her

The earliest commentary we have reads the Woman as the Church. St. Hippolytus of Rome, about the year 200:

"By the woman then clothed with the sun, he meant most manifestly the Church, endued with the Father's word, whose brightness is above the sun. And by the 'moon under her feet' he referred to her being adorned, like the moon, with heavenly glory. And the words, 'upon her head a crown of twelve stars,' refer to the twelve apostles by whom the Church was founded" (On Christ and Antichrist 61). A Father or Doctor

The rest of the chapter supports him. The Woman flees into the wilderness and is nourished there (12:6, 14), and the dragon makes war on "the rest of her offspring, those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus" (12:17). Those are things said of the Church through her history.

How the three belong together

Pope St. John Paul II holds them in one sentence. By Mary's "ecclesial identification as the 'woman clothed with the sun' (Rev. 12:1), it can be said that 'in the Most Holy Virgin the Church has already reached that perfection whereby she exists without spot or wrinkle'" (Redemptoris Mater 47). Church teaching

The Catechism gives the principle: in Mary "we contemplate what the Church already is in her mystery on her own 'pilgrimage of faith,' and what she will be in the homeland at the end of her journey" (CCC 972). Church teaching

So the sign is one. Israel is the people from whom the Messiah came. Mary is the daughter of Israel who bore him. The Church is the mother of all who belong to him. What is said of the Woman is said first of Mary, and through her of the people she comes from and the people she is mother to.

What the Fathers said

Hippolytus, quoted above, speaks for most of the Fathers: the Woman is the Church. Two of them name Mary. A Father or Doctor

The Woman as Mary.

  • Oecumenius (sixth century), author of the earliest Greek commentary that survives, says plainly that the vision speaks of the mother of our Savior. It shows her in heaven and not on earth because she is pure in soul and body, "a citizen of heaven," though she shares our nature. He insists on that last point against Eutyches, who denied that the Virgin, and so her Son, was of one substance with us. The sun is Christ, the sun of righteousness (Malachi 3:20). It would have been more exact, he remarks, to say that the Woman clothed the sun, since she carried him in her womb. The vision says the reverse to show that the Lord she carried was the shelter of his own mother and of all creation, as the angel told her that the power of the Most High would overshadow her (Luke 1:35). The moon under her feet he takes for the law of Moses, which has its light from Christ as the moon has its light from the sun (Commentary on the Apocalypse 12.1–2).
  • Quodvultdeus, bishop of Carthage and a friend of St. Augustine, preaching to those about to be baptized around 430: "We all know that the dragon is the devil and that that woman signifies the Virgin Mary," who remained a virgin in giving birth to our Head. He adds at once that she presents in herself a figure of the holy Church, which brings forth Christ's members without losing her virginity (On the Symbol 3.1). This is the oldest Latin witness to the Marian reading, and he speaks of it as something his hearers already know.

The Woman as the Church.

  • St. Methodius: she is our mother the Church, clothed with the brightness of the Word as with a robe, and adorned with stars in place of jewels. She stands on the moon, which he takes for the faith of those cleansed in baptism, and labors to bring forth natural people as spiritual. Methodius hears the prophet speaking to her when he calls Jerusalem to rise and shine because her light has come: darkness covers the earth, the glory of the Lord rises on her, nations walk toward her light, and her sons and daughters are gathered to her from far away (Isaiah 60:1–4). These, he says, are the Church's children, who will run to her from every side after the resurrection (Symposium 8.5–6). His words are given more fully on Who is the woman?
  • Tyconius: the Woman is the Church, who in baptism puts on Christ, the sun of righteousness (Galatians 3:27). The moon under her feet may be the church of the heretics, which Christ does not light; or, taken in a good sense, the Church herself, of whom it is written that she is fair as the moon (Song of Songs 6:10; Psalm 89:36–38). The twelve stars are the apostles, set as a crown over the twelve tribes (Commentary on the Apocalypse 12.1).
  • Primasius begins from Christ, in whom human nature is joined to the Wisdom of God. The Woman is the Church who has put on Christ and tramples every changeable thing, because she clings to the good that does not change: "for me, to be near God is my good" (Psalm 73:28). He adds that she is a sojourner here, and that since the human birth of Christ there has been no end of false teaching about this temple of his body. He names Valentinus, Bardesanes, Apollinaris, Nestorius, Eutyches and Timothy Aelurus, each teaching something different. Out of these controversies the faithful won their reward (Commentary on the Apocalypse 12.1).
  • St. Caesarius of Arles: the sun is the hope of the resurrection, when the just will shine like the sun (Matthew 13:43). The moon under her feet is the hypocrites and bad Christians she has to bear. The stars are the apostles (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 9).

The Woman as God's people before Christ. St. Victorinus reads her as the ancient Church of the patriarchs, prophets and apostles. The sun is the hope of the resurrection. The moon is the death of the saints' bodies, which wanes and is never wholly put out. The crown of twelve stars is the fathers from whom Christ took flesh (Commentary on the Apocalypse 12.1). See The birth pangs.

Source: unless another source is linked, the Fathers in this section are cited from Revelation, Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 12:1.

Where readers differ

  • Which reading comes first. Hippolytus reads the Church first; the modern liturgy and the popes quoted above read Mary first; many Scripture scholars begin with Israel. The Church has not ruled between them, and she uses all three. Paul VI's words are careful: the liturgy applies the sign to Mary "not without foundation." Disputed
  • The birth pangs. Verse 2 says the Woman "wailed aloud in pain as she labored to give birth." Those who read the Church or Israel first find this easy; those who read Mary first must explain it, as Pius X did when he read the pangs as her care for the children still to be born to God. See The birth pangs.
  • The Fathers after Hippolytus. Most read the Woman as the Church. Oecumenius reads her directly as the Mother of God, and Quodvultdeus as Mary who is a figure of the Church, as set out above. See also The Commentators.

An approved apparition

The image of Our Lady of Guadalupe (1531) shows a woman who stands before the sun, with the moon beneath her feet and stars on her mantle, and who is with child. The Church has approved the devotion and keeps her feast on December 12. An apparition adds nothing to the faith (CCC 67), but this one has taught generations to see Mary in John's sign. See The Woman in approved apparitions. Approved apparition (aid)

What it asks of us

A sign is given to be read. John is shown the Woman before he is shown the dragon, and clothed in God's light before any attack begins. The order is a mercy: we are meant to see who she is, and whose she is, before we see what rises against her. Every child of the Church is among "the rest of her offspring" (12:17). To look at the Woman is to learn where we come from and what we are meant to become.
