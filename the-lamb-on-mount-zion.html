Revelation 14:1–5

The Lamb on Mount Zion

After the dragon on the sand and the two beasts, John looks up and sees the Lamb standing on the mountain, and with him those who did not take the mark.

The text
"Then I looked and there was the Lamb standing on Mount Zion, and with him a hundred and forty-four thousand who had his name and his Father's name written on their foreheads… They were singing what seemed to be a new hymn before the throne… No one could learn this hymn except the hundred and forty-four thousand who had been ransomed from the earth. These are they who were not defiled with women; they are virgins and these are the ones who follow the Lamb wherever he goes. They have been ransomed as the firstfruits of the human race for God and the Lamb. On their lips no deceit has been found; they are unblemished" (Revelation 14:1–5).
In one sentence
This is the answer to chapter 13: against the beast's mark stands the Father's name, against the lie stands a people in whom no deceit is found, and against the dragon on the sand stands the Lamb on the mountain.
Authority
Scripture

Where this vision stands

Chapter 12 ended with the dragon standing on the sand of the sea (The dragon on the shore). Chapter 13 showed what he called up: a beast that all the world worships, and a mark without which no one can buy or sell. A reader who stopped there would think the dragon had won the earth.

John does not let him stop there. "Then I looked": and the very next sight is the Lamb. The notes of the New American Bible call it "a tender and consoling vision of the Lamb and his companions" (note on 14:1–5). Every detail answers something in chapter 13.

Chapter 13 Chapter 14
The dragon stands on the sand (12:18) The Lamb stands on Mount Zion (14:1)
The beast's name or number on forehead or hand (13:16–17) The Lamb's name and his Father's name on the forehead (14:1)
"All the inhabitants of the earth" worship the beast (13:8) A people "ransomed from the earth" (14:3)
The false prophet deceives by signs (13:14) "On their lips no deceit has been found" (14:5)
"Who can compare with the beast?" (13:4) A new hymn, sung before the throne (14:3)

Scripture

The words

  • Standing. The Lamb was first seen "standing" though "slain" (5:6). He stands as one who has been through death.
  • Mount Zion. The hill of Jerusalem where God set his king: "I myself have installed my king on Zion, my holy mountain" (Psalm 2:6). This is the psalm of the male child and the iron rod (12:5). The child caught up to the throne is here seen with his people.
  • Written on their foreheads. This is the seal of 7:3, now explained: the seal is a name. At the end of the book it is said of all the blessed: "They will look upon his face, and his name will be on their foreheads" (22:4).
  • Ransomed. Bought at a price. The song to the Lamb says how: "with your blood you purchased for God those from every tribe and tongue, people and nation" (5:9).

Mount Zion

The prophets had promised that a remnant would be gathered there: "Everyone who calls upon the name of the LORD will escape harm. For on Mount Zion there will be a remnant, as the LORD has said" (Joel 3:5). The Letter to the Hebrews says where Christians already stand: "You have approached Mount Zion and the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and countless angels in festal gathering, and the assembly of the firstborn enrolled in heaven… and Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant" (Hebrews 12:22–24).

Is John's Zion on earth or in heaven? The Lamb stands on the mountain, and the hymn is heard "from heaven… before the throne." The scene joins the two, as the liturgy does: the Church on earth, gathered around the Lamb, sings what heaven sings. Scripture

The hundred and forty-four thousand

They were first seen in chapter 7, sealed "on the foreheads of the servants of our God," twelve thousand from each tribe, and immediately afterward John saw "a great multitude, which no one could count, from every nation, race, people, and tongue" (7:3–9).

The notes of the New American Bible read the number as a symbol: "the square of twelve (the number of Israel's tribes) multiplied by a thousand, symbolic of the new Israel… that embraces people from every nation, race, people, and tongue" (note on 7:4–9); and here, "the 144,000 whose foreheads are sealed represent all Christian people" (note on 14:4). The Catechism lists them among those who take part in heaven's worship as "the new People of God (the one hundred and forty-four thousand)" (CCC 1138). Church teaching

The number, then, is not a limit on how many are saved. It is the people of God counted as complete: none missing. The seal and this number have pages of their own in Part 3 of this study.

The new hymn

"Sing to the LORD a new song" runs through the Psalms (33:3; 96:1; 98:1) and Isaiah (42:10), always in answer to a new act of God's deliverance. In Revelation the new song is the song to the Lamb who was slain (5:9).

"No one could learn this hymn except" those who had been ransomed. The song is not a secret kept from others. It is a song that can only be sung from experience, by those who have been bought back and who held fast. A theologian

"They are virgins"

This is the hardest line in the passage, and two readings must be kept apart.

The whole people, faithful to God. Throughout the Old Testament, and in Revelation itself, idolatry is called fornication and adultery (2:14, 20–22; 17:1–6). The New American Bible takes "virgins" in that sense: "metaphorically, because they never indulged in any idolatrous practices" (note on 14:4). St. Paul uses the same image of an entire local church: "I betrothed you to one husband to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ" (2 Corinthians 11:2). On this reading the 144,000 are all the faithful, men and women, married and unmarried, who did not give to the beast what belongs to God. This is the reading that fits the number as "all Christian people." A theologian

Those consecrated to Christ in virginity. The Church has also heard in these words the vocation of those who give up marriage for the kingdom. The Catechism uses the verse itself: "From the very beginning of the Church there have been men and women who have renounced the great good of marriage to follow the Lamb wherever he goes, to be intent on the things of the Lord, to seek to please him, and to go out to meet the Bridegroom who is coming" (CCC 1618). Such a life is "a powerful sign of the supremacy of the bond with Christ and of the ardent expectation of his return" (CCC 1619). Church teaching

The two readings are related as a sign to what it signifies. Consecrated virginity shows in one way of life what the whole Church is called to be toward Christ: his alone. The verse does not speak against marriage, which the same paragraph of the Catechism calls a "great good."

Follow, firstfruits, unblemished

  • "They follow the Lamb wherever he goes." It is the Gospel's word for discipleship, and it includes where the Lamb went: to the Cross.
  • "Firstfruits." The first sheaf of the harvest, offered to God as a pledge of the rest. "He willed to give us birth by the word of truth that we may be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures" (James 1:18). Christ himself is "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). The image looks ahead to the harvest at the end of this chapter (14:14–16).
  • "No deceit… unblemished." Zephaniah had said of the remnant on Zion: "They shall do no wrong and speak no lies; nor shall there be found in their mouths a deceitful tongue" (Zephaniah 3:13). "Unblemished" is the word for an animal fit for sacrifice. The note in the New American Bible gives the reason no lie is found in them: "because they did not deny Christ or do homage to the beast." Scripture

The Woman and the Lamb's companions

Chapter 12 ended with "the rest of her offspring, those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus" (12:17), and the dragon going off to make war on them. Chapter 14 shows the same people from the other side. The offspring the dragon hunts are the companions the Lamb keeps. Verse 12 of this chapter repeats their description almost word for word.

A reader who sees Mary in the Woman will recognize her at the head of this company: the Virgin, in whom no deceit was found, who followed the Lamb to the Cross. That is a reading by likeness, and the text does not make it. A theologian

What the Fathers said

The Fathers read this vision in more than one way, and their differences fall on the same two questions this page has already raised. A Father or Doctor

Who stands with the Lamb?

  • St. Victorinus (died about 304), in the oldest commentary we have, takes the 144,000 as those of the Jewish people who will come to faith at the end through the preaching of Elijah (Commentary on the Apocalypse 20.1).
  • Oecumenius sees in the Lamb on Zion "the conversion of Israel by faith in the last days," as St. Paul foretold (Romans 11:25–26). Yet he thinks these 144,000 are not the same company as in chapter 7, but people drawn from Israel and from the nations together (Commentary on the Apocalypse 14.1–5).
  • Andrew of Caesarea says the mountain is "not the ancient city, but the new city of the living God," and he too distinguishes this company from the tribes of chapter 7 (Commentary on the Apocalypse 14.1).
  • St. Bede reads them as the Church, and notices the contrast this page began with: "the beast stands on the sand of the sea, while the Lamb stands upon Mount Zion." The number, he says, stands for a multitude without limit, and the name on their foreheads answers the mark of the beast (Explanation of the Apocalypse 14.1).

Who are the "virgins"?

  • Consecrated virgins. St. Methodius calls the incarnate Word "chief Virgin" and leader of their choir (Symposium 1.5). St. Cyprian (The Dress of Virgins 4–5), St. Jerome (Against Jovinian 1.40) and St. Augustine (Holy Virginity) all take the verse of those who have given up marriage for Christ. Augustine asks what "follow" can mean but "imitate," and adds that married people too "can walk in his footsteps."
  • The whole Church. St. Caesarius of Arles: "we do not understand the 'virgins' to be only those who are chaste in the body," but "the whole church that holds to the pure faith," as in 2 Corinthians 11:2 (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 11). Bede records that Tyconius read it the same way. Caesarius warns that bodily virginity is of no profit if purity of heart is lost.

No one left out. Augustine, preaching on the martyrs, will not let anyone conclude that the Lamb's company is closed to them. The Lord's garden holds "the lilies of virgins, and the ivy of married people and the violets of widows," and no one need despair of his vocation, for "Christ suffered for all" (Sermon 304.2).

The new song. Oecumenius and Andrew say the song is known in the measure of a person's purity. Bede and Augustine add that those who cannot sing it will still hear it and rejoice in it without envy.

Source: the Fathers are cited from Revelation, Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 14:1–5.

Where readers differ

  • A literal number. Some groups outside the Catholic Church take 144,000 as an exact count of a special class. The Church does not: the number is symbolic, and the great multitude of 7:9 cannot be counted. Disputed
  • The same people as chapter 7? Most read the 144,000 of chapters 7 and 14 as one and the same, the whole people of God. Some distinguish them from the great multitude as Jewish believers from Gentile, or as the Church on earth from the Church in glory. Among the Fathers, Oecumenius and Andrew of Caesarea distinguish the two companies.
  • "Virgins," as above.
  • The Fathers differ among themselves, as set out above; see also The Commentators.

What it asks of us

The passage is addressed to people for whom chapter 13 is the daily view: the beast admired, the mark required. It asks them to look where John looked. The Lamb is standing, and he is not alone.

And it names what his companions have in common. They bear his name openly, where it can be seen. They are his and no one else's. They follow where he goes. And they do not lie. In a chapter about a deception that takes in the whole world, the last and simplest mark of the Lamb's people is that "on their lips no deceit has been found."
