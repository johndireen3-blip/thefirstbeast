How to Test a Reading of Revelation

The Church names no one and sets no date. She does ask her members to watch, to think, and to test what they conclude.

Home › How to test a reading

✓ Reviewed by Fr. John How this site is made

Is a Catholic allowed to form a view about what Revelation means for our own time? Yes, and more than allowed. Scripture asks for it: "Test everything; retain what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21). What the Church asks is that a reading be tested. These eleven questions are a way to do that.

Why this page exists

The Church has defined very little about the details of Revelation. She names no one as the beast and sets no date for the end. Some readers take that as a reason to stop thinking about the book. It is the opposite. The book itself says, "Wisdom is needed here" (Revelation 13:18). The Lord told his disciples, "stay awake!" (Matthew 24:42), and he asked the crowds: "why do you not know how to interpret the present time? Why do you not judge for yourselves what is right?" (Luke 12:56–57). A question the Church has left open is one she has left to the prayer, study and vigilance of her members.

The risk on the other side is real too. Confident names and dates have been announced in every century, and each failure has taught many people to ignore the book altogether. The remedy is not silence. It is to test, as the people of Beroea did, who "examined the scriptures daily to determine whether these things were so" (Acts 17:11).

The text

  1. Does it begin with what the text says? Establish what the passage means in its own words and setting before building anything on it (CCC 109–110, 116).
  2. Does it read Scripture with Scripture? Revelation is woven from Daniel, Ezekiel, Exodus and the Gospels. A reading should fit the whole (CCC 112).
  3. Does it account for what the text meant first? John wrote to seven real churches under Rome. A reading for today should build on that first meaning, not skip it.

The Church

  1. Has it listened to the Fathers and the Church? Where they agree on a matter of faith, follow them. Where they differ, say so; that is where a reader is free (CCC 113).
  2. Does it agree with the faith as a whole? No true reading of one passage contradicts the Creed (CCC 114).
  3. Does it avoid dates? "It is not for you to know times or seasons" (CCC 673, quoting Acts 1:7). A reading may say what to watch for. It may not say when.

The evidence

  1. Can its evidence be checked? If it points to events, are they on the record, with sources another person can verify?
  2. Does it weigh words and deeds, and never souls? Only God judges a heart. A reading may examine what someone has publicly said and done. It may not pronounce on his salvation, and it may never use a person's religion or people as evidence.
  3. Does it give the case against? State the strongest objection, and the other readings as those who hold them would state them.

The one who holds it

  1. Is its confidence graded? Say whether a thing is possible, probable or certain, and never call settled what the Church has not settled.
  2. What fruit does it bear? A sound reading of Revelation leads to endurance, faith, hope and charity. One that leads to fear, contempt or despair has gone wrong somewhere, whatever its arguments.

Then what?

Hold your reading humbly. Share it with people able to test it. Be ready to revise it, and submit it to the judgment of the Church. That is not a way of discouraging thought. It is how thinking is done in the Church, and it is how the Fathers themselves held their different readings of this book.

Keep watch

Take one passage that has caught your attention, and one view of it you have heard or formed. Put it through the eleven questions in writing. Which questions does it pass easily, and which does it struggle with?
