Revelation 12:18–13:1

The Dragon on the Shore

The last verse of chapter 12 and the first of chapter 13 are one scene: the dragon stands at the edge of the sea, and a beast comes up out of it.

The text
"It took its position on the sand of the sea. Then I saw a beast come out of the sea with ten horns and seven heads; on its horns were ten diadems, and on its heads blasphemous names" (Revelation 12:18–13:1).
In one sentence
Unable to reach the Son or destroy the Woman, the dragon calls up another to make his war for him; chapter 13 is chapter 12 continued.
Authority
Scripture

A second sentence divided by a chapter number

This study began with one chapter break that hides a connection, between the ark and the Woman (The ark in heaven). It reaches another here. In some Bibles the dragon's taking his stand is the last verse of chapter 12 (12:18); in others it is the opening of chapter 13. Either way John wrote it as one movement: the dragon goes off to make war on the Woman's children (12:17), stands on the shore (12:18), and the beast rises (13:1).

The beasts of chapter 13 are therefore not a new subject. They are how the dragon carries out the war of 12:17.

"It stood" or "I stood"

The oldest manuscripts read estathē, "it stood": the dragon. Many later manuscripts add one letter and read estathēn, "I stood": John. The notes of the New American Bible record it: "many later manuscripts and versions read 'I took my position,' thus connecting the sentence to the following paragraph" (note on 12:18). Older English Bibles, which follow the later manuscripts, have John standing on the sand.

The difference matters for the picture. If John stands there, he is only a spectator. If the dragon stands there, the beast comes at his summons, and the verse that follows explains the relation: "To it the dragon gave its own power and throne, along with great authority" (13:2). A theologian

The words

  • Took its position. The same verb as in 12:4, where the dragon "stood before the woman." Then he waited for a birth, to devour it. Now he waits for a rising, to use it.
  • The sand of the sea. The boundary between the land and the deep.
  • Come out of. The Greek is anabainon, "coming up," as from below. Revelation uses the same word of "the beast that comes up from the abyss" (11:7; 17:8).

Scripture

The sea

In Daniel. John's scene is Daniel's: "The four winds of heaven stirred up the great sea, from which emerged four immense beasts" (Daniel 7:2–3). Daniel's beasts are kingdoms. John's one beast gathers the features of all four (13:2).

As the peoples in turmoil. Later the angel explains the waters to John: they "represent large numbers of peoples, nations, and tongues" (17:15). Isaiah says, "the wicked are like the tossing sea which cannot be still" (Isaiah 57:20). The beast comes up out of the restlessness of the nations.

As the deep. In the Old Testament the sea is the place of the dragon himself, Leviathan (Isaiah 27:1; Psalm 74:13–14). The notes of the New American Bible, at 12:15, say "the serpent is depicted as the sea monster." He stands at the edge of his own element and draws from it something made in his likeness: seven heads and ten horns, as he has (12:3; 13:1).

As a thing God has bounded. The sea in Scripture is never a rival to God. "I made the sandy shore the sea's limit, which by eternal decree it may not overstep. Toss though it may, it is to no avail" (Jeremiah 5:22). "Thus far shall you come but no farther, and here shall your proud waves stop" (Job 38:11). The dragon stands on that very limit. And at the end of the book, when the new creation comes, "the sea was no more" (21:1). Scripture

What the dragon cannot do

The scene shows how he works, and the limits of it.

  • He does not create. The beast is assembled from what Daniel had already seen, and its heads and horns copy his own. Evil makes nothing new; it imitates and distorts.
  • He works through others. Thrown out of heaven, he does not rule the earth in his own person. He gives "power and throne" to a creature of the sea, and soon a second beast rises from the land to serve the first (13:11–12). Many commentators see here a counterfeit of God's own way of acting: the dragon, the beast and the false prophet set against the Father, the Son and the Spirit. A theologian
  • Even this is permitted. Chapter 13 says again and again that the beast "was given" what it has and "was allowed" to do what it does (13:5, 7, 14, 15). The Catechism: Satan's action "is permitted by divine providence which with strength and gentleness guides human and cosmic history" (CCC 395). Church teaching

The crowns

There is a detail easy to miss. The dragon has seven heads and ten horns, with seven diadems on the heads (12:3). The beast has ten horns and seven heads, with ten diadems on the horns (13:1). The likeness is exact, and the crowns have moved from the heads to the horns. Whatever the heads and horns are, authority has passed from one set to the other. What they are is the subject of the partner site.

Where this study hands on

From here the story of the two beasts runs to the end of chapter 13, and it is studied verse by verse on The First Beast: the beast from the sea, its marks, and the response asked of the faithful.

This study takes up the thread again at chapter 14, where John looks away from the shore and sees the Lamb standing on Mount Zion. The two pictures are meant to be seen together: the dragon standing on sand, and the Lamb standing on the mountain.

What the Fathers said

The dragon on the sand. St. Bede reads "he stood on the sand of the sea" of the dragon, and takes the sand for the multitude of people, like the chaff "which the wind drives away" (Psalm 1:4). When the devil means to raise plots and wars, he takes his stand on that crowd which is always ready to take up his plans (Explanation of the Apocalypse 12.17–18). A Father or Doctor

What rises from the sea. The Fathers read the sea as this page does, and differ on the beast.

  • The antichrist, from the sea of this life. St. Andrew of Caesarea follows St. Methodius and St. Hippolytus: the beast is the antichrist, coming up out of the rough and troubled sea of human life. The horns and heads he shares with the dragon show their union. Andrew also reads them of earthly rule, divided into ten at the end, after seven successive kingdoms (Commentary on the Apocalypse 13.1). St. Irenaeus, long before, says that God, foreseeing all, lets this man come at the proper time, so that those who refused the love of the truth may be judged; in him all apostasy is gathered into one (2 Thessalonians 2:10–12) (Against Heresies 5.28.2).
  • A chief of the demons. Oecumenius counts three beasts: the dragon in heaven, who is Satan; this one from the sea; and the one from the earth, whom he takes for the antichrist. The second he thinks is the leader of the other demons under Satan, the one who appears in the book of Job as going to and fro upon the earth (Job 1:7). For him to come up from the sea is a promotion: he is raised up by the unstable lives of those who chose him for their master (Commentary on the Apocalypse 13.1–4). Andrew reports this reading and does not follow it.
  • A people. Tyconius: John sees "a people arising from a people." In this book the beast is sometimes the devil, sometimes his body, sometimes one head of it, sometimes its leaders, and the reader must judge passage by passage. Here it is the body of the devil, which bears a blasphemous name because it wishes to be taken for God (Commentary on the Apocalypse 13.1). St. Caesarius of Arles says the same: all the ungodly, who are the devil's body (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 10).

Leopard, bear and lion (13:2). St. Victorinus: the antichrist's kingdom will be a mixture of many nations; its feet are its leaders, and its mouth is armed for bloodshed (Commentary on the Apocalypse 13.1). Andrew names the kingdoms of Daniel: the Greeks, the Persians and the Babylonians, over all of which the antichrist will rule as a king of the Romans. Oecumenius reads the animals of the devil's character: quick to change his plans, steady in pursuing them, and prowling like a roaring lion (1 Peter 5:8).

"The dragon gave it his own power." This is the point of this page, and the Fathers do not let it pass. Without question, says Oecumenius, the power of the apostate dragon dwells in these deceits, for he is their instigator and teacher. Tyconius: as the soul of a just person is the seat of wisdom, so the enemy gives his throne to those he possesses most fully and uses to mislead others.

The head that seemed slain (13:3). Tyconius sees a counterfeit of the Lord's resurrection, offered so that the beast may be received in place of Christ. Andrew gives two readings: one of the antichrist's lieutenants will seem to be raised by sorcery, as Simon the magician pretended before St. Peter; or the Roman Empire, wounded by division, will seem healed under one ruler, as in the time of Augustus. Victorinus thought of Nero, who took his own life, and whom some in the early Church expected to return. Caesarius reads the wound of heresies, struck down by the testimony of Scripture and reviving. Oecumenius reads it of the past: the devil was wounded by the true worship offered in Israel, and healed when Israel too fell into the idolatry the prophets lament.

Forty-two months, and war on the saints (13:5–7). Oecumenius: beside the unending ages, all time is short. The proud mouth is Isaiah's "I will ascend to the heavens" (Isaiah 14:13–14). God's dwelling, which the beast blasphemes, is the holy angels (Oecumenius), the Word's dwelling in the flesh and among the saints (Andrew), or the saints themselves (Caesarius). Primasius finds the number in the Church's own life: the ten commandments kept in the four quarters of the world make forty, and the two commandments of love make forty-two. Why is the beast allowed to conquer? The Lord told Pilate that he would have no power unless it were given from above (John 19:11). If Christ gave his persecutors power over himself for a time, it is no wonder that he allows it over his Church; and the beast conquers only that part which can be conquered (Commentary on the Apocalypse 13.5–7).

The book of life, and the endurance of the saints (13:8–10). Oecumenius notes how carefully the vision limits "all who dwell on earth": there were always some who kept themselves from the devil's worship, Job and Melchizedek among the nations and the prophets in Israel. Their names are written in heaven, which is what the Lord told his disciples to rejoice in (Luke 10:20). Andrew: those who harm their neighbor are made captives of the one who conquered them; those of pure faith and firm endurance are written in the book. He ends with a prayer that we may count the sufferings of this time as nothing beside the glory to come (Romans 8:18) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 13.9–10).

Source: unless another source is linked, the Fathers in this section are cited from Revelation, Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture, New Testament XII, ed. William C. Weinrich (InterVarsity Press, 2005), on Revelation 12:17–13:10.

Where readers differ

  • The text, "it stood" or "I stood," as above. Modern editions and the Church's current translations read "it stood."
  • Where the verse belongs, at the end of chapter 12 or the beginning of 13. The numbering differs between editions; the sense does not.
  • The sea, as the nations, the abyss, or (for the first-century reading) the Mediterranean, across which Rome's power came to the churches of Asia. These are not exclusive. Disputed

What it asks of us

To read chapter 13 in the light of chapter 12. A reader who meets the beasts without the dragon behind them may think the trouble is only political, and look only for political remedies. A reader who meets them without the Woman, the child on the throne and the hymn of 12:10–12 may think the beasts are winning. John gives the order on purpose: first the victory, then the war that follows from it.

It also asks for sobriety about where our footing is. The dragon stands on sand. "Everyone who listens to these words of mine but does not act on them will be like a fool who built his house on sand" (Matthew 7:26).
