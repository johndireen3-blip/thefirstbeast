Revelation 12:2

The Birth Pangs

The Woman cries out in the pain of labor. It is the hardest verse for those who read her as Mary, and one of the richest when its Old Testament sources are heard.

The text
"She was with child and wailed aloud in pain as she labored to give birth" (Revelation 12:2).
In one sentence
The pangs are the long travail of God's people for the Messiah, and the Church has also read them of Mary: not of Bethlehem, but of her motherhood of the faithful, which came to its hour at the Cross.
Authority
Scripture

The words

The Greek uses two verbs. The first, ōdinousa, is the ordinary word for a woman in labor. The second, basanizomenē, is not. It means "tormented" or "tortured," and elsewhere in Revelation it is used of the torments of judgment (9:5; 14:10; 20:10). Scripture scholars point out that it is not a word used elsewhere in the Bible for childbirth. John has chosen a word that says more than the pain of a delivery. A theologian

The Old Testament behind it

The prophets had a fixed picture: God's people as a woman in labor, suffering toward a birth that only God could bring about.

  • The sentence on Eve. "In pain you shall bring forth children" (Genesis 3:16), spoken one verse after the promise that the woman's offspring would strike the serpent's head.
  • Labor without a child. "As a woman about to give birth writhes and cries out in pain, so were we before you, LORD. We conceived and writhed in pain, giving birth only to wind; salvation we have not achieved for the earth" (Isaiah 26:17–18).
  • Zion's travail in exile. "Writhe, go into labor, O daughter Zion, like a woman giving birth... To Babylon you shall go, there you shall be rescued" (Micah 4:10). And of the Messiah: the Lord will give them up "until the time when she who is to give birth has borne" (Micah 5:2).
  • The birth that comes from God. "Before she is in labor, she gives birth; before her pangs come upon her, she delivers a male child... Shall I bring a mother to the point of birth, and yet not let her child be born? says the LORD" (Isaiah 66:7, 9).

The Lord himself used the image for his Passion and its outcome: "When a woman is in labor, she is in anguish because her hour has arrived; but when she has given birth to a child, she no longer remembers the pain because of her joy that a child has been born into the world. So you also are now in anguish. But I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice" (John 16:21–22). Scripture

The question about Mary

The Church confesses that Mary remained a virgin "even in the act of giving birth to the Son of God made man," and that Christ's birth "did not diminish his mother's virginal integrity but sanctified it" (CCC 499; Lumen Gentium 57). Church teaching The Roman Catechism of 1566 adds, with the common teaching of the Fathers, that she who was free of Eve's sin was free of Eve's sentence, and bore her Son without pain (Part I, article 3). That she felt no pain at Bethlehem has not been solemnly defined, but it is the Church's long-held teaching. Church teaching

So if the Woman is Mary, the pangs of verse 2 cannot simply be the pain of the Nativity. Four readings have been given.

Four readings

1. The travail of God's people for the Messiah. This is the reading closest to the prophets. St. Victorinus saw in the Woman "the ancient Church of fathers, and prophets, and saints, and apostles," and in her cries the groans and torments of its longing until Christ came (Commentary on the Apocalypse, on 12:1–2). The notes of the New American Bible say: "Because of Eve's sin, the woman gives birth in distress and pain" (note on 12:2). On this reading the pangs belong to Israel, and Mary, who is Israel's daughter, stands at the end of them. A Father or Doctor

2. The Church in labor with her children. St. Hippolytus: the words "mean that the Church will not cease to bear from her heart the Word that is persecuted by the unbelieving in the world" (On Christ and Antichrist 61). St. Paul speaks the same way of himself: "My children, for whom I am again in labor until Christ be formed in you!" (Galatians 4:19). Benedict XVI calls her "the Church which in all ages, with great suffering, brings forth Christ ever anew" (General Audience, August 23, 2006). A Father or Doctor

3. Mary's motherhood of us. Pope St. Pius X read the verse of Mary and asked what birth it could mean: "John therefore saw the Most Holy Mother of God already in eternal happiness, yet travailing in a mysterious childbirth. What birth was it? Surely it was the birth of us who, still in exile, are yet to be generated to the perfect charity of God, and to eternal happiness. And the birth pains show the love and desire with which the Virgin from heaven above watches over us" (Ad Diem Illum 24). Church teaching

4. Mary at the Cross. She bore her Son without pain; she became our mother in great pain. Simeon had told her, "you yourself a sword will pierce" (Luke 2:35). At Calvary she stood "grieving exceedingly with her only begotten Son, uniting herself with a maternal heart with His sacrifice," and there she was given "as a mother to His disciple" (Lumen Gentium 58; John 19:26–27). Pope St. John Paul II calls this her "new motherhood," "born from the heart of the Paschal Mystery" (Redemptoris Mater 23, 44). Church teaching

Some Catholic Scripture scholars join this fourth reading to the text of verse 5. The child is "caught up to God and his throne" with nothing said of his life between: the birth John sees may be the birth of the Messiah into glory through his death and resurrection, the "hour" of the woman in John 16:21. On that reading the pangs are the Passion, shared by his mother and by the little flock of his disciples. It is offered as a theologian's reading, and it fits the unusual word tormented. A theologian

How the readings fit together

They are not rivals. The first two say whose pain it is in history: Israel's before Christ, the Church's after him. The last two say how the verse can be true of Mary without touching her virginity or the painless birth: she is in travail for the Body, not for the Head. Pius X's reading and the reading from the Cross are the same motherhood seen at two moments.

This is also the moment where the Woman as Mary and the Woman as the Church meet. The Catechism says of Mary that she is mother of the faithful, "in whose generation and formation she co-operates with a mother's love" (CCC 501). The Church does the same work by preaching and Baptism (CCC 507). One labor is being described.

Where readers differ

  • Those who read the Woman only as Israel or the Church see no difficulty here, and some of them think the Marian reading of this verse forced. Those who read her first as Mary answer with Pius X or with the Cross. The Church has not settled which of the four readings is John's own. Disputed
  • The reading of verse 5 as the Paschal birth is held by some scholars and not by others; most commentators take the birth as Bethlehem and the catching up as the Ascension, as the notes of the New American Bible do ("reference to Christ's ascension"). Disputed

What it asks of us

"All creation is groaning in labor pains even until now; and not only that, but we ourselves" (Romans 8:22–23). The verse tells us that the suffering of God's people is not the pain of dying but the pain of giving birth: it is going somewhere, and God has promised the child. "Shall I bring a mother to the point of birth, and yet not let her child be born? says the LORD" (Isaiah 66:9).
