REVELATION 18:01–08

Depart from her, my people.

Depart from her, my people.

01John sees another angel come down from heaven with great authority, and the earth is lit up by his splendor.

02He cries out in a mighty voice: Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great! She has become a haunt of demons, a prison for every unclean spirit and every unclean and loathsome bird.

03All the nations have drunk the wine of her passionate immorality; the kings of the earth were unfaithful with her, and the merchants of the earth grew rich from her excess.

04Then John hears another voice from heaven: Come out of her, my people, so that you do not share in her sins or receive a share of her plagues,

05for her sins are piled up to the sky, and God has remembered her crimes.

06Pay her back as she has paid others; give her double for her deeds; in the cup she mixed, mix her a double portion.

07As much as she gave herself glory and luxury, give her the same in torment and grief. In her heart she says: I sit as a queen; I am no widow and will never know mourning.

08So in a single day her plagues will come, death, mourning and famine, and she will be consumed by fire; for mighty is the Lord God who judges her.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Chapter 18 is a funeral lament for a city that has not yet fallen. The USCCB notes describe it as a dirge over Rome written as though its fall were already past, in language drawn from the Old Testament oracles against Babylon, Tyre and Nineveh (Isa 13; 23; Jer 50–51; Ezek 26–27). Its harshness, the notes explain, expresses God’s holiness and justice. Rome is condemned for its idolatry and for persecuting the Church. The call “come out of her” echoes Jeremiah 51:45. It does not mean leaving a place on the map: the notes say it calls Christians to separate themselves from the city’s sinful ways. Babylon’s boast, “I sit as a queen,” repeats the pride condemned in Isaiah 47:7–8.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 18:09–19

In one hour.

In one hour.

09The kings of the earth who shared her immorality and luxury will weep and mourn over her when they see the smoke of her burning.

10Standing far off for fear of her torment, they will say: Alas, alas, great city, mighty Babylon! In one hour your judgment has come.

11The merchants of the earth weep and grieve over her, because no one buys their cargo any more:

12cargo of gold, silver, precious stones and pearls; fine linen, purple, silk and scarlet; every kind of scented wood, every article of ivory, costly wood, bronze, iron and marble;

13cinnamon, spice, incense, myrrh and frankincense; wine, olive oil, fine flour and wheat; cattle and sheep, horses and chariots; and slaves, that is, human lives.

14The fruit your soul longed for has left you; all your splendor and glitter are lost, never to be found again.

15The merchants who grew rich from her will stand far off for fear of her torment, weeping and mourning,

16and saying: Alas, alas, great city, dressed in fine linen, purple and scarlet, adorned with gold, jewels and pearls!

17In one hour all this wealth has been laid waste. Every ship’s captain, every traveler by sea, sailors and all who make their living from the sea stand far off

18and cry out as they watch the smoke of her burning: What city was ever like the great city?

19They throw dust on their heads and cry out, weeping and mourning: Alas, alas, great city, where all who had ships at sea grew rich from her wealth! In one hour she has been ruined.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Three groups mourn, and each mourns for itself. The kings lose their partner in power, the merchants their market, the sailors their trade. The USCCB notes point out the irony that the merchants weep less for the city than for their lost business. The long cargo list, modeled on Ezekiel’s lament over Tyre (Ezek 27), moves from gold and jewels down to grain and cattle, and ends with “human beings,” literally “bodies and souls of men” (compare Ezek 27:13). An economy that trades in persons has reached the bottom of the list. Three times the mourners say “in one hour”: what seemed eternal is gone in a moment.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 18:20–24

Never again.

Never again.

20Rejoice over her, heaven, you holy ones, apostles and prophets, for God has judged her on your behalf.

21Then a mighty angel picks up a stone like a great millstone and throws it into the sea, saying: With such violence will Babylon the great city be thrown down, and it will never be found again.

22The sound of harpists and musicians, flute players and trumpeters will never be heard in you again; no craftsman of any trade will be found in you again; the sound of the millstone will never be heard in you again;

23the light of a lamp will never shine in you again; the voice of bridegroom and bride will never be heard in you again. Your merchants were the great ones of the earth, and by your sorcery all nations were led astray.

24In her was found the blood of prophets and holy ones, and of all who have been slain on the earth.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The millstone sign comes from Jeremiah 51:63–64, where a scroll of judgment is tied to a stone and thrown into the Euphrates: so shall Babylon sink. Jesus used the same image for those who lead little ones astray (Matt 18:6). The silence that follows is the saddest passage in the book: no music, no craft, no grinding of grain, no lamp, no wedding. A city built on idolatry and violence loses even its ordinary human joys. The last verse names the deepest charge: Babylon is guilty of the blood of the prophets and the holy ones. The wedding voices that fall silent here will be heard again in heaven at the wedding of the Lamb (19:7–9).

WHAT SEEMS ETERNAL FALLS IN AN HOUR
01

REVELATION 18:2

Fallen, fallen is Babylon

Isaiah’s cry over the ancient city (Isa 21:9), already heard in 14:8. The proud city becomes a ruin haunted by wild creatures, like Babylon in Isaiah 13:21 and Jeremiah 50:39.

Read Isaiah 21
02

REVELATION 18:4

Come out of her, my people

Jeremiah urged the exiles to flee Babylon before its fall (Jer 51:45). For Christians the exodus is moral: to live in the world without adopting its idols, its greed or its contempt for the poor (Rom 12:2).

Read Jeremiah 51
03

REVELATION 18:7

I sit as a queen

The boast of Babylon in Isaiah 47:7–8, who thought she would never be a widow. Pride that believes itself untouchable is the surest sign that its hour is near.

Read Isaiah 47
04

REVELATION 18:11–13

The merchants’ cargo

Modeled on the lament for Tyre (Ezek 27). The list ends with human lives: Rome’s wealth rested on the slave trade. The Church teaches that any system treating persons as merchandise offends human dignity (CCC 2414).

Read Ezekiel 27
05

REVELATION 18:21

The millstone in the sea

From Jeremiah 51:63–64: a stone thrown into the river as a sign that Babylon will sink and not rise. A millstone cannot float. What God throws down stays down.

Read Jeremiah 51
READ WITH CARE

Chapter 18 has been applied to many modern cities and nations, and some readers search the news for the “one hour” in which a particular economy will collapse. The USCCB notes read the lament as Rome’s fall, written in the symbolic language of the prophets, not as a literal report. Its message is timeless: every society that builds itself on idolatry, luxury and the exploitation of persons stands under the same judgment. Nor is the call to “rejoice” a call to gloat. Heaven rejoices that justice is done for the victims, not in suffering for its own sake.

01 / 03

COMING OUT & CONVERSION

What do I need to come out of?

God calls his people out of Babylon’s sins, not out of the world. What habit, purchase or way of thinking do I share with Babylon that I could leave behind this week?

REVELATION 18:4

GOODS & PERSONS

Who pays the price for what I buy?

Babylon’s cargo ends with human lives. Can I learn where one thing I buy regularly comes from, and choose in a way that respects the workers who made it?

REVELATION 18:13

TREASURE & HEART

What would I mourn if it were gone in an hour?

The merchants weep for lost markets. If my possessions vanished in a single hour, what would remain? Where is my true treasure (Matt 6:19–21)?

THE HEART OF THE CHAPTER

WHERE IT FITS

From witness to reign.

Chapter 18 completes the picture begun in chapter 17: there the harlot was revealed, here her city falls. Its laments are heard from the earth, by kings, merchants and sailors; its final verses turn to heaven, which rejoices. That rejoicing bursts into song at the start of chapter 19, with the great Alleluia and the announcement of the wedding of the Lamb. The silence of Babylon’s weddings (18:23) is answered by the wedding feast of the Lamb (19:9).
