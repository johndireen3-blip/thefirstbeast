REVELATION 19:01–05

Salvation, glory and might.

Salvation, glory and might.

01After this John hears what sounds like the roar of a vast crowd in heaven: Alleluia! Salvation, glory and power belong to our God,

02for his judgments are true and just. He has judged the great harlot who corrupted the earth with her immorality, and he has avenged on her the blood of his servants.

03A second time they cry: Alleluia! The smoke from her rises forever and ever.

04The twenty-four elders and the four living creatures fall down and worship God, who sits on the throne, saying: Amen. Alleluia!

05A voice comes from the throne: Praise our God, all you his servants, you who fear him, small and great.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Chapter 19 completes the section on Babylon (17:1–19:10) with heaven’s answer to the laments of chapter 18. The earth mourned; heaven sings. The USCCB notes observe that “Alleluia,” Hebrew for “Praise the Lord,” appears in the New Testament only here, though it rings through the Psalms (Ps 113–118, 146–150) and Jewish worship. The great multitude, the elders and the living creatures, all the worshipers of chapters 4–7, unite in one liturgy. The Church still sings this Alleluia: it is the song of Easter and of every Gospel acclamation, and the canticle of Revelation 19 is prayed at Sunday Evening Prayer.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 19:06–10

His bride has made herself ready.

His bride has made herself ready.

06Then John hears what sounds like a great crowd, like rushing waters and mighty thunder: Alleluia! The Lord our God, the almighty, has begun to reign.

07Let us rejoice and be glad and give him glory, for the wedding day of the Lamb has come, and his bride has made herself ready.

08She has been given fine linen, bright and clean, to wear. (The fine linen is the righteous deeds of the holy ones.)

09The angel says to John: Write this: Blessed are those who have been called to the wedding feast of the Lamb. And he adds: These are true words of God.

10John falls at the angel’s feet to worship him, but the angel says: Do not do that! I am a fellow servant with you and your brothers who hold to the testimony of Jesus. Worship God. For the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The wedding of the Lamb, the USCCB notes explain, symbolizes the beginning of God’s reign, and the bride is the Church. Scripture often describes God’s covenant with his people as a marriage (Hos 2:16–22; Isa 54:5–6), which is why idolatry is called adultery, and why Babylon was pictured as a harlot. Jesus spoke of the kingdom as a wedding feast (Matt 22:1–14) and called himself the Bridegroom (Mark 2:19). The bride’s linen is “given” to her, yet it is her righteous deeds: grace and good works together. Catholics hear verse 9 at every Mass, adapted in the invitation to Communion: “Blessed are those called to the supper of the Lamb.” The angel’s refusal of worship is a lesson for every age: only God is to be adored.

REVELATION 19:11–21

The Word of God.

The Word of God.

11John sees heaven opened, and there is a white horse. Its rider is called Faithful and True, and he judges and wages war with justice.

12His eyes are like a flame of fire, and on his head are many crowns. He has a name written that no one knows but himself.

13He wears a robe dipped in blood, and his name is called the Word of God.

14The armies of heaven, dressed in pure white linen, follow him on white horses.

15From his mouth comes a sharp sword to strike the nations. He will shepherd them with an iron rod, and he treads the wine press of the fury of God the almighty.

16On his robe and on his thigh he has a name written: King of kings and Lord of lords.

17Then John sees an angel standing in the sun, who cries out to all the birds flying overhead: Come, gather for God’s great supper,

18to eat the flesh of kings, commanders and the mighty, of horses and their riders, the flesh of all, free and slave, small and great.

19John sees the beast and the kings of the earth with their armies gathered to make war against the rider and his army.

20The beast is captured, and with it the false prophet who performed signs on its behalf and deceived those who took the beast’s mark and worshiped its image. The two are thrown alive into the lake of fire burning with sulfur.

21The rest are killed by the sword that comes from the rider’s mouth, and all the birds gorge on their flesh.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The USCCB notes describe the rider as a symbolic picture of the exalted Christ, who with the armies of heaven overcomes the beast and its followers. His name, “the Word of God,” connects him with the Word who was God in the prologue of John’s Gospel (John 1:1, 14), and his robe is dipped in blood before any battle begins: his own blood, shed on the Cross (compare 5:9; 7:14). His only weapon is the sword from his mouth (Isa 11:4; Heb 4:12). The battle announced at Armageddon (16:16) turns out to be no battle at all: the beast and the false prophet are simply seized. The grim “great supper of God” (Ezek 39:17–20) is the dark counterpart to the wedding supper of the Lamb: every person is invited to one feast or the other.

01

REVELATION 19:1–6

Alleluia

Hebrew for “Praise the LORD,” the refrain of the Hallel psalms (Ps 113–118) that Jesus likely sang at the Last Supper (Matt 26:30). Heaven’s Alleluia is the Church’s Easter song.

Read Psalm 113
02

REVELATION 19:7

The Bride of the Lamb

God promised to espouse his people forever (Hos 2:21–22). Paul calls Christian marriage a sign of Christ and the Church (Eph 5:25–32). The Church is the Bride; the harlot Babylon was her counterfeit.

Read Ephesians 5
03

REVELATION 19:8

Fine linen, bright and clean

The linen is given, yet it is the saints’ righteous deeds: grace bears fruit in good works (Eph 2:8–10). The baptismal white garment is the first gift of this wedding clothing.

Read Matthew 22
04

REVELATION 19:9

The supper of the Lamb

The fourth beatitude of the book. The Mass adapts it at the invitation to Communion: every Eucharist is a foretaste of the wedding feast (CCC 1329, 1402).

Read our page on Revelation and the Mass
05

REVELATION 19:13, 15

The Word and the sword

Christ conquers by his word, the sword of his mouth (Isa 11:4; Heb 4:12), and his robe is red with his own blood. The Lamb’s victory was won on the Cross, not on a battlefield.

Read John 1
READ WITH CARE

The rider on the white horse is often read as a description of a literal, armed Second Coming in which Christ leads a military campaign at the end of time, and verses 17–21 have been used to picture a great final battle in the Middle East. The USCCB notes read the rider as a symbol of the exalted Christ. His only weapon is his word, his robe is stained with his own blood, and the enemy is captured without a fight. The Church professes that Christ “will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead,” but Revelation’s imagery describes the meaning of that victory, not its military details.

WHERE IT FITS

From witness to reign.

Chapter 19 is the turning point from judgment to fulfillment. Its first half closes the Babylon section begun in chapter 17, answering the earth’s laments with heaven’s Alleluia and replacing the harlot with the Bride. Its second half begins the final visions: the rider fulfills the harvest of 14:14–20 and the gathering at Armageddon (16:16) and defeats two of the three enemies of chapters 12–13, the beast and the false prophet. The third, the dragon, is dealt with in chapter 20. The Bride announced here appears in full glory as the new Jerusalem in chapter 21.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or go on to the next chapter and come back to it.

The first five rows below, from the Alleluia to the angel who refuses worship, are drawn in full from the Ancient Christian Commentary volume. The last three are a summary. The Fathers on the rider and on the capture of the beast are set out in full on The Woman and the Ark: The Rider Called Faithful and True and The Beast and the False Prophet Taken.

01

REVELATION 19:1–3

“Alleluia!” Salvation and glory belong to God

Oecumenius: the great multitude in heaven is the holy angels, whose ranks are without number. One of the fathers said that the ninety-nine sheep that did not stray are the angels, and the one that strayed is the whole human race. “Alleluia” (“Hallelujah”) is a Hebrew word that means “praise God” or “sing to God.” They sing in thanksgiving for the just judgment on the spiritual Babylon. They sing it a second time, and later a third. By this threefold Alleluia they give glory to the holy Trinity, who is God. The smoke shows that the city is wholly in flames, for smoke is the sign of fire (Commentary on the Apocalypse 19.1–5).

St. Andrew of Caesarea: “Alleluia” is divine praise, and “Amen” means “truly” or “let it be so.” The praise belongs to the angelic powers and to humankind together, and it is sent up three times for the three Persons of the one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit. God has avenged the blood of his servants on Babylon, and has done good even to her inhabitants by bringing sin to an end through its punishment. The smoke that rises for ever shows that the punishment will not be forgotten, or that she who has been punished in part will be punished eternally in the age to come (Commentary on the Apocalypse 19.1–4).

Tyconius: while the Church remains in her joyful praise, the smoke of the ruin of the wicked remains as well. Fire begins in smoke, and there is smoke as long as there is fire. The Lord gave an example of both in the poor man and the rich man (Luke 16:19–30) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 19.3).

Apringius of Beja: the saints exult over the fall of the traitorous city. This is the repayment of evildoers and the rewarding of the good, of which Daniel spoke: some shall awake to everlasting life, and others to reproach and everlasting disgrace (Daniel 12:2) (Tractate on the Apocalypse 19.1–3).

St. Caesarius of Arles: this is the voice of the Church when the separation has taken place and the wicked have gone out from her. Babylon and the harlot are to be understood as lustful, adulterous and arrogant people, and whoever wants to avoid these punishments should not want to commit such sins. It is not the smoke of a burned city that rises for ever. It is the smoke of people who remain in their arrogance. The text says that it “goes up,” in the present. In this age Babylon is always going to destruction and burning in part, as Jerusalem is always passing into paradise in the saints who leave this world. The Lord showed it in the poor man and the rich man (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 18).

St. Bede: two deeds of the harlot are named: she corrupted herself with evil deeds, and she persecuted the good. In these two every kind of transgression is included (Explanation of the Apocalypse 19.2).

02

REVELATION 19:4–5

“Amen. Alleluia!” Praise from small and great

Oecumenius: the elders and the living creatures say “Amen” to show that they agree with the praise offered by the angels. Translated from Hebrew into Greek, “Amen” means “let it be.” The small and the great are those who are greater in holiness and those who are outstanding in it (19.1–5).

Primasius: the praise is commanded and then it is given. This shows that God has accepted the praise of his elect as pleasing, and that it is to last for ever. The great in the Church are those of whom the Apostle says that he speaks wisdom among the mature (1 Corinthians 2:6), and of whom the Lord says that whoever obeys and teaches the commandments will be called great in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 5:19). The small are those fed with milk, as infants in Christ (1 Corinthians 3:1–2) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 19.5).

Andrew: the throne of God is the seraphim and the cherubim. Great or small in what they have achieved, all are urged to praise God as they are able. He thinks that those small in age are meant as well: children not yet grown will be great when they sing to God, who has done such great things (19.5).

Bede: the Church worships her Lord with her lips and also with the affection of the highest devotion. “Amen” and “Alleluia” can be translated: they mean faith, or truth, and the praise of the Lord. Out of reverence for the holiness of the original language the words themselves are kept. The Church sings Alleluia on the Lord’s days, and until Lent, because of the hope of the resurrection, which lies ahead in the praise of the Lord. To be small in natural ability does not matter, if heart and tongue are full of the praise of the Lord (19.4–5).

03

REVELATION 19:6

The voice of a great multitude: “The Lord reigns”

St. Nicetas of Remesiana: the apostles sang psalms and hymns, and did not stop even in prison. St. Paul tells the assembly that one has a psalm, another an instruction (1 Corinthians 14:26), and that he will sing praise with the spirit and also with the mind (1 Corinthians 14:15). St. James writes: “Is anyone in good spirits? He should sing praise” (James 5:13). John heard the voice of the heavenly army, like many waters and mighty thunder, saying Alleluia. No one should doubt, then, that this ministry of singing, when it is celebrated with true faith and devotion, is one with that of the angels, who never cease to praise the Lord in heaven (Liturgical Singing 10).

Oecumenius: in Isaiah the seraphim say “holy” three times and so offer a threefold hymn to one lordship, showing three Persons and one divine essence (Isaiah 6:3). Here the angels have said the Alleluia three times, giving homage to each of the three Persons, and now they sing it to the holy Trinity as one. Our Lord Jesus Christ reigned over heaven and earth with the Father and the Holy Spirit before his incarnation, as the only-begotten Son and Word and maker of all things. After the incarnation he is Lord and King of all in the same way, for by taking flesh he lost nothing of his rule. We do not yet see all things made subject to him. In the age to come they will be, death itself included: “The last enemy to be destroyed is death” (1 Corinthians 15:26). So the angels sing of the age to come. Some will know that subjection as torment. Others will know it as direct knowledge, no longer as in a mirror (1 Corinthians 13:12) (19.6–9).

Primasius: his Latin text reads “the voice of trumpets.” The trumpets are the preaching of which Isaiah speaks: “lift up your voice like a trumpet blast” (Isaiah 58:1). The thunder is the dread of God’s judgment. The many waters are the peoples who praise God with Alleluia. When the Church preaches and fears and praises, she exults with trembling (Psalm 2:11). God’s omnipotence is rightly named. He created from nothing what did not exist. He sought those who had perished through sin, and from those who deserved punishment he mercifully made righteous those whom he willed. From these, as from members, he made for himself the Church, and he made her his bride, though she had not deserved to be even his servant (19.6).

A note on Primasius. “Those whom he willed” is the language of St. Augustine on grace: no one is made righteous by his own merit. The Church teaches that God predestines no one to hell (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1037), and that he wills all to be saved. See the note on the word “predestined” on The Woman and the Ark, and the note on Revelation 3:11 in chapter 3.

Andrew: the voice like many waters and thunder is the piercing clarity of the hymn, for the angelic powers are without number. Some have taken them to be the waters above the heavens. With them the whole body of the righteous gives glory to the Creator. Christ rules by nature all those whom he created. He rules them also by his incarnation, either through the fellowship he freely chose with them or by the authority that is his as King and Judge (19.6–7).

Bede: the great voice of the singers is the great devotion of the heart. By repeating its praise it rejoices in the ruin of the wicked and in the eternal glory of the Lord and of those who are his (19.6).

04

REVELATION 19:7–9

The wedding feast of the Lamb

The marriage.

  • Oecumenius: in this age the marriage of the Lord with the Church is still a betrothal, as St. Paul told the Corinthians: “I betrothed you to one husband to present you as a chaste virgin to Christ” (2 Corinthians 11:2). We hold the pledge of the Spirit as the sign of it. The marriage is made perfect when the Church becomes one spirit with Christ, as husband and wife become one body: “This is a great mystery, but I speak in reference to Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32). The Gospel teaches the same in the wedding feast of the king’s son, where one guest has no wedding garment (Matthew 22:2–14), and in the ten virgins (Matthew 25:1–13). None of this belongs to the present time. The bride is now ready to receive the gifts beyond words that come from union with Christ (19.6–9).
  • Apringius: the marriage comes at the end of the world, when Christ has destroyed every rule and authority and handed over the kingdom to God the Father, so that God may be all in all (1 Corinthians 15:24, 28). Then his wife, the Catholic Church, will be joined to him in the purity of faith (19.6–8).
  • Bede: the Church is united with her Lord in the wedding chamber of the heavenly kingdom. She has made herself ready by persevering always in the works of righteousness. Or she is like the virgins of the parable, who rose to trim their lamps when the bridegroom was coming, that is, to consider the deeds for which they hope to receive eternal blessedness (19.7–8).

The fine linen. Apringius: it is no beautiful garment. It is the righteousness of the saints. Oecumenius: it is a robe made of the virtues, bright from her way of life and fine from her teaching and her convictions about God. Bede: she is given to be clothed with her own deeds. It is not so with the wicked, whose webs, as Isaiah says, cannot serve as clothing (Isaiah 59:6).

Those invited.

  • Apringius: they are those to whom the Lord said that he would not drink the fruit of the vine again until he drinks it new with them in the kingdom of his Father (Matthew 26:29), and that many will come from east and west and recline with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Matthew 8:11). This is to drink the new cup, to receive new bodies at the resurrection, to keep a new joy, and to be repaid for the sincere righteousness of a true faith (19.9–10).
  • Primasius: the Lord says that the master will have his servants recline at table and will wait on them (Luke 12:37). The invited are those “called according to his purpose” (Romans 8:28), who make up the full number of the elect, for “Many are invited, but few are chosen” (Matthew 22:14) (19.9).
  • Andrew: the marriage supper is the feast of those who are being saved and the joy that goes with it. The blessings of the age to come surpass every understanding, and so they are given many names. They are called the kingdom of heaven for their glory, paradise for the unending banquet of good things, the bosom of Abraham for the rest of those who repose there, and the bridal chamber and the marriage for the endless joy and the perfect union of God with his servants. This union is as far above every bodily union as light is from darkness (19.7–9).
  • Bede: they are invited to a supper and not to a midday meal, for the supper at the end of the day is the great feast. When the present life is over, those who come to the refreshment of the vision of heaven are truly invited to the supper of the Lamb (19.9).
05

REVELATION 19:10

“Worship God”

St. Augustine: the lowest human being must worship the same God as the highest angel. Human nature was brought low by refusing to worship him. Angels and human beings have one source of wisdom and truth. The unchanging Wisdom of God, consubstantial and coeternal with the Father, took our nature to teach us that we must worship what every rational creature worships. The highest angels want us to join them in the worship of the one God, in whose contemplation they find their happiness. We are not made happy by seeing an angel. We are made happy by seeing the Truth, by which we love the angels too and rejoice with them. So we honour them with love and not with divine worship. We build them no temples, and they do not wish it, because they know that when we are good we are ourselves the temples of the most high God (Of True Religion 110).

Apringius: John fell down to worship the one who spoke with him. The angel forbade it, out of regard for his own office and to show that God is above all things. The testimony of Jesus is the true profession of the Catholic faith, and the Spirit of prophecy is truth, judgment and justice, which that faith holds in their fullness (19.9–10).

Oecumenius: the pagans of his day had heard the Christian teaching that the holy angels, by God’s good will, protect the nations, the churches and each person. They answered that Christians and pagans differ only in names: those whom Christians call angels, they call the gods who rule the nations. He replies that the two have nothing in common, as light has nothing in common with darkness, or Christ with Beliar (2 Corinthians 6:15). The pagans worship their gods and make no mention of the God who orders all things for the care of humankind. They assign one nation to Ares and another to Athena, and their gods govern by their own passions. The angel in this verse does the opposite. His “Don’t!” forbids worship altogether. He calls himself a fellow servant of all who confess that they are servants of Christ and bear witness that he is God made man. Whoever bears witness to the lordship and divinity of Christ is filled with the grace of prophecy, and the angel has that grace in equal measure with his fellow servants (19.10).

The Church’s practice. She honours the angels and asks their help, and gives adoration to God alone (Catechism of the Catholic Church 335–336, 2096–2097).

06

REVELATION 19:11–13

The rider on the white horse

The rider is Christ, for every Father cited. The horse is the body he took (Apringius, Bede), or his saints, on whom he rides as he rode on Paul (Oecumenius, Acts 9:15). Origen reads the vision of the soul: heaven is open to those who bear the image of the heavenly man (1 Corinthians 15:49).

Many diadems. Origen: if the lie were one, one crown would do. The lies that pass for truth are many, and the Word has overcome each.

The name no one knows. St. Andrew of Caesarea: the hidden name is the incomprehensibility of his being. From his works he has many names, such as Shepherd, Light and Life; what he is in himself is known to the Trinity alone.

07

REVELATION 19:17–18

The angel in the sun and God’s supper

The angel. Apringius: Michael, who stands up for the people (Daniel 12:1). Primasius, St. Caesarius of Arles and Bede, following Tyconius: the Church’s preaching, now in the open.

Two suppers. Andrew sets the wedding feast beside this one. God’s first will is that all be saved (1 Timothy 2:4); each one chooses his supper by his works.

08

REVELATION 19:19–21

The beast and the false prophet taken

Seized, not fought. The chapter tells of no battle. The two are simply taken. The sister site gives the Fathers’ readings of who the two captives are, and where they differ.
