Revelation 19:11–16

The Rider Called Faithful and True

The child who was caught up to God's throne in chapter 12 comes back. Heaven opens, and the one destined to rule the nations with an iron rod rides out to do it.

Home › The Study › The rider called Faithful and True

The text
"Then I saw the heavens opened, and there was a white horse; its rider was [called] 'Faithful and True.' He judges and wages war in righteousness. His eyes were [like] a fiery flame, and on his head were many diadems. He had a name inscribed that no one knows except himself. He wore a cloak that had been dipped in blood, and his name was called the Word of God… Out of his mouth came a sharp sword to strike the nations. He will rule them with an iron rod… He has a name written on his cloak and on his thigh, 'King of kings and Lord of lords'" (Revelation 19:11–13, 15–16).
In one sentence
The rider is Christ, the Woman's son, returning in glory to finish what his cross began; his only weapon is the word of his mouth.
Authority
Scripture

Why the study turns here

Chapter 12 left three things unfinished. The child was "destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod," and was caught up to God's throne before he ruled (12:5). The dragon was thrown down to earth, still at war (12:17). And the two beasts he raised up were left in power (chapter 13).

Chapters 19 and 20 finish all three, in reverse order of their appearing. The dragon came first, then the beast, then the false prophet. The beast and the false prophet are taken first (19:20), then the dragon (20:10). This part of the study follows the story to its end.

The words

  • The heavens opened. At 4:1 John saw "an open door to heaven." At 11:19 the temple in heaven was opened and the ark was seen. Now heaven itself stands open, and someone comes out.
  • A white horse. White is the color of victory throughout the book. A Roman general rode a white horse in his triumph.
  • Faithful and True. The titles the risen Lord gave himself in the letter to Laodicea: "the faithful and true witness" (3:14).
  • He judges and wages war in righteousness. The one war in the book that is just. Isaiah said of the Messiah, "he shall judge the poor with justice" (Isaiah 11:4).
  • Many diadems. The dragon wore seven (12:3) and the beast ten (13:1). The rider's are beyond number. Theirs were copies.
  • A name that no one knows except himself. "In Semitic thought, the name conveyed the reality of the person," say the notes of the New American Bible (note on 19:12). He is known truly, and never exhausted.
  • The Word of God. The title of the prologue of John's Gospel: "In the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1). The notes: "Christ is the revelation of the Father."
  • King of kings and Lord of lords. Already said of the Lamb at 17:14.

Scripture

So he bears three names here: one hidden, one that says who he is, one that says what he rules. The notes of the New American Bible call the whole passage a "symbolic description of the exalted Christ… who together with the armies of heaven overcomes the beast and its followers."

The Old Testament behind it

The iron rod. "With an iron rod you will shepherd them" (Psalm 2:9). Chapter 12 quoted this psalm at the child's birth. Chapter 19 quotes it again at his return. The same verse stands at both ends, so that the reader will know the rider is the child.

The sword from his mouth. Isaiah said of the shoot from Jesse's stump: "He shall strike the ruthless with the rod of his mouth, and with the breath of his lips he shall slay the wicked" (Isaiah 11:4). The book of Wisdom pictures the night of the Passover: "Your all-powerful word from heaven's royal throne leapt into the doomed land, a fierce warrior bearing the sharp sword of your inexorable decree" (Wisdom 18:15–16). John joins the two: the Word is the warrior, and his word is the sword.

The garments and the wine press. "Why is your apparel red, and your garments like one who treads the wine press? 'The wine press I have trodden alone, and from the peoples no one was with me'" (Isaiah 63:2–3). The prophet saw the Lord coming alone from judgment on Edom. See also The harvest and the winepress. Scripture

A sword that is a word

The rider carries no weapon in his hand. The sword comes "out of his mouth." It is his speech.

This is how God has always acted. He made the world by speaking. Christ calmed the sea, drove out demons and raised the dead with a word. St. Paul says of the last enemy that "the Lord [Jesus] will kill with the breath of his mouth and render powerless by the manifestation of his coming" (2 Thessalonians 2:8). The end comes by a word, not by a struggle whose outcome is in doubt.

And those who ride with him carry nothing at all. "The armies of heaven followed him, mounted on white horses and wearing clean white linen" (19:14). White linen is wedding dress: a few verses earlier it is the garment of the Bride, and "the linen represents the righteous deeds of the holy ones" (19:8). They come to a battle dressed for a feast, and they do not fight. He does.

The robe dipped in blood

His cloak is stained before any battle has been described. Whose blood is it?

  • His enemies'. This is the picture in Isaiah 63, which John is following, and the notes of the New American Bible refer the cloak to the wine press of verse 15.
  • His own. Many Christian readers, from the Fathers on, have seen here the blood of the cross. The rider is the Lamb "that seemed to have been slain" (5:6), and in this book robes are made white "in the blood of the Lamb" (7:14). On this reading he rides out already marked by the only battle that decided anything.

The two are not far apart. In Isaiah the Lord says, "I have trodden alone." On the cross Christ trod the wine press alone, and he was himself the one pressed. Disputed

What the Church teaches

The Church confesses in the Creed that Christ "will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead." The Catechism teaches that his reign, "already present in his Church," is "yet to be fulfilled 'with power and great glory' by the King's return to earth," and that until then it "is still under attack by the evil powers, even though they have been defeated definitively by Christ's Passover" (CCC 671).

That sentence holds the whole of this passage. The victory was won at the cross. What the rider does is make it manifest. Church teaching

Read with the Woman

She bore "a son, a male child, destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod" (12:5). This is that rule beginning.

Read as Mary, she is the mother of the rider. The one she wrapped in swaddling clothes is the one clothed in the blood-dipped cloak. Read as the Church, she is the Bride in white linen who follows him. The Catechism names both when it lists those who share in heaven's worship: "the all-holy Mother of God (the Woman), the Bride of the Lamb" (CCC 1138). Church teaching

Where readers differ

  • One event or a pattern. Most read this as Christ's coming at the end of history. Some also see in it the whole course of the Gospel going out into the world: the Word riding, and the sword of his mouth striking the nations by preaching. The two are compatible; the first is what the Creed confesses.
  • The rider of the first seal. At 6:2 another rider on a white horse "rode forth victorious to further his victories." Some of the Fathers read that rider as Christ or his Gospel; many modern commentators read him as conquest, the first of four woes. The Church has not settled it. Disputed
  • The blood on the cloak. As above.

What it asks of us

To believe that history ends with a person arriving, and that we know him. The rider is not a stranger. Every name he bears here was given earlier in the book, or in the Gospel. The one who comes is the one who came.

And to notice what his followers wear. They are not armed. Their part was "the righteous deeds of the holy ones." His part is the victory.
