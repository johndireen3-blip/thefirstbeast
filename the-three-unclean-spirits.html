The figures · Revelation 16

The Three Unclean Spirits

Three spirits like frogs come out of three mouths: the dragon's, the beast's and the false prophet's. They work signs and gather the kings of the world. Between their going out and the battle, the Lord speaks one sentence to his own.

Texts
Revelation 16:12–16; 13:13–14; 19:19–20; Exodus 7:26–8:11; Matthew 24:24, 42–44; 2 Thessalonians 2:9–11; 1 John 4:1
Figures
The dragon; the beast (the beast from the sea); the false prophet (the beast from the earth); three unclean spirits; the kings of the whole world
Authority
Scripture

What the text shows

The sixth of the seven bowls is poured out. "The sixth angel emptied his bowl on the great river Euphrates. Its water was dried up to prepare the way for the kings of the East" (Revelation 16:12). Then John sees "three unclean spirits like frogs come from the mouth of the dragon, from the mouth of the beast, and from the mouth of the false prophet. These were demonic spirits who performed signs. They went out to the kings of the whole world to assemble them for the battle on the great day of God the almighty" (16:13–14).

A voice breaks in: "Behold, I am coming like a thief. Blessed is the one who watches and keeps his clothes ready, so that he may not go naked and people see him exposed" (16:15). Then the narrative ends in one line: "They then assembled the kings in the place that is named Armageddon in Hebrew" (16:16).

Four things stand out.

  • Three mouths. The three enemies of chapters 12 and 13 are named together here for the first time, and the second beast is here first called "the false prophet." What comes from them comes from their mouths. Their weapon is speech.
  • Signs. The spirits do what the false prophet did in chapter 13: they perform signs, and by signs they persuade (13:13–14; 19:20).
  • Kings. They go to rulers, and they gather them. The beast's power in chapter 13 was over "every tribe, people, tongue, and nation"; here it is exercised by persuasion.
  • No battle here. The kings are assembled and nothing more is said. The outcome is told in chapter 19, where the beast and the false prophet are simply seized (19:19–20).

The frogs of Egypt

The second plague covered Egypt with frogs, and Pharaoh's magicians "did the same by their magic arts" (Exodus 8:3). It was the last sign they managed to copy; at the next they said, "This is the finger of God" (8:15). The notes of the New American Bible see in John's frogs "possibly an allusion to the second Egyptian plague" (note on 16:13). The bowls as a whole are "modeled in part on the plagues of Egypt" (note on 16:1–21). A theologian

Under the law, creatures of the water without fins and scales are unclean (Leviticus 11:10–12). John's image is of something small, numerous, noisy and unclean, coming out of mouths that claim great things.

For John's first readers

The Euphrates was the eastern frontier of the Roman world, and beyond it lay Parthia. The notes of the New American Bible identify "the kings of the East" as the Parthians. Armageddon "in Hebrew… means 'Mountain of Megiddo.' Since Megiddo was the scene of many decisive battles in antiquity… the town became the symbol of the final disastrous rout of the forces of evil" (note on 16:16). A theologian

"I am coming like a thief"

The one sentence addressed to the reader stands in the middle of the passage, between the spirits going out and the kings assembling. It is the Lord's own saying from the Gospel (Matthew 24:42–44), and it was said earlier in this book to the church at Sardis (Revelation 3:3). The reader is not told how to identify the spirits or how to stop the kings. He is told to stay awake and keep his garment.

The Fathers

The commentators on the Apocalypse read the frogs as demons or as false teachers. None of them names anyone. A Father or Doctor

The Euphrates dried up. Oecumenius reads the way made easy for kings to make war on one another, by the devil's work and God's permission; the Lord said there would be wars near the end (Matthew 24:6–8) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.12–16). St. Andrew of Caesarea thinks the kings may come from the region of the Scythians, since Gog and Magog are named later in the book, and bring bodily death to some for their faith and spiritual death to others for their cowardice (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.12). Primasius, following Tyconius, has a text that reads "king," and takes him for Christ, whose name in the prophet is the Rising, the East (Zechariah 6:12). The dried river is peoples with nothing green or living left in them, ready for the judgment in which the wicked are consumed like straw and the saints are proved like gold (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.12).

Why frogs?

  • Demons who love the mud. Oecumenius: they are called frogs because they delight in the life of people that is muddy and slimy, and take pleasure in the loose life of sinners more than in the steady and austere life of the just (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.12–16). Andrew: they have a poisonous and unclean nature and creep toward foul pleasures. At the command of the devil, the Antichrist and the false prophet, whom he keeps distinct, they will show false signs and wonders (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.13).
  • False teachers who make a great noise. Primasius, following Tyconius, reads one body of the devil in three parts: the dragon; the beast, which is the Antichrist with his followers; and the false prophet, which is the leaders and teachers of harmful doctrine. John sees one spirit and names three, to show that every one of the lost is led by the same spirit. Frogs croak at night in foul places. So these teachers make "a great noise," and hinder the unlearned from receiving the truth in the quiet practice of devotion. Those who cannot refute them should at least avoid them: what fellowship has light with darkness? (2 Corinthians 6:14–16) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.13).
  • Hypocrites. St. Caesarius of Arles: frogs seem to belong to the water and cannot bear it when the water is gone; they roll in the mud beneath it. So hypocrites do not live in the water of baptism and penance, as they seem to, but in what others laid aside there. He adds a warning to the one who says, "I do what I want and later I will do penance," and is overtaken by the last day (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 13).

Signs, and the gathering of the kings. Tyconius recalls Pharaoh's magicians. Up to the sign of the frogs they had been allowed to prevail by their arts, and the ministers of Satan will work wonders in the same way. He does not expect all the nations to assemble in one place. Each nation, made captive to its own superstitions, will wish to serve in that army. "The day of the Lord" he reads in three ways: the whole time since the Lord's Passion, the day of judgment itself, and the time of persecution (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.14). Oecumenius reads wars among the kings at the end, on the day the prophet Joel calls great and terrible (Joel 3:4). Andrew: those who follow the false signs will make war on that day, be utterly defeated, and lament their deception too late (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.14).

"Blessed is the one who keeps his clothes ready." Tyconius reads the garment of baptism: as many as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ (Galatians 3:27), and it is not to be torn by a second baptism or stained by sins (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.15). Oecumenius reads a virtuous life, or the body kept pure. Andrew reads good works, the garments of the saints, and recalls the guest without a wedding garment (Matthew 22:12–13). He turns the verse into a prayer that the Lord will wash the robes of our souls, so that we may enter the bridal chamber in white (Ecclesiastes 9:8) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 16.15–16). St. Bede hears the psalm that calls blessed the one whose sin is covered (Psalm 32:1, as his Latin Bible reads): a blameworthy life is covered at the judgment by the good works that followed it (Explanation of the Apocalypse 16.15).

Armageddon. Andrew gives the name the sense of "cutting": the nations gathered there by the devil will be cut down, for he delights in human blood. Bede connects the place with the last gathering against "the camp of the holy ones and the beloved city" (Revelation 20:9), which is the Church. He also reads it of the devil taking up again, in the man of perdition, the plots he was once forbidden (Explanation of the Apocalypse 16.16).

The Church Church teaching

The Church teaches that before Christ's return there will be "a religious deception offering men an apparent solution to their problems at the price of apostasy from the truth" (CCC 675). The spirits of this passage deceive by signs and by speech, and the Lord warned of "false messiahs and false prophets" whose signs would deceive, if that were possible, even the elect (Matthew 24:24). The Church also teaches the limit: "The power of Satan is, nonetheless, not infinite. He is only a creature" (CCC 395). She has not said who or what the three spirits are.

Where interpreters differ Disputed

  • Demons: Oecumenius and St. Andrew of Caesarea; the plain sense of "demonic spirits."
  • False teachers and their teaching: Tyconius and Primasius.
  • Hypocrites within the Church: St. Caesarius of Arles.
  • A gathering in one place, or in every place: Andrew and the first-century reading think of armies and a region; Tyconius reads every nation captive to its own error.
  • Persons: some readers in every age have looked for three individuals. The Fathers cited here name none, and this site names no one.

The Church has not decided which the text intends at the end.

The response asked of us

"Blessed is the one who watches and keeps his clothes ready" (Revelation 16:15). The passage gives marks to watch for: speech that comes with signs, that gathers the powerful, and that is unclean at its source. "Beloved, do not trust every spirit but test the spirits to see whether they belong to God" (1 John 4:1). And it gives the one thing to do meanwhile: keep the garment of Baptism clean.

Keep watch. This site names no one. That is no reason to stop looking. Watch what is said and done in your own day, and weigh it; How to test a reading of Revelation gives the questions to ask.
