REVELATION 3:01–06

A reputation for life, but asleep

A reputation for life, but asleep.

01To the angel of the church in Sardis: the one who has the seven spirits of God and the seven stars says this. I know your works: you have the reputation of being alive, but you are dead.

02Wake up and strengthen what remains before it dies, for I have not found your works complete in the sight of my God.

03Remember what you received and heard; keep it, and repent. If you do not keep watch, I will come like a thief, at an hour you do not know.

04Yet a few in Sardis have not soiled their garments. They will walk with me in white, for they are worthy.

05The victor will be dressed in white. I will never erase his name from the book of life, and I will acknowledge his name before my Father and his angels.

06Whoever has ears ought to hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The last three cities lay along a Roman road through the province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey. Each letter draws on what its readers knew about their own city. Sardis was the old capital of Lydia, built around a citadel thought to be unassailable. Twice in its history, under Cyrus and later Antiochus, it fell by surprise because no one was keeping watch. Christ’s warning, “I will come like a thief,” would have stung.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 3:07–13

Little strength, an open door

Little strength, an open door.

07To the angel of the church in Philadelphia: the holy one, the true, who holds the key of David, who opens and no one closes, who closes and no one opens, says this.

08I know your works. I have set before you an open door that no one can close. You have little strength, yet you have kept my word and have not denied my name.

09Those who have opposed and slandered you will come and bow at your feet, and they will learn that I love you.

10Because you have kept my message of endurance, I will keep you safe in the hour of trial that is coming upon the whole world.

11I am coming quickly. Hold fast to what you have, so that no one takes your crown.

12The victor I will make a pillar in the temple of my God, never to leave it. I will write on him the name of my God, the name of the new Jerusalem coming down from heaven, and my own new name.

13Whoever has ears ought to hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Philadelphia (modern Alaşehir) was founded as an “open door” for Greek culture into the interior. Shaken by a great earthquake in A.D. 17 and rebuilt, it knew what it meant to need a lasting pillar. The key of David recalls Isaiah 22:22; the USCCB notes cross-reference the keys given to Peter (Mt 16:19). Verse 9 speaks of “those of the assembly of Satan who claim to be Jews and are not.” As in chapter 2, it reflects a local conflict in the first century. The Church firmly rejects any use of such passages against the Jewish people (Vatican II, Nostra Aetate, no. 4), and this guide follows that teaching.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 3:14–22

Lukewarm, and invited to the table

Lukewarm, and invited to the table.

14To the angel of the church in Laodicea: the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the source of God’s creation, says this.

15I know your works: you are neither cold nor hot. I wish you were one or the other.

16Because you are lukewarm, I will spit you out of my mouth.

17You say, “I am rich and need nothing,” and do not see that you are wretched, pitiable, poor, blind and naked.

18Buy from me gold refined by fire so that you may be truly rich, white garments to cover your nakedness, and ointment for your eyes so that you may see.

19Those whom I love, I correct and discipline. Be earnest, then, and repent.

20Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, I will come in and dine with him, and he with me.

21To the victor I will give a place with me on my throne, as I conquered and sit with my Father on his throne.

22Whoever has ears ought to hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Laodicea was wealthy, proud of its banks, its medical school, its eye salve and its fine wool, and it rebuilt itself without imperial help after the earthquake of A.D. 60/61. Its water came by aqueduct and may have arrived tepid, unlike the hot springs of nearby Hierapolis or cold mountain water. Christ answers each civic boast: true gold, white garments, ointment for the eyes.

REVELATION 3:4–5

White garments and the book of life

White is the color of victory, joy and resurrection. Those who have kept their baptismal garments unstained walk with Christ, and he will acknowledge their names before his Father, as he promised in the Gospel (Mt 10:32).

02

REVELATION 3:7

The key of David

Isaiah 22:22 gives the key of the royal household to a faithful steward. Christ holds that key; he alone opens and shuts the way into the kingdom, and he shares that authority with Peter (Mt 16:19).

Read Isaiah 22
03

REVELATION 3:8, 12

The open door and the pillar

A small church with little strength is given an open door to proclaim the faith. In a city often shaken by earthquakes, the victor is promised to be a pillar in God’s temple, never to be moved.

Read Revelation 3
04

REVELATION 3:15–16

Lukewarm water

Neither refreshing like cold water nor healing like hot, lukewarm water is simply useless. Laodicea’s danger was not open sin but self-satisfied indifference: “I am rich and need nothing.”

Read Revelation 3
05

REVELATION 3:20–21

The door and the meal

Christ does not force the door; he knocks and waits. The meal he offers points to the messianic banquet (Isa 25:6; Lk 22:30) and, for Catholics, to the Eucharist, a foretaste of the heavenly throne he promises to share.

Read Luke 22
READ WITH CARE

Verse 20 is often read as an invitation to a first conversion. In context it is addressed to baptized members of a church who have grown complacent. Christ knocks on the door of believers, calling them back to fervor and to his table.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or come back to it.

The last three letters were preached to Christians in every state of life: bishops, monks, the wealthy, the newly returned. The readings below come from Tertullian, St. Cyprian, St. Gregory the Wonderworker, St. Jerome, St. Augustine, Salvian and St. Fulgentius, and from the commentators on the whole book: St. Victorinus, Tyconius, Apringius of Beja, Primasius, St. Caesarius of Arles, Oecumenius, St. Andrew of Caesarea and St. Bede.

01

REVELATION 3:1–6

Sardis: a name for being alive

Christians in name.

  • St. Victorinus: these are the negligent, empty of works, Christian in name only. It is not enough for a tree to be alive and bear no fruit, nor to be called a Christian and not do the works of one (Commentary on the Apocalypse 3.1).
  • Oecumenius: Scripture calls those caught in sins “dead,” as St. Paul says that God made us alive with Christ when we were dead in our trespasses (Ephesians 2:5). He adds the saying of the Shepherd of Hermas, that we go down into the water of Baptism dead and come up living (Commentary on the Apocalypse 3.1–6).
  • St. Bede applies the letter to the bishop. He may seem blameless, but a ruler’s works are not complete before God unless he also labours to bring others to life. Bede takes Sardis to mean “precious stone,” for the few there who walk in white (Explanation of the Apocalypse 3.1–2).

The seven spirits and the seven stars. St. Andrew of Caesarea: the stars are the angels. The seven spirits are either those same angels or the workings of the life-giving Spirit. Both are in Christ’s hand: he rules the angels as Lord, and as one in being with the Father he gives the Spirit (Commentary on the Apocalypse 3.1).

“Remember, keep it, and repent.” Apringius of Beja: he rebukes those who hold the right faith only outwardly, so that they may wake and strengthen what is left. What they are to remember is the teaching of the apostles, received at the beginning of their faith (Tractate on the Apocalypse 3.2–3). Andrew on the thief: no one knows the hour of his own death or of the end of all things. To the prepared it is rest from labour; to the unprepared it is a thief (3.3).

“A few names.” Bede: he does not say “a few” but “a few names,” because the Lord, who knew Moses by name, calls his own sheep by name and writes their names in heaven (3.4). Apringius: whoever is not soiled by sin walks with the Lord in white and follows in the steps of the Lamb.

The white garments.

  • Oecumenius: the unsoiled garments are the bodies of the saints, kept pure. The patriarch Jacob spoke of a robe washed in wine (Genesis 49:11).
  • Tertullian: garments in Scripture are a figure of the hope of the flesh. Those who have not defiled their garments are the virgins (Revelation 14:4), and the white robe is the flesh itself made glorious. The wedding garment of the Gospel (Matthew 22:11–12) he reads the same way, as the holiness of the flesh. Isaiah’s promise, “your light shall break forth like the dawn” (Isaiah 58:8), he reads of the rising of the flesh after its fall in death (On the Resurrection of the Flesh 27).
  • Bede: all are called to imitate those who have kept the robe of Baptism without stain (3.5).

The book of life and the confession. Oecumenius: the pure are written among the living, for the blessed life that does not end. Christ will acknowledge them as his faithful servants, as he promised in the Gospel (Matthew 10:32). That he says “before my Father and his angels” does not mean the angels are not also his; elsewhere he sends out “his angels” (Matthew 24:31).

02

REVELATION 3:7–13

Philadelphia: an open door

The church. Victorinus: this is the highest way of life among the saints: people who are humble in the world and unlearned in the Scriptures, yet hold the faith steadily and do not draw back from it out of fear. He does not let them be handed over to the trial (3.2). Bede: Philadelphia means “brotherly love”; the door of the kingdom is opened to her, and she is promised that the Lord will love her (3.7).

“The holy one, the true.” Oecumenius: he is holy, as the seraphim sing, and without sin even when made flesh (Isaiah 53:9). He is “true” because he truly is what he is said to be: truly God, and truly man. Nothing in him is a mere title, as Nestorius held, or a mere appearance, as Eutyches and the Manichaeans held (3.7–13).

The key of David.

  • Tyconius: royal authority. Christ was born of David’s line, and David foretold him. By his coming, as by a key, the hidden things of the law, the prophets and the psalms were opened (Luke 24:44) (Commentary on the Apocalypse 3.7).
  • Oecumenius: as David ruled Israel according to the flesh, Christ rules the spiritual Israel as well, with an authority beyond comparison. So Gabriel told the Virgin: “the Lord God will give him the throne of David his father” (Luke 1:32–33).
  • Andrew: the Holy Spirit is the key of the Psalter and of every prophecy, for through him the treasures of knowledge are opened. Some copies read “key of Hades,” which gives Christ authority over life and death (3.7).

“Who opens and no one shall close.”

  • St. Gregory the Wonderworker: the same Spirit is needed by the one who prophesies and by the one who hears. No one rightly hears a prophet unless the Spirit who spoke gives him the power to understand (Address to Origen 15).
  • Tyconius: Christ opens to those who knock. To hypocrites who say, “Lord, open to us,” he closes the door of life: “I never knew you” (Matthew 25:11; 7:23). The door he opened to the Church is the understanding of the Scriptures (Luke 24:45), and no one can shut it in any part of the world. He writes this against his own party, the Donatists (3.7–8).
  • Primasius: the secrets of God’s law are opened to the faithful by the power of Christ alone and closed to unbelievers (Commentary on the Apocalypse 3.7).

“You have limited strength.” Oecumenius: Philadelphia was a small city, and by keeping the faith of Christ it rose above its own strength. Primasius: the Church receives these gifts because she trusts in the power of Christ her king and not in her own (3.8).

“They will come and fall prostrate at your feet.” Tyconius reads a promise to the whole Church of “one flock, one shepherd” (John 10:16): what will be gathered at the end is gathered from the whole world. Later he applies “the assembly of Satan” to schismatics, who have broken the bond of charity and left the house of God, and who think themselves the whole Church though they are not everywhere (3.9, 12).

The time of trial. Tyconius: the Church is always being tested, from within and from without. The hour may also mean the time of the Antichrist, from which Christ promises to free every church that stands firm in his commandments (3.10). Andrew: either the persecution by the rulers of Rome, which came almost at once, or the coming of the Antichrist at the end. From this he pledges to free the zealous, taking them from this life beforehand so that they are not tested beyond their strength (3.10–11).

“Hold fast to what you have.”

  • St. Cyprian: having confessed Christ does not make one safe from the devil. A confessor is no greater or dearer to God than Solomon, who kept grace as long as he walked in the Lord’s ways and lost it when he left them. The Lord would not warn of losing the crown unless the crown departs when righteousness does (The Unity of the Church 20).
  • St. Augustine: he reads the verse of the number of those predestined to the kingdom: if one loses a crown and another receives it, the number is fixed (Admonition and Grace 13.39). The Church teaches that God predestines no one to hell (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1037); the warning to “hold fast” is real. See the note on the word “predestined” on The Woman and the Ark.
  • Oecumenius: what you hold is your sincere love for the Lord. The victor’s prize goes only to those who stay to the end.

A pillar in the temple. Victorinus: a pillar adorns a building, and the one who perseveres will have honour in the Church. Oecumenius: to be a pillar of the divine temple is to rejoice always in the vision of God. Bede: holy people hold the Church up now and will adorn her then, like the two pillars at the entrance of Solomon’s temple (3.12).

“He will never leave it again.” Tyconius: the nations went out from God and gave to idols the worship owed to him; yet “all the families of nations will bow low before him” (Psalm 22:28). At the end there will be a last struggle and another separation. Whoever has been set free will not go out, and will stay in the house as a son and not a slave (John 8:35). Those in the ark were allowed to come out, because there was still time to turn from sin; at the end there will be none (3.12).

The new name.

  • Tyconius: we are signed with the name of Christian, and with the name of the Church, which comes down from God every day as she is reborn. The name is “new” because of the Son of man, who died, rose and ascended. As Son of God he had this glory before the world was (John 17:5); now at his name every knee bends (Philippians 2:9–10).
  • Apringius: to be signed with God’s name and with the name of Jerusalem, the vision of peace. In God nothing grows old; the Lord’s name is always new (3.12).
  • Oecumenius: the saints are already called friends, brothers and servants. The new name goes beyond these, and has not been spoken anywhere. That he says “my God” shows that he is not ashamed of the lowliness he took on for us.
03

REVELATION 3:14–22

Laodicea: neither cold nor hot

The church. Victorinus: these are wealthy believers in positions of honour. The Scriptures are expounded in their private rooms while the faithful stand outside; they are confident in their learning and empty of works. Because such people would do great good to many if they turned back, he promises them no small reward: to sit on the throne of judgment (3.3). Bede takes Laodicea to mean “a tribe beloved of the Lord,” or else a people about to be spat out, since the letter holds both a threat and the words, “Those whom I love, I reprove.” In Greek, he adds, the name means “just people” (3.14).

“The Amen… the source of God’s creation.”

  • Primasius: Amen means “true” or “faithful,” and here it names the divine being that said to Moses, “I AM” (Exodus 3:14). He is called the beginning of God’s creation because of the Incarnation: he is the new and living way, opened through his flesh (Hebrews 10:19–21). He is the way and the homeland, the author of faith and its finisher (3.14).
  • Oecumenius: the Arians took this to make the Son a creature. But St. Paul calls him “firstborn of all creation” (Colossians 1:15), not first-created, and the psalm says “I begot you.” He cites St. Gregory of Nazianzus, who refers “the Lord created me” (Proverbs 8:22) to the Lord’s humanity and “he brought me forth” to his divinity. “Beginning” here means ruler: the one through whom all things were made rightly rules them (3.14–22).

Lukewarm.

  • Oecumenius: the “hot” are fervent in the Spirit (Romans 12:11); the “cold” have never received the Spirit, and may yet hope to. The “lukewarm” received the Spirit in Baptism and have quenched that grace through sloth and attention to passing things (1 Thessalonians 5:19). Such a person is in danger of losing both Baptism and the zeal he once had.
  • Primasius: people of rank who have plenty and use it tepidly, with no warmth of compassion; or those who study the Scriptures in private and bear no fruit from them. Let them buy the gold of wisdom and complete it in works (3.15–16).
  • Salvian: they confess God in words and deny him in deeds (Titus 1:16). Christians are worse for this very reason, that they ought to be better: the holy name becomes the charge against an unholy life (The Governance of God 4.19).
  • St. Caesarius of Arles, preaching to monks: it would have been better to stay cold in the world or to be fervent in the monastery. To leave the world and then grow careless is to become lukewarm. “With all vigilance guard your heart” (Proverbs 4:23) (Sermon 235.4).
  • Andrew: you have mixed the seed of the divine word with the thistles of wealth, and no longer see your own poverty, blindness and nakedness (3.16–17).

Gold, white garments, and salve.

  • St. Cyprian to the wealthy: you are mistaken to think yourself rich in this world. Buy from Christ gold tried by fire and you yourself will be pure gold, your stains burned away. Put on the white garment of Christ in place of Adam’s nakedness, and anoint your eyes with his salve, by good works and character (Works and Almsgiving 14).
  • Victorinus: the gold is to suffer something for the Lord’s name. The salve is to do what you already know from the Scriptures.
  • Caesarius: by almsgiving and constancy in good works you yourself become gold (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 3).
  • Andrew: the gold is the word of teaching, made bright in the fire of trials. The garment is the bright stole of virtue. The salve is poverty, since gifts blind the eyes of those who see (3.18).
  • Bede: being content with bare faith, you seek the riches of righteousness in vain. Buy the fervour of love, proved by affliction. To anoint the eyes is to gain understanding of the Scriptures by doing a good work (3.17).
  • Primasius: the naked are those without works of righteousness. The white garment is Baptism, for all who were baptized into Christ have put on Christ (Galatians 3:27), together with faith working through love (Galatians 5:6). The salve is the commandment of the Lord, which enlightens the eye (Psalm 19:9) (3.18).

“Those whom I love, I reprove and chastise.”

  • Tertullian: if God sends a blow, to whom should we show patience more than to him? We should even rejoice to be thought worthy of his correction. Blessed is the servant whom the Lord troubles to correct (On Patience 11.3–4).
  • Cyprian to bishops and priests: the one who soothes a sinner with flattery feeds the sin. A physician who handles a wound too gently lets the poison spread; it must be opened and cut, and the patient who cries out now will give thanks when he is well (The Lapsed 14).
  • St. Fulgentius of Ruspe: God’s kindness leads us to penance. He corrects us with illness and teaches us with cares, so that what we lost by misusing health we may regain through infirmity. “It is necessary for us to undergo many hardships to enter the kingdom of God” (Acts 14:22) (Letter 7.16).
  • Caesarius: do the proud and wicked seem happy because nothing touches them in this world? The psalm describes them (Psalm 73:5–7). God disciplines every child he receives (Hebrews 12:6); to be left unchastised is no sign of his favour (Sermon 5.3).

“I stand at the door and knock.”

  • St. Jerome: the “kings of the earth” are the saints, who rule their own flesh, as the Apostle says: “sin must not reign over your mortal bodies” (Romans 6:12), and “I drive my body and train it” (1 Corinthians 9:27). Let the soul command and the body obey, and Christ will come at once to make his home with us. Every day he stands at the door of our hearts and longs to enter. Let us open wide to him, and he will be both host and guest (Homilies on the Psalms 9).
  • Caesarius, preaching before Christmas: if a king invited you to his birthday, you would come in your best clothes. Adorn your soul with simplicity, temperance, chastity, charity and almsgiving, and Christ will not only visit it but remain in it (2 Corinthians 6:16) (Sermon 187.3).
  • Oecumenius: here the Lord shows his humble and peaceful nature. The devil breaks down doors with axe and hammer; the Lord says to the bride, “Open to me, my sister” (Song of Songs 5:2). If someone opens, he comes in; if not, he goes away. The supper with him is the receiving of the holy mysteries.
  • Bede: he knocks when he puts the memory of his will into our heart, through a teacher or by his own inward prompting. We open when we freely consent and set about doing what we see must be done. He dwells in his elect by love, to feed their desire for heaven as at a banquet (Homilies on the Gospels 1.21).

The throne. Tyconius: that the Son sits on the Father’s throne means that he is of one and the same substance; and as he conquered and sat down, so will the one who conquers (3.21). Andrew: the throne is the kingdom and rest of the age to come. “As I myself won the victory” is said in a human way; God the Word did not win the kingdom as a prize, for it is his by nature, and from his fullness he shares it with the saints (John 1:16). Having become man, he shared all that is ours except sin, and gives all that is his to those who conquer, as far as human nature can receive it. So he promised the apostles twelve thrones, and said through the Apostle that the saints will be caught up in the clouds to meet him (1 Thessalonians 4:17) (3.21–22).

WHERE IT FITS

The letters to the churches.

Chapter 3 completes the seven letters. Each again opens with a title of Christ from chapter 1 (the seven stars, the key, the faithful witness) and closes with a promise “to the victor.” The last promise, a place on Christ’s throne (3:21), leads straight into chapter 4, where John sees an open door to heaven and is called up to see the throne of God. The door Christ knocks on in 3:20 and the door opened in heaven in 4:1 frame the turn from the churches on earth to the worship of heaven.
