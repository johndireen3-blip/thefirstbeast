REVELATION 5:01–05

Who is worthy?

Who is worthy?

01In the right hand of the one seated on the throne John sees a scroll, written on both sides and sealed with seven seals.

02A mighty angel calls out in a loud voice, “Who is worthy to open the scroll and break its seals?”

03No one in heaven, on earth or under the earth is able to open the scroll or even look inside it.

04John weeps bitterly, because no one is found worthy.

05Then one of the elders says to him, “Do not weep. The Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered. He can open the scroll and its seven seals.”

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

Chapter 5 continues the throne vision of chapter 4 and turns it on a single question: who can carry out God’s plan for the world? The USCCB notes read the sealed scroll as that plan, hidden from all but God. No one in creation is worthy to open it, and John weeps. The elder announces a conquering Lion, the messianic King from the tribe of Judah and the line of David (Gen 49:9; Isa 11:1, 10).

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 5:06–10

The Lion is a Lamb.

The Lion is a Lamb.

06John looks, and between the throne, the four living creatures and the elders he sees a Lamb standing, though he seemed to have been slain. He has seven horns and seven eyes, the seven spirits of God sent out into all the earth.

07The Lamb comes and takes the scroll from the right hand of the one seated on the throne.

08As he takes it, the four living creatures and the twenty-four elders fall down before the Lamb. Each holds a harp and golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the holy ones.

09They sing a new song: “You are worthy to take the scroll and open its seals, for you were slain, and by your blood you bought for God people from every tribe and language, people and nation.

10You made them a kingdom and priests for our God, and they will reign on the earth.”

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

John turns and sees a Lamb that seemed to have been slain. This is the heart of the book: Christ conquers not by force but by his sacrifice. The Lamb stands, crucified and risen, still bearing the marks of his death. The USCCB notes call Christ “the Paschal Lamb without blemish, whose blood saved the new Israel from sin and death,” recalling the Passover lamb (Exod 12), the Suffering Servant led like a lamb to slaughter (Isa 53:7) and John the Baptist’s words, “Behold, the Lamb of God” (John 1:29). “Lamb” is Christ’s main title in Revelation, used twenty-eight times. His seven horns and seven eyes signify the fullness of power and knowledge.

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 5:11–14

Worthy is the Lamb.

Worthy is the Lamb.

11John hears the voices of countless angels surrounding the throne, the living creatures and the elders,

12crying out with a loud voice: “Worthy is the Lamb who was slain to receive power and riches, wisdom and strength, honor, glory and blessing.”

13Then he hears every creature in heaven, on earth, under the earth and in the sea, all that is in them, singing: “To the one seated on the throne and to the Lamb be blessing and honor, glory and might, forever and ever.”

14The four living creatures answer, “Amen,” and the elders fall down and worship.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The elders hold golden bowls of incense, “which are the prayers of the holy ones” (5:8). The prayers of God’s people on earth rise before his throne, a picture of the communion of saints. The new song echoes 1:6: the Lamb has made his people “a kingdom and priests” (Exod 19:6). At every Mass the priest lifts the Host and says, “Behold the Lamb of God,” and the people sing the Agnus Dei. The worship of chapter 5 is the worship the Church joins at the altar.

FORCE ≠ VICTORY
01

REVELATION 5:1

The scroll with seven seals

Written on both sides and sealed seven times, the scroll holds God’s plan for history, completely hidden from all but God (compare Isa 29:11; Ezek 2:9–10). Only the Lamb can open it and set that plan in motion.

Read Ezekiel 2
02

REVELATION 5:5

The Lion of Judah, the Root of David

Jacob blessed Judah as a lion’s cub (Gen 49:9), and Isaiah foretold a shoot from the stump of Jesse (Isa 11:1, 10). These messianic titles name Christ as the promised King of Israel.

Read Isaiah 11
03

REVELATION 5:6

The Lamb that was slain

The Lion turns out to be a Lamb: Christ conquers by his sacrifice. He stands, risen, yet bears the marks of his death, the Paschal Lamb whose blood saves (Exod 12; John 1:29).

Read John 1
04

REVELATION 5:6

Seven horns and seven eyes

In Scripture a horn signifies power and eyes signify knowledge (compare Zech 4:10). Seven means fullness: the Lamb has all power and all knowledge, and his Spirit goes out into the whole earth.

Read Zechariah 4
05

REVELATION 5:8

Golden bowls of incense

“Let my prayer be incense before you” (Ps 141:2). The prayers of God’s holy ones on earth rise before the throne. The Church has long seen here an image of the saints joining their prayer to ours.

Read Psalm 141
READ WITH CARE

The scroll is not a coded timetable of future events waiting to be deciphered. The USCCB notes read it as God’s saving plan, which Christ has already set in motion by his death and resurrection. The question of chapter 5 is not “when?” but “who?” The answer is the crucified and risen Lamb.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or come back to it.

The sealed scroll and the Lamb drew some of the Fathers’ most memorable preaching, St. Augustine’s above all. The readings below come from St. Irenaeus, St. Hippolytus, Origen, St. Augustine and St. Gregory the Great, and from the commentators on the whole book: St. Victorinus, Tyconius, Apringius of Beja, Primasius, St. Caesarius of Arles, Oecumenius, St. Andrew of Caesarea and St. Bede.

01

REVELATION 5:1

The scroll with seven seals

The Scriptures.

  • Origen: the scroll is the whole of Scripture. It is written on the front because its plain sense is easy, and on the back because its spiritual sense is hidden. Only the Lion of Judah, who holds the key of David, opens it (Commentary on the Gospel of John 5.6).
  • Primasius, following Tyconius: the scroll is both Testaments, the Old on the outside, where it could be seen, and the New inside, hidden within the Old. It is one scroll because neither can be without the other. At Sinai the people could not bear what they heard (Hebrews 12:18–20); now we look to what is unseen (2 Corinthians 4:18). The Old Testament is the messenger and veil of the New; the New is the fulfilment and unveiling of the Old (Commentary on the Apocalypse 5.1).
  • St. Caesarius of Arles: a testament is made by one who is about to die, and stays sealed until his death. So after the death of Christ every mystery was revealed (Exposition on the Apocalypse, Homily 4).
  • St. Andrew of Caesarea: what is written outside, in the letter, is easier to grasp; what is written inside, in the Spirit, is harder. The scroll is also the prophecy that Christ said was fulfilled in the Gospel (Luke 4:21); the rest will be fulfilled in the last days (Commentary on the Apocalypse 5.1).

The seven seals. Apringius of Beja first reads the scroll as the present world, whose inner thoughts and outward deeds God alone sees, sealed for the seven days in which it was made. Then he reads it as the Old Testament, and names the seals: the Incarnation, birth, Passion, death, Resurrection, glory and kingdom of Christ. The seals are Christ himself. By completing all these in his humanity he opened what had been closed in the Scriptures (Tractate on the Apocalypse 5.1).

God’s book of remembrance. Oecumenius reads the scroll as God’s memory of the whole human race, the book of which the psalmist and Moses speak (Psalm 139:16; Exodus 32:32). Those written “within” may be the faithful of Israel, guided by the law; those “without,” the Gentiles who came to Christ from idolatry. Seven, the perfect number, shows how securely it is closed (Commentary on the Apocalypse 5.1–7). Andrew agrees: it is the all-wise memory of God and the depth of his judgments.

02

REVELATION 5:2–4

No one was found worthy

St. Victorinus: to open the testament is to suffer and conquer death for the human race. No angel in heaven, no one on earth and none of the souls at rest was worthy of this, only Christ. What the law had foreshadowed in its offerings and sacrifices he had to fulfil himself. He is the testator who conquered death, and so it was right that he be made God’s heir and receive as his own the property of the one who was dying, that is, the human race (Commentary on the Apocalypse 5.1).

Primasius: the angels were not ignorant of the mystery; it was simply not to be accomplished through an angel. As Isaiah says, it was the Lord himself who saved them (Isaiah 63:9). No one on earth was worthy, because no just person stays perfect in this life. No one under the earth, because none of the saints who had died could bring it about. They could foresee this glory; they could not accomplish it (5.3).

St. Gregory the Great: no one but our Redeemer, made man, opened the way into the mysteries of the sacred word, by his death, Resurrection and Ascension. In passing he takes “under the earth” as a reason to think that hell lies beneath the earth, as the earth lies beneath the sky (Psalm 86:13) (Dialogues 4.44).

St. Bede: neither an angel nor any of the just already freed from the flesh could look into the book, that is, contemplate the splendour of the grace of the New Testament, as the Israelites could not look on the face of Moses (Explanation of the Apocalypse 5.1–3).

John’s tears. Primasius: it is the Church that weeps in John, weighed down by sins and begging for her redemption (5.4).

03

REVELATION 5:5

The Lion of Judah has triumphed

Tyconius: the one elder who speaks is the whole body of the prophets, who consoled the Church by foretelling that Christ would come from Judah and redeem the world by his blood (Genesis 49:9). The same Christ is lamb and lion: he accepted death in innocence and by his power killed the death he accepted (Hosea 13:14; Isaiah 11:10). Whoever weeps for his sins and remembers the promises of Scripture knows that he has the hope of salvation (Commentary on the Apocalypse 5.5).

St. Augustine, preaching at the Ascension: he is called a lion for courage and a lamb for innocence. The devil is called a lion too, for ferocity (1 Peter 5:8); and who could have escaped his teeth if the Lion of Judah had not conquered? The devil rejoiced when Christ died, and by that death he was beaten. The cross of the Lord was “the mousetrap for the devil,” and the bait was the Lord’s death (Sermon 263.2).

Augustine again: he was silent before his judges like a lamb before the shearer (Isaiah 53:7), and he is the Lion of Judah. He endured death as a lamb and devoured it as a lion. In his Passion he is a lamb because he died without guilt, and a lion because in being slain he slew death. In his Resurrection he is a lamb because his innocence is everlasting, and a lion because his might is everlasting (Sermon 375A.1).

St. Hippolytus: because Christ was foretold as a lion for his royalty and glory, the Antichrist will imitate him in everything. Christ is a lion and a king; the deceiver will present himself as both. Christ appeared as a lamb; he will look like a lamb and be a wolf within. Christ sent apostles and gave his own a seal; he will send false apostles and give a seal of his own. Christ came among the circumcised, and so will he. Christ raised up his own body as a temple (John 2:19); he will raise a temple of stone in Jerusalem (On the Antichrist 6). The Church speaks of this imitation as a pseudo-messianism (Catechism of the Catholic Church 675).

The Root of David. Oecumenius: the Lord came from Judah according to the flesh (Hebrews 7:14). Why is he called the root, and not the shoot from the stump of Jesse, as in Isaiah (Isaiah 11:1)? In his humanity he is a shoot from David’s root. In his divinity he is the root of David and of every creature, since he is the source of them all (5.1–7).

04

REVELATION 5:6

A Lamb that seemed to have been slain

Origen: the Lamb whom John the Baptist pointed out (John 1:29) is the humanity of the Son of God. He was led like a sheep to the slaughter (Isaiah 53:7; Jeremiah 11:19), and became the expiation of the whole world, buying us back with his own blood (Commentary on the Gospel of John 6.273–74).

Oecumenius: he is called a lamb for his innocence and for what he provides, as a lamb gives its wool each year. He is not slain but “as though slain,” because he came back to life, having trampled death and emptied the netherworld. Even after rising he carried the trophies of his death, the print of the nails and his body reddened with blood (Isaiah 63:2). The seven horns are his great power (Psalm 75:11). The seven eyes are the Spirit who rests on him, as Isaiah lists his gifts (Isaiah 11:2).

Apringius: he stands in the midst of the throne, in divine power; among the four living creatures, because he is known through the four Gospels; among the elders, the company of the law, the prophets and the apostles. Seven is the measure of the world he rules. The seven spirits sent into all the earth are the gifts of the Holy Spirit, poured out everywhere (Romans 8:11) (5.6).

The Lamb and the Church. Tyconius: whenever Christ is preached in the Church as slain, the Lamb is seen as though slain; and whenever the Church dies to the world to live for God, the Lamb is said to be sacrificed, the head for the body. The horns are the gifts of the Spirit by which Christ reigns through the world in his Church. They are on the head: “upon this rock I will build my church” (Matthew 16:18), as if to say, I will build you upon myself (5.6). Caesarius: the Church dies for Christ in order to live with Christ, and the Lamb slain can also be seen in her martyrs (Homily 4).

05

REVELATION 5:7

The Lamb receives the scroll

St. Irenaeus: “All things have been handed over to me by my Father” (Matthew 11:27), and nothing is held back; so he is judge of the living and the dead. No one could open the Father’s book except the Lamb who was slain and redeemed us with his blood. The Word who rules in heaven was made flesh so that he might rule on earth as a just man, and hold first place under the earth as the firstborn of the dead (Colossians 1:18). In this way the Father’s light rests on the flesh of our Lord and comes from it to us, and humanity is clothed in that light and reaches immortality (Against Heresies 4.20.2).

Victorinus: Moses knew that the testament had to stay sealed until the Passion, and so he veiled his face when he spoke to the people. He sprinkled them with blood and said, “This is the blood of the covenant” (Exodus 24:8). A testament is made by one about to die and is sealed until his death. It is fittingly opened by the slain Lamb who, as a lion, destroyed death. Now the face of Moses is uncovered, and that is why the book is called a revelation. Now the sacrifices, the priesthood, the temple and the prophecies are understood (5.2).

Apringius: the Lamb is the manhood he assumed, offered freely for our salvation. He received the scroll, that is, authority over all the works of God, as he said: “Everything that the Father has is mine” (John 16:15). He received it when, rising from the dead, he made known the mystery of the Trinity, hidden from the ages (5.7).

Primasius: this is not to be pictured in a bodily way, as if he took the book from someone else’s hand; the Son is himself the right hand of the Father, and each reigns on the throne with the Holy Spirit. And since the one who said, “I have conquered the world” (John 16:33), conquers in those who are his, the Church too is said to receive the book (5.7).

06

REVELATION 5:8

Harps and bowls of incense

Origen: the spirit of every good person is an altar, and from it rises a truly sweet incense, the prayers of a pure conscience (Psalm 141:2). The offerings that please God are the virtues the Word forms in us (Against Celsus 8.17).

Victorinus: the harp, with its strings stretched on wood, is the body of Christ bound to his Passion. He is the first to make this comparison. The bowls are the confession of faith and the spread of the new priesthood (5.3).

Primasius: the bowls are sincere confession, and the golden vessels of the great house, fragrant with Christ. The prayers of the saints are their thanksgiving for the world’s salvation and their intercession for the helpless. When those who belong to Christ crucify the flesh with its passions (Galatians 5:24), it is as if they play to Christ on harps, doing for him what he did for them (1 Peter 2:21; Hebrews 12:2) (5.8).

Andrew: the harps are the harmonious praise of God; the incense is the sacrifice of the faithful, offered through a pure life, for “we are the aroma of Christ” (2 Corinthians 2:15). The bowls are the thoughts from which good works and pure prayer come (5.7–8).

St. Bede: when the Lord showed in his Passion that both Testaments were fulfilled in him, the Church gave thanks and gave herself to suffering, to fill up what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ (Colossians 1:24). The harps are bodies ready to die, their strings stretched on wood; the bowls are hearts opened wide by charity (5.8).

Bede, preaching at Easter: the women came to the tomb early, carrying the spices they had prepared. Our spices are our prayers. We carry them to the Lord’s tomb when, mindful of his Passion, we show our neighbour the light of good works and burn inwardly with compunction. We should do this always, and most of all when we enter the church to pray and come to the altar to receive the Body and Blood of the Lord (Homilies on the Gospels 2.10).

07

REVELATION 5:9–14

A new song

Why the song is new.

  • Victorinus: the Old Testament joined to the New shows the Christian people singing a new song, their public confession of faith. It is new that the Son of God became man, was given over to death, rose on the third day and ascended in the body; new that sins are forgiven and that people are sealed with the Holy Spirit; new that believers receive a priestly service of prayer and await a kingdom of such promises (5.3).
  • Primasius: Christ wrote the new song himself: by his new kind of birth from the Virgin, by giving himself over to death by his own power, by rising and ascending. He gave it to the Church to sing without ceasing, so that she may “live in newness of life” (Romans 6:4) and take up her cross and follow him (Luke 9:23) (5.9).
  • Oecumenius: it is new because it was sung to God made flesh, and no such song was offered before the Incarnation (5.8–12).
  • Andrew: it is the song of those from every tribe and tongue who have been freed from the oldness of the letter and enlightened by the Spirit (5.9–10).

“From every tribe and tongue, people and nation.” Oecumenius: he says “from” every nation with good reason. He did not gain all, since many died in unbelief; from every nation he gained those who are saved (Psalm 82:8).

“A kingdom and priests.” Oecumenius: besides those who lead the churches, the kings are all who rule their passions and are not ruled by them, and the priests are all who offer their bodies as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to God (Romans 12:1). Andrew: they will reign on the new earth that the Lord promised to the meek.

“Worthy is the Lamb… to receive.” Caesarius: this is not said of his divinity, in which are hidden all the treasures of wisdom (Colossians 2:3). It is said of the manhood he assumed, and of his body, the Church, which receives all things in her head (Romans 8:32). “All power in heaven and on earth has been given to me” (Matthew 28:18): he receives it as man (Homily 4).

Every creature. Andrew: God is glorified by all that exists, whether it thinks, or lives, or merely is, each in the way proper to its nature; and with him his only Son, of one being with the Father, who renewed humankind and the creation made through him (5.11–13).

One Church of angels and men. Andrew: the living creatures and the elders worship together. Through Christ, who broke down the dividing wall, one flock and one Church has been formed of angels and human beings. He ends with a prayer that we too may be found worthy of that worship (5.14).

WHERE IT FITS

The Lamb receives the scroll.

Chapters 4 and 5 form one vision. Chapter 4 shows God the Creator enthroned; chapter 5 shows the Lamb receiving the scroll, sharing the throne and the worship of heaven (5:13). The worship of creation in 4:11 becomes the worship of redemption in 5:9–10. In chapter 6 the Lamb begins to open the seals one by one, and the rest of the book unfolds from his hand. The Lamb remains at the center to the very end, where the new Jerusalem has “the Lamb” as its lamp (21:23).
