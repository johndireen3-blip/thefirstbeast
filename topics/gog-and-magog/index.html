REVELATION 20:7-10

Who Are Gog and Magog in Revelation?

Who are Gog and Magog, from a Catholic perspective? They are not a country on a map. In Revelation the two names stand for all the nations deceived into a last attack on God’s people, an attack that God himself ends.

What John sees

“When the thousand years are completed, Satan will be released from his prison. He will go out to deceive the nations at the four corners of the earth, Gog and Magog, to gather them for battle; their number is like the sand of the sea. They invaded the breadth of the earth and surrounded the camp of the holy ones and the beloved city. But fire came down from heaven and consumed them” (Revelation 20:7-9).

Where the names come from

The prophet Ezekiel told of “Gog of the land of Magog,” a prince from the far north who leads many nations against Israel and is destroyed by God alone (Ezekiel 38-39). The notes of the New American Bible say that even there “the name is symbolic,” and that the oracles were given “to strengthen Israel’s hope in God, since they end with God’s triumph on behalf of the people.”

What John changes

In Ezekiel, Gog is a man and Magog his land, and they come from the north. In Revelation they are two names for “the nations at the four corners of the earth.” John has widened the picture on purpose. The notes of the New American Bible call them “symbols of all pagan nations.”

Is Gog a modern nation?

In nearly every century readers have matched Gog and Magog to a people of their own day: Goths, Huns, Mongols, and in modern times one great power or another. None of those identifications has held. The text suggests why: John gives the name to all the nations, not to one. That does not close the question of what the last gathering against God’s people will look like. It tells the reader what to look for: not a flag, but a deception wide enough to take in the world.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism speaks of a final trial: “Before Christ’s second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers” (CCC 675). The kingdom will come “not by a historic triumph of the Church through a progressive ascendancy, but only by God’s victory over the final unleashing of evil” (CCC 677). The Catechism’s own footnote to those words points to this passage.

No battle

The camp of the holy ones is surrounded, and nothing suggests it can win. No army goes out. “Fire came down from heaven.” Then the deceiver himself is thrown down for ever (20:10).

For reflection today

The Church may look small and surrounded at the end. Do I place my hope in her numbers, or in the One who defends her?

Keep watch

The mark of this last gathering is deception on the scale of the world, aimed at “the camp of the holy ones.” Watch for that, and not for a place-name: where are whole peoples being persuaded to turn against what is holy? Weigh what you see with the questions on How to Test a Reading of Revelation.
