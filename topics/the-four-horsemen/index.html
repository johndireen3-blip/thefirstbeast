REVELATION 6:1-8

Who Are the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse?

Who are the four horsemen, from a Catholic perspective? They are four riders John sees when the Lamb opens the first four seals of the scroll: conquest, war, famine and death. They show the sorrows that run through all of history, and they ride only when the Lamb breaks the seal.

What John sees

The Lamb opens the seals one at a time, and each time a living creature cries, “Come forward” (Revelation 6:1–8).

  • A white horse. “Its rider had a bow. He was given a crown, and he rode forth victorious to further his victories” (6:2).
  • A red horse. “Its rider was given power to take peace away from the earth, so that people would slaughter one another. And he was given a huge sword” (6:4).
  • A black horse. “Its rider held a scale in his hand,” and a voice says, “A ration of wheat costs a day's pay” (6:5–6).
  • A pale green horse. “Its rider was named Death, and Hades accompanied him.” They were given authority “to kill with sword, famine, and plague, and by means of the beasts of the earth” (6:8).

Where the picture comes from

The notes of the New American Bible say “the imagery is adapted from” the prophet Zechariah, who saw horses of different colors sent out to patrol the earth (Zechariah 1:8–10; 6:1–8). The four kinds of death in verse 8 echo Ezekiel's “four evil punishments—sword, famine, wild beasts, and plague” (Ezekiel 14:21).

Who is the rider on the white horse?

This is where readers have differed most.

  • A conquering power. The notes of the New American Bible call the first rider “a conquering power,” and suggest the bow “may perhaps allude specifically to the Parthians,” the mounted archers on Rome's eastern border.
  • The Gospel going out. St. Victorinus, who wrote the oldest commentary we have, read him differently: “the white horse is the word of preaching with the Holy Spirit sent into the world.” The other three, he says, “very plainly signify the wars, famines, and pestilences announced by our Lord in the Gospel.”
  • A counterfeit. Some later readers take him as a false Christ, since he looks like a victor and is followed by ruin.

The Church has not settled it. One thing in the text helps: Christ does ride a white horse later in the book, and there he is named. “Its rider was [called] ‘Faithful and True’” (19:11). Compare the two riders for yourself: one carries a bow and is given a crown; the other has “many diadems” and a sword that comes from his mouth.

“He was given”

Notice how often the words “was given” appear. A crown was given, power was given, a sword was given, authority was given. None of the riders acts on his own. The seals are opened by the Lamb who was slain, and the riders go only as far as they are allowed: “a quarter of the earth,” not the whole (6:8). The Catechism teaches the same about evil in general: God permits it and draws good from it, and nothing escapes his providence (CCC 309–314).

Are the horsemen riding now?

Jesus told his disciples they would hear of “wars and reports of wars,” of famines and earthquakes, and added: “these are the beginning of the labor pains” (Matthew 24:6–8). Conquest, war, hunger and death have marked every century since John wrote, and they mark ours. So the honest answer is yes, in the sense that they have never stopped. What the text does not give is a way to match each rider to one event or to count down from them to the end. “It is not for you to know times or seasons” (Acts 1:7).

For reflection today

After the four riders, John sees the martyrs under the altar, who cry, “How long?” and are told “to be patient a little while longer” (6:10–11). When the news is full of war and want, is my prayer theirs, or have I stopped asking?

Keep watch

Read Revelation 6 with a newspaper beside it. Where do you see conquest dressed as victory, peace taken from the earth, or bread priced out of reach of the poor? Then ask the harder question: what does the Lamb who opened the seals ask of me in the face of it? If you form a view about the white rider, test it: see How to Test a Reading of Revelation.
