Full text & notes on USCCB
REVELATION 4:01–05

Come up here.

Come up here.

01After this John sees a door standing open in heaven, and the trumpet-like voice he heard before says, “Come up here, and I will show you what must happen next.”

02At once he is caught up in the Spirit. A throne stands in heaven, and someone is seated on it.

03The one on the throne gleams like jasper and carnelian, and around the throne shines a rainbow like an emerald.

04Around the throne are twenty-four other thrones, and on them sit twenty-four elders, robed in white, with gold crowns on their heads.

05Lightning, rumblings and thunder come from the throne. Before it burn seven flaming torches, the seven spirits of God.

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The letters of chapters 2–3 showed the churches as they were on earth: small, pressured, sometimes compromised. Chapter 4 lifts the reader through an open door to see who truly reigns. For Christians living under an emperor who demanded worship, the message was clear: the throne at the center of all things is God’s, not Caesar’s. The USCCB notes point out that much of this imagery comes from Ezekiel 1 and 10 and Isaiah 6. John reverently avoids describing God himself. He speaks only of light, precious stones and the worship that surrounds the throne. The lightning and thunder recall God’s appearance at Sinai (Exod 19:16).

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 4:06–08

All creation awake before God.

All creation awake before God.

06Before the throne is something like a sea of glass, clear as crystal. In the center and around the throne are four living creatures, covered with eyes in front and behind.

07The first is like a lion, the second like a young ox, the third has a human face, and the fourth is like an eagle in flight.

08Each has six wings and is covered with eyes inside and out. Day and night they never stop singing: “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God almighty, who was, and who is, and who is to come.”

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The twenty-four elders represent the twelve tribes of Israel and the twelve apostles (compare 21:12–14): the whole People of God, Old Covenant and New, in priestly white and royal crowns. The four living creatures are drawn from the cherubim of Ezekiel and the seraphim of Isaiah. The USCCB notes say they represent all creation; lion, ox, human and eagle stand for what is noblest, strongest, wisest and swiftest. The seven spirits of God are, in the notes, the seven angels of the presence (8:2; Tob 12:15).

ORIGINAL VERSE SUMMARIES
REVELATION 4:09–11

Worthy are you.

Worthy are you.

09Whenever the living creatures give glory, honor and thanks to the one seated on the throne, who lives forever and ever,

10the twenty-four elders fall down before him and worship. They cast their crowns before the throne and cry out:

11“You are worthy, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power, for you created all things; by your will they came to be and were created.”

HISTORICAL CONTEXT / USCCB NOTES

The Catechism opens its teaching on the liturgy with this chapter: “A throne stood in heaven, with one seated on the throne” (CCC 1137). It describes the heavenly powers, all creation and the servants of the Old and New Covenants joined in one act of praise (CCC 1138). Every Mass enters that worship. At the Sanctus, the congregation sings the same “Holy, holy, holy” that the living creatures sing day and night.

REVELATION 4:1

The open door

The door Christ knocked on in 3:20 now opens from heaven’s side. The ancients pictured heaven as a vault with real doors; John is invited through, as the Church is in every liturgy.

Read Revelation 4
02

REVELATION 4:2–3

The throne

The word “throne” appears more than a dozen times in this chapter alone. Whatever empires claim, God alone rules history. John describes the one seated there only in light and precious stone, as Isaiah and Ezekiel did.

Read Ezekiel 1
03

REVELATION 4:4, 10

The twenty-four elders

Twelve tribes and twelve apostles: Israel and the Church together, robed as priests and crowned as kings. They cast their crowns before the throne, confessing that every victory is God’s gift.

Read Revelation 21
04

REVELATION 4:6–8

The four living creatures

Lion, ox, human face and eagle stand for all creation awake before God. Since the second century the Church has also read them as the four evangelists: Mark the lion, Luke the ox, Matthew the human face and John the eagle.

Read Isaiah 6
05

REVELATION 4:6

The sea of glass

In the ancient world the sea meant chaos and danger. Before God’s throne it lies still and clear as crystal (compare Exod 24:10). Nothing is out of his control.

Read Exodus 24
READ WITH CARE

Some popular teachers read the open door of 4:1 as a secret “rapture” of the Church before a time of tribulation. The text describes John alone being caught up in a vision, and the Church has never taught a pre-tribulation rapture. Revelation’s point is the opposite of escape: God reigns now, even while his people suffer on earth.

WHERE IT FITS

The worship of heaven.

Chapter 4 opens the book’s central section. The door Christ knocked on in 3:20 now opens in heaven (4:1), and the scene shifts from the churches on earth to the worship of heaven. Chapters 4 and 5 form one vision: chapter 4 shows God the Creator enthroned, and chapter 5 brings forward the Lamb who alone can open the scroll of history. Everything that follows, the seals, trumpets and bowls, flows from this throne.

04 / GOING DEEPER

What the Fathers said.

The sections above introduce the chapter. This one is for further study: how the Fathers of the Church and the early commentators read it, verse by verse. Read it now, or go on to the next chapter and come back to it.

The vision of the throne gave the Church some of her best-known images, above all the four living creatures as figures of the four Gospels. The readings below come from St. Irenaeus, Tertullian, St. Athanasius, Rufinus and St. Augustine, and from the commentators on the whole book: St. Victorinus, Tyconius, Apringius of Beja, Primasius, Oecumenius and St. Andrew of Caesarea.

01

REVELATION 4:1

An open door

“After this.” Tyconius: the gap in time belongs to the visions, not to the events. John describes one reality in several ways, and so goes back over the whole life of the Church under different figures (Commentary on the Apocalypse 4.1). This is the principle later called recapitulation.

The door.

  • Tyconius: the open door is Christ, who was born, suffered and rose: “I am the gate” (John 10:9). Heaven is the Church, the dwelling of God where heavenly things are done; so we pray that his will be done on earth as in heaven.
  • St. Victorinus: John sees the preaching of the New Testament. The door had been closed to humankind, and was fully opened when Christ ascended in the body to the Father. The voice that calls him is the same voice that spoke through the prophets (Commentary on the Apocalypse 4.1).
  • St. Andrew of Caesarea: the opening of the door is the revelation of the hidden mysteries of the Spirit (Commentary on the Apocalypse 4.1).

“Come up here.” Primasius: the trumpet is the voice of prophecy (Isaiah 58:1). One goes up who despises the world and comes to the Church: “Come, let us go up to the LORD’s mountain” (Isaiah 2:3). A newly baptized Christian is taught that Christ suffered, rose and ascended, and is then led on to contemplate him with the Father; this too is to go up. So the Lord said, “Stop holding on to me, for I have not yet ascended to the Father” (John 20:17): the touch he asks for is faith (Commentary on the Apocalypse 4.1). Andrew: the hearer’s mind is to turn wholly from earthly things toward heaven. Apringius of Beja: John was “in the spirit,” and so saw nothing in a fleshly way (2 Corinthians 5:16) (Tractate on the Apocalypse 4.2).

02

REVELATION 4:2–3

The throne, the jewels, the rainbow

Jasper and carnelian.

  • Oecumenius: God is not like these stones; he is invisible and without form, and “no one has ever seen God” (John 1:18; Exodus 33:20). The vision shows him by what he does. Green jasper is his life-giving nature, since all food begins in what grows from the earth (Psalm 104:14–15). Fiery carnelian is his fearsomeness: “the LORD, your God, is a consuming fire” (Deuteronomy 4:24). The jasper is named first, because God is by nature good and gentle and wishes to be our Father; it is we who make him severe (Commentary on the Apocalypse 4.1–3).
  • Victorinus: jasper is the colour of water and carnelian of fire. Two judgments are set over God’s tribunal until the end of the world: one already carried out by water, the other to come by fire (4.2).
  • Apringius: the two stones are the flesh of Christ, taken without sin from the humble Virgin and bright with the divine power that dwells in it (4.3).

The rainbow. Primasius: the rainbow was the sign of safety after the flood, and here it is the sign of the Church’s reconciliation with God. As clouds lit by the sun make a rainbow, so the souls of the saints are lit by Christ, the Sun of righteousness (4.2–3). Oecumenius: an ordinary rainbow has many colours; this one has a single colour, like emerald. It is the host of ministering angels around God, of many orders and one colour, because all alike imitate their Lord in doing good.

03

REVELATION 4:4

The twenty-four elders

Victorinus: the books of the law and the prophets, which give testimony at the judgment; and also the twelve patriarchs and the twelve apostles (4.3).

Tyconius: the whole Church, leaders and people, as Isaiah says that the Lord will be glorified before his elders (Isaiah 24:23). Twelve is doubled because the same Church stands in both Testaments. The thrones are in truth one throne: Christ alone will sit in judgment, and the Church sits and judges in him, the members in one head (4.4). Primasius, who draws on him, adds that twenty-four is four sixes: the perfection that comes through the clear preaching of the fourfold Gospel.

Apringius: the patriarchs and the apostles, seated on the chair of holy teaching. Their white garments are works of righteousness, and their crowns the crown of which St. Paul speaks (2 Timothy 4:7–8) (4.4).

Oecumenius says that God alone knows who they are, and then offers his own list of the saints of both Testaments, from Abel, Enoch and Noah to John the Baptist, James and Stephen (4.4–6). Andrew mentions this view and sets it aside; he agrees with Apringius that they are the twelve patriarchs and the twelve apostles.

04

REVELATION 4:5–6

Lightning, seven torches, a sea of glass

Lightning and thunder. Primasius: the voices and thunder are the preaching of the Gospel, and the lightning the miracles that confirmed it (Mark 16:20; Psalm 77:19) (4.5). Apringius: the words of the saints and the voices of the preachers all come from one source, God. Andrew: what is fearful to the unworthy is light to those being saved. The lightning enlightens the eyes of the mind, and the thunder falls on spiritual ears (4.5).

The seven torches. Primasius: the Holy Spirit, who came on the apostles as tongues of fire, in his sevenfold working. Where is he more properly said to be present than at Baptism, where each of the faithful receives him? (4.5)

The sea of glass.

  • Victorinus: the gift of Baptism, which God poured out through his Son in the time of repentance, before the judgment begins; that is why it is “in front of the throne.” It is pure water, smooth, not roughened by wind or running downhill like a river: a gift that does not move (4.2).
  • Primasius: Baptism is like glass because of faith: in glass nothing is seen on the outside that is not true within (Romans 10:10). It stands before the throne to show the undivided unity of the Church, so that no one may think it can be had apart from her. Writing against the Donatists, he says that the gift is not evil but becomes harmful to one who misuses it, like the morsel the Lord gave to Judas, and that one who receives it outside the Church “is born to destruction” (4.6).
  • A note on Primasius. The Church teaches more carefully than this sentence taken alone. Anyone, even someone not baptized, can baptize validly who uses water and the Trinitarian formula and intends what the Church does (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1256). Such a Baptism is real, is never repeated, and joins the baptized to Christ; all who have received it are rightly called Christians and are brothers and sisters in the Lord (CCC 1271–1272). What Primasius and St. Augustine before him had in view was those who knowingly hold the sacrament against the unity and charity of the Church: the sacrament is true, and it does not profit one who refuses the communion it is given for.
  • Apringius: the font of Baptism and the grace of rebirth, which cleanses, enlightens and clothes with righteousness. These are the living waters that flow from Jerusalem in summer and in winter (Zechariah 14:8) (4.6).
  • Oecumenius: the sea is the multitude of holy spirits around God, as many as a sea and as pure as crystal: “thousands upon thousands were ministering to him” (Daniel 7:10) (4.4–6).
05

REVELATION 4:6–8

The four living creatures

The four Gospels. The Fathers agree that the creatures are the four Gospels and differ on which is which.

  • St. Irenaeus is the first to say it. There cannot be more Gospels than four or fewer, as there are four regions of the world and four winds, and the Church is spread over all the earth with the Gospel as her pillar (1 Timothy 3:15). The Word, who is enthroned on the cherubim (Psalm 80:2), gave us the Gospel in four forms held together by one Spirit. The lion is his royal power, the calf his priestly sacrifice, the man his coming as man, the eagle the gift of the Spirit. He gives John the lion, because his Gospel opens with the Word’s glorious birth from the Father; Luke the calf, because he opens with Zechariah the priest offering sacrifice; Matthew the man, because he opens with the human genealogy; and Mark the eagle, because he opens with the prophetic Spirit coming down from on high (Against Heresies 3.11.8).
  • Victorinus follows Irenaeus, and adds that Christ himself bore all four likenesses: the lion of Judah, a man for our salvation, a calf in his sacrifice, an eagle in his ascension, spreading his wings over his people. There are four evangelists and one proclamation, since it comes from one mouth, as the river of paradise was one source parted into four streams (4.3–4).
  • St. Augustine gives Matthew the lion, for the royal line of David; Luke the calf, for the priesthood of Zechariah; and Mark the man. These three walk with the Lord on earth. John is the eagle, who preaches what is highest and looks with steady eyes on the inner and eternal light (Tractates on the Gospel of John 36.5.2).
  • Apringius, following St. Jerome, gives the order that became usual in the West: Mark the lion, for the voice crying in the desert; Luke the ox, for Zechariah the priest; Matthew the man, for the genealogy; John the eagle, who leaves lowly things behind and begins, “In the beginning was the Word.” He also explains why Mark puts a verse of Malachi under Isaiah’s name: Isaiah means “the salvation of the Lord” and Malachi “messenger,” and the evangelist wished to begin with the salvation and then name its messenger (4.7).

The Church. Primasius reads the four of the Church as well. She has the strength of the Lion of the tribe of Judah (Genesis 49:9). She is the calf, the victim, whenever one of the faithful is slain for Christ (Romans 12:1). She has the face of a man in her humility, carrying her treasure in earthen vessels (2 Corinthians 4:7) and following the Master who is “meek and humble of heart” (Matthew 11:29). And she is the eagle, lifted above earthly desires by the two Testaments (4.7).

God’s care for the earth. Oecumenius reads the creatures of God’s providence. It does not stop at heaven; it reaches everything on earth. Every earthly thing is made of four elements, and each creature stands for one: the lion fire, the ox earth, the man air, the eagle water. They surround the throne to show that what is on earth is worth God’s attention and care (4.6–8).

Six wings. Victorinus: the wings are the testimonies of the Old Testament, twenty-four books, as many as the elders. A creature cannot fly without wings, and the preaching of the New Testament does not win faith unless it is seen to match what was foretold. He says this against those, like the followers of Marcion, who would not use the prophets (4.5). Tyconius: six wings on four creatures make twenty-four, so the creatures and the elders are one: the Church, carried upward on the two wings of the two Testaments (4.8).

06

REVELATION 4:8–11

“Holy, holy, holy”

Tertullian: the angels never cease to say, “Holy, holy, holy” (Isaiah 6:3). We hope to be their companions, and so we learn here on earth that heavenly cry of praise, the task of our future glory (On Prayer 3.2–3).

St. Athanasius: the cherubim and seraphim are nearest to God, and even they offer their praise with veiled faces. That they say “Holy” three times shows three perfect Persons; that they say “Lord” once declares the one divine being. The Trinity is one and undivided (On Luke 10:22).

Rufinus of Aquileia: the Father governs all things through the Son, through whom all things were made (Colossians 1:16; Hebrews 1:2). As light is begotten from light, so Almighty from Almighty. The one “who is to come” is called the Almighty here, and who is to come but Christ? (Commentary on the Apostles’ Creed 5)

Primasius: the Church, spread through every city, province, language and people, praises God without ceasing in hard times and good, by word and deed. Her praise joins that of the Jerusalem above, in whose likeness the pilgrim Church rejoices to be formed (4.8).

The crowns thrown down. Victorinus: when the Gospel had fulfilled what the prophets foretold, they rejoiced that they had served the word of the Lord rightly; and because he had come who would conquer death, all who had crowns laid their victories at his feet (4.7). Primasius: by casting down their crowns they give to God whatever virtue and dignity they have, since every good thing we gain comes from the one who helps us conquer. He sees a figure of it when the crowd spread their cloaks and branches before the Lord and cried, “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord” (Matthew 21:9) (4.10).

“Because of your will they came to be.” Tyconius: all things existed in the wisdom of God before they were made. They were created so that what was written in his wisdom might also exist visibly (4.11).
